Chapter 01 · SPEED FAMILY · 2025 Q54, Q64

Speed, time and distance: the one formula

Every question in this family grows from one line: distance = speed × time. If you know two of the three, you can find the third. Speed = distance ÷ time, and time = distance ÷ speed. The examiner never changes this rule; he only hides it under trains, races and boats.

In 2025 the family took 6 of the 50 maths questions: Robert on his cycle 2025 Q54, a train passing a man 2025 Q59, a race "by 28 metres in 7 seconds" 2025 Q60, a car going 10 km/h faster 2025 Q64, a 100 m race with a head start 2025 Q65 and another train passing a man 2025 Q82.

1.1Units: km/h and metres per second

Most mistakes in this family are unit mistakes. A train question gives lengths in metres and times in seconds, but speeds in km/h. Change them before you do anything else.

One km is 1,000 m and one hour is 3,600 seconds, so 1 km/h = 1000/3600 = 5/18 m/s. To go back, multiply by 18/5.

ChangeMultiply byExample
km/h → m/s5/1890 km/h × 5/18 = 25 m/s
m/s → km/h18/520 m/s × 18/5 = 72 km/h

Learn these four by heart

18 km/h = 5 m/s · 36 km/h = 10 m/s · 54 km/h = 15 m/s · 72 km/h = 20 m/s. Every multiple of 18 turns into a whole number of metres per second, which is why examiners love 54 and 72.

1.2Speed and time go opposite ways

For a fixed distance, if the speed goes up, the time goes down in the same ratio. If you go at 3/4 of your usual speed, you take 4/3 of your usual time. This one idea solves the "late" questions.

Example: walking at 3/4 of his usual speed, a man is 20 minutes late. The new time is 4/3 of the usual, so the extra time is 4/3 − 1 = 1/3 of the usual time. That 1/3 is 20 minutes, so the usual time is 60 minutes.

1.3Late at one speed, early at another

The question gives two speeds and says he is late by one amount and early by another. The two times differ by late + early (the right time sits between them). If he is late both times, they differ by the difference.

Example: at 4 km/h a boy is 10 minutes late; at 5 km/h he is 5 minutes early. The times differ by 15 minutes = 1/4 hour. So d/4 − d/5 = 1/4, which is d/20 = 1/4, and d = 5 km.

Shortcut: distance = (product of speeds ÷ difference of speeds) × time difference = (20 ÷ 1) × 1/4 = 5 km.

The real 2025 question was this type with clock times. Robert reaches at 2 p.m. at 10 km/h and at 12 noon at 15 km/h; at what speed will he reach at 1 p.m.? 2025 Q54 The times differ by 2 hours: d/10 − d/15 = 2, so d/30 = 2 and d = 60 km. At 10 km/h he rides 6 hours, so he starts at 8 a.m. To arrive at 1 p.m. he has 5 hours: 60 ÷ 5 = 12 km/h. Note that the answer is not 12.5, the plain average of 10 and 15.

1.4Faster by a few km/h, so hours less

The 2025 car question: a car covers 715 km; at 10 km/h more it would take 2 hours less. Find the speed. 2025 Q64 This makes a quadratic: 715/v − 715/(v + 10) = 2. Do not solve it. Try the options. 715 ÷ 55 = 13 hours and 715 ÷ 65 = 11 hours, a difference of exactly 2. So the speed is 55 km/h.

Try the options

In speed questions with a quadratic inside, checking each option is faster than algebra. Start with an option that divides the distance exactly.

1.5Average speed

Average speed is total distance ÷ total time. It is almost never the plain average of the speeds.

FIG_001Equal distances, unequal times: the slow part lasts longer.

When the two distances are equal (going and coming back), use 2xy ÷ (x + y). At 30 km/h there and 60 km/h back: 2 × 30 × 60 ÷ 90 = 40 km/h, not 45. For three equal distances, use 3xyz ÷ (xy + yz + zx), or simply take each part as the LCM of the speeds and add up the times.

When the distances are not equal, work out each time and add them. 30 km at 10 km/h (3 h) and 30 km at 15 km/h (2 h) is 60 km in 5 hours = 12 km/h.

1.6Stoppages

A bus runs at 80 km/h without stops but averages 60 km/h with them. In each hour it loses 20 km. At 80 km/h, 20 km takes 20/80 hour = 15 minutes. So it stops 15 minutes every hour. Formula: (difference ÷ speed without stops) × 60.

Likely next in this chapter

At 5/6 of his usual speed a man is 12 minutes late. What is his usual time?

New time is 6/5 of usual, so the extra 1/5 of usual is 12 minutes. Usual time = 60 minutes.

54 km/h in metres per second?

54 × 5/18 = 15 m/s.

A man goes at 20 km/h and returns at 30 km/h. Average speed?

2 × 20 × 30 ÷ 50 = 24 km/h.

Without stops a train runs at 45 km/h; with stops 36 km/h. Minutes stopped per hour?

9/45 × 60 = 12 minutes.

Chapter 02 · SPEED FAMILY · moving objects

Meeting and chasing: relative speed

When two things move at the same time, think about the gap between them. The gap closes (or opens) at the relative speed.

  • Moving towards each other (opposite directions): speeds ADD.
  • Moving the same way (one chasing the other): speeds SUBTRACT.

This one rule is the key to trains, races and boats too, so learn it well.

2.1Meeting

Two towns are 200 km apart. Cars leave both towns at the same time towards each other at 30 km/h and 20 km/h. The gap closes at 30 + 20 = 50 km/h, so they meet after 200 ÷ 50 = 4 hours, at 30 × 4 = 120 km from the first town.

If one car leaves an hour later, first move the early car forward by one hour, then use the gap that is left. Forgetting this head start is the common trap.

2.2Chasing

A thief is 1 km ahead. He runs at 8 km/h and a policeman follows at 10 km/h. The gap closes at 10 − 8 = 2 km/h, so the policeman catches him after 1 ÷ 2 = half an hour. In that time the thief runs 4 km and the policeman 5 km. Read whose distance the question asks for.

Whose distance?

The options always hold both runners' distances. The policeman runs the thief's distance PLUS the starting gap.

Likely next

Two trains start towards each other from places 330 km apart at 50 and 60 km/h. After how long do they meet?

The gap closes at 110 km/h: 330 ÷ 110 = 3 hours.

A cyclist leaves 2 hours after a walker at 6 km/h, riding at 10 km/h on the same road. When does he catch up?

Walker's head start = 12 km; gap closes at 4 km/h; 3 hours after the cyclist starts.

Chapter 03 · SPEED FAMILY · 2025 Q59, Q82

Trains: poles, platforms and other trains

A train question is a relative-speed question where the length of the train counts. The front of the train must travel until its back end has cleared whatever it is passing.

FIG_002A pole has no length; a platform and another train add their lengths.

3.1Worked: the two 2025 questions

A train 100 m long takes 6 seconds to cross a man walking at 5 km/h in the opposite direction. Find the train's speed. 2025 Q59 Relative speed = 100/6 m/s = 100/6 × 18/5 = 60 km/h. Opposite directions add, so train + 5 = 60 and the train runs at 55 km/h.

A train 220 m long runs at 59 km/h. How long does it take to pass a man running at 7 km/h towards it? 2025 Q82 Opposite directions, so 59 + 7 = 66 km/h = 66 × 5/18 = 55/3 m/s. Time = 220 ÷ 55/3 = 12 seconds.

Both questions are one family, asked from two sides: find the speed, then find the time. The next paper may ask for the length (given speed and time) or put the man on the same side (subtract).

3.2Pole and platform together

A train passes a pole in 10 s and a 200 m platform in 20 s. The extra 10 s cover the 200 m platform, so the speed is 20 m/s and the train is 20 × 10 = 200 m long.

3.3Two trains

Trains of 100 m and 200 m at 40 and 50 km/h, opposite ways: 300 m at 90 km/h = 25 m/s, so 12 seconds. Same pair going the same way: 300 m at 10 km/h = 25/9 m/s, so 108 seconds.

3.4After they meet

Two trains start at the same time towards each other. After meeting, one takes a hours and the other b hours to finish. Then speed of first: speed of second = √b: √a. With 4 hours and 9 hours, the ratio is 3: 2. The faster train is the one with less time left.

Likely next

A train at 72 km/h crosses a 250 m bridge in 25 seconds. Length of the train?

20 m/s × 25 = 500 m in all, so the train is 250 m.

A 150 m train passes a man walking at 6 km/h in the same direction in 10 seconds. Speed of the train?

15 m/s = 54 km/h relative, so the train runs at 60 km/h.

Two trains, 120 m and 80 m, cross each other in 10 s going opposite ways. If one runs at 30 km/h, the other?

20 m/s = 72 km/h together, so 42 km/h.

Chapter 04 · SPEED FAMILY · 2025 Q60, Q65

Races and circular tracks

A race is two runners on the same road. Three phrases do all the work, so learn what each one means.

PhraseMeaning
A beats B by 20 mwhen A finishes, B is 20 m short of the line
A beats B by 5 secondsB finishes 5 seconds after A
A gives B a start of 20 mB starts 20 m ahead, so B runs 20 m less
dead heatboth reach the line together

4.1Beats by metres AND by seconds

"A beats B by 28 metres or 7 seconds" means B needs 7 seconds to run those last 28 m. So B's speed = 28 ÷ 7 = 4 m/s. This is the key step. 2025 Q60 In a 1 km race, B takes 1000 ÷ 4 = 250 s, and A finishes 7 s earlier: 243 s = 4 min 3 s.

4.2Head start and still wins

In a 100 m race, A runs at 8 km/h. A gives B a start of 4 m and still beats him by 15 seconds. Find B's speed. 2025 Q65 8 km/h = 20/9 m/s, so A takes 100 ÷ 20/9 = 45 s. B runs only 96 m but takes 45 + 15 = 60 s. B's speed = 96/60 = 1.6 m/s = 1.6 × 18/5 = 5.76 km/h.

4.3Chains

In a km race, A beats B by 100 m and B beats C by 100 m. When A runs 1,000, B runs 900; when B runs 900, C runs 900 × 900/1000 = 810. So A beats C by 190 m, not 200.

4.4Circular tracks

On a closed track the runners meet when the faster one gains one full lap (same direction) or when together they cover one full lap (opposite directions). On a 400 m track at 6 and 4 m/s: same direction, 400 ÷ 2 = 200 s; opposite, 400 ÷ 10 = 40 s. To meet again at the starting point, take the LCM of their lap times.

Likely next

In a 500 m race A beats B by 50 m or 10 seconds. A's time?

B's speed 5 m/s, B's time 100 s, so A takes 90 s.

A runs 7/4 times as fast as B. What start must A give B so they finish 84 m together?

Distances 7: 4 in the same time; B runs 4/7 × 84 = 48 m, so a start of 36 m.

Chapter 05 · SPEED FAMILY · downstream and upstream

Boats and streams

A river adds its own speed to everything floating in it. Going downstream (with the water), the stream helps: D = b + s. Going upstream (against it), the stream slows the boat: U = b − s. Here b is the boat's speed in still water and s is the stream's speed.

FIG_003The stream helps one way and hurts the other by the same amount.

Two lines answer most questions. Boat = (D + U) ÷ 2 and stream = (D − U) ÷ 2. If a boat goes downstream at 20 km/h and upstream at 12 km/h, the boat is 16 km/h and the stream 4 km/h.

5.1There and back

A boat at 8 km/h in still water, on a stream of 2 km/h, goes 30 km and back. Down at 10 km/h takes 3 hours; up at 6 km/h takes 5 hours. Total 8 hours. If the question gives the total time and asks the distance, write d/D + d/U = total time and solve.

5.2Upstream takes n times as long

If going up takes n times as long as coming down the same distance, then b: s = (n + 1): (n − 1). Twice as long gives 3: 1; three times gives 2: 1.

Three traps

  • Using the still-water speed for a trip on the river.
  • Giving the stream's speed when the question asks for the boat's.
  • Giving the downstream speed (b + s) as the boat's speed.

Likely next

A man rows 24 km downstream in 3 hours and 12 km upstream in 3 hours. Speed of the stream?

D = 8, U = 4; stream = (8 − 4) ÷ 2 = 2 km/h.

A man rows at 6 km/h in still water. It takes him 3 times as long upstream as downstream. Stream speed?

b: s = 4: 2 = 2: 1, so s = 3 km/h.

Chapter 07 · NUMBER SYSTEM · 2025 Q100

Divisibility: test a number without dividing

The number system is the biggest arithmetic group left: about 21 weighted questions across the three papers, more than any other family. It has four parts: divisibility, remainders, HCF and LCM, and decimals. They all rest on one idea: what divides what, and what is left over. This chapter starts with the quickest tool, the divisibility tests. They tell you whether a number divides exactly without doing the division.

7.1The tests you need

7.2Big divisors: split into co-prime parts

To test 12, 15, 18, 36, 45, 72 or 90, split the divisor into two parts that share no factor (co-prime parts), and test both. 12 = 3 × 4, 15 = 3 × 5, 18 = 2 × 9, 36 = 4 × 9, 45 = 5 × 9, 72 = 8 × 9, 90 = 9 × 10.

The parts must be co-prime. 12 = 2 × 6 does not work: 18 divides by 2 and by 6, but not by 12.

7.3The 2025 question: unknown digits

If the number 653xy is divisible by 90, then (x + y) = ? 2025 Q100 90 = 9 × 10. Divisible by 10 means y = 0. Divisible by 9 means the digit sum 6 + 5 + 3 + x + 0 = 14 + x is a multiple of 9, so x = 4. x + y = 4.

The next angle is the same number with 72 = 8 × 9. Then 3xy (the last three digits) must divide by 8, and 14 + x + y by 9, so x + y is 4 or 13. That gives two answers (65304 and 65376), so the examiner will add a hint such as "y = 4". With y = 4: 3x4 divides by 8 for x = 0, 4 or 8, and 18 + x divides by 9 for x = 0 or 9. Only x = 0 passes both.

7, 11 and 13 together

7 × 11 × 13 = 1001. So any six-digit number made by writing a three-digit number twice (such as 386386 = 386 × 1001) divides by 7, 11 and 13.

Likely next in this chapter

If 85x47 is divisible by 11, what is x?

Odd places from the left: 8 + x + 7 = 15 + x. Even places: 5 + 4 = 9. The difference 6 + x must be 0 or 11, so x = 5 (85547 = 11 × 7777).

If 7x52y is divisible by 45 and y = 5, what is x?

45 = 5 × 9. y = 5 is fine for 5. Digit sum 7 + x + 5 + 2 + 5 = 19 + x must divide by 9, so x = 8.

Is 2,345,678 divisible by 4?

Last two digits 78. 78 ÷ 4 leaves 2. No.

Chapter 08 · NUMBER SYSTEM · 2025 Q56, Q70 · 2018 Q52 · 2021 Q82

Remainders: least number to add or take away

A remainder is what is left after division. Every number can be written as number = divisor × quotient + remainder, and the remainder is always smaller than the divisor. Four question types grow from this one line.

8.1Least number to take away or add

What least number must be subtracted from 1672 to obtain a number which is completely divisible by 17? 2025 Q56 1672 = 17 × 98 + 6. Take away the remainder: 6.

To add, take the remainder from the divisor. 1672 needs 17 − 6 = 11 more to reach 1683 = 17 × 99.

What least number of five digits is exactly divisible by 41? 2018 Q52 The smallest five-digit number is 10,000. 10,000 = 41 × 243 + 37, so add 41 − 37 = 4: 10,004. For the largest four-digit number, start at 9,999 and take away the remainder: the largest four-digit multiple of 88 is 9,999 − 55 = 9,944.

8.2How many multiples between two numbers

How many natural numbers are there between 23 and 100 which are exactly divisible by 6? 2021 Q82 The first is 24 and the last is 96. Count = (last − first) ÷ 6 + 1 = 72 ÷ 6 + 1 = 13. The "+ 1" counts the first one; leaving it out is the classic trap.

8.3Successive division

A number was divided successively in order by 4, 5 and 6. The remainders were respectively 2, 3 and 4. The number is: 2025 Q70 "Successively" means: divide the number by 4, then divide the quotient by 5, then that quotient by 6.

Work backwards from the last remainder. Start with 4 (the last remainder, taking the last quotient as 0). Multiply by the divisor before it and add its remainder: 4 × 5 + 3 = 23. Again: 23 × 4 + 2 = 94. So 94 is the smallest such number. Every number 94 + 120k also works (120 = 4 × 5 × 6), which is why the 2025 answer was 214 = 94 + 120. Check: 214 ÷ 4 = 53 r 2; 53 ÷ 5 = 10 r 3; 10 ÷ 6 = 1 r 4.

FIG_004Work backwards from the last remainder; add the product of the divisors for the next answers.

8.4Using a remainder again

A number divided by 899 leaves 63. What does it leave when divided by 29? Since 899 = 29 × 31, the number is (29 × 31 × q) + 63. The first part divides by 29, so the answer is 63 ÷ 29, which leaves 5. This only works when the new divisor is a factor of the old one.

8.5Last digit of a power

The last digit of 7, 7², 7³, 7⁴ goes 7, 9, 3, 1 and then repeats every 4. To find the last digit of 7⁹⁵, divide the power by 4: 95 leaves 3, so the last digit is the 3rd in the cycle: 3.

Likely next in this chapter

Least number to be added to 3,000 to make it divisible by 19?

3,000 = 19 × 157 + 17. Add 19 − 17 = 2.

A number divided successively by 3 and 5 leaves 1 and 4. The least such number?

Backwards: 4 × 3 + 1 = 13. Check: 13 ÷ 3 = 4 r 1; 4 ÷ 5 = 0 r 4.

Last digit of 3⁴⁰?

40 divides by 4 exactly, so take the 4th in 3, 9, 7, 1: the last digit is 1.

Chapter 09 · NUMBER SYSTEM · 2025 Q61, Q79, Q84 · 2018 Q53, Q82 · 2021 Q52, Q53

HCF and LCM

The HCF (highest common factor) is the biggest number that divides all the given numbers. The LCM (lowest common multiple) is the smallest number that all of them divide. The key to every question is to decide which one is asked. "Largest measure", "greatest number that divides", "biggest size" → HCF. "Together again", "least number divisible by", "smallest number" → LCM.

9.1Finding them with prime factors

Write each number as a product of prime powers. HCF takes the lowest power of each common prime; LCM takes the highest power of every prime.

FIG_005HCF takes the lowest power of each common prime; LCM takes the highest power of every prime.

For two numbers only: HCF × LCM = first × second. Check: 12 × 144 = 1728 = 36 × 48. This rule does not work for three numbers.

9.2"Together again": LCM

Four devices beep every 30 minutes, 1 hour, 1½ hours and 1 hour 45 minutes. All beeped together at 12 noon. They will again beep together at: 2025 Q61 Change everything to minutes: 30, 60, 90 and 105. LCM = 2² × 3² × 5 × 7 = 1260 minutes = 21 hours. 12 noon + 21 hours = 9 a.m.

9.3"Largest measure": HCF

Three containers hold 496, 403 and 713 litres. What biggest measure can measure all of them exactly? 2025 Q84 496 = 31 × 16, 403 = 31 × 13, 713 = 31 × 23. The HCF is 31 litres. The twist the examiner may add: "how many such measures are needed in all?" 16 + 13 + 23 = 52.

9.4Numbers from a ratio

Two numbers are in the ratio 15: 11 and their HCF is 13. The numbers are: 2025 Q79 Write them as 15x and 11x; since 15 and 11 share no factor, the HCF is x = 13. The numbers are 195 and 143. If the LCM is given instead: for 4: 5 with LCM 180, the LCM is 4 × 5 × x = 20x = 180, so x = 9 and the numbers are 36 and 45.

9.6HCF and LCM of fractions

HCF of fractions = HCF of the tops ÷ LCM of the bottoms. LCM of fractions = LCM of the tops ÷ HCF of the bottoms. Write each fraction in its lowest terms first.

What is the HCF of 1/5, 2/7 and 3/11? 2021 Q53 HCF(1, 2, 3) = 1; LCM(5, 7, 11) = 385. The HCF is 1/385. 2018 asked the same with 9/10, 12/25, 18/35 and 21/40: HCF(9, 12, 18, 21) = 3 and LCM(10, 25, 35, 40) = 1400, so 3/1400. 2018 Q53

HCF or LCM?

The most common mistake is to pick the wrong one. Ask yourself: is the answer bigger than the given numbers (LCM: a time, a total) or smaller (HCF: a size, a measure)? The HCF can never be bigger than the smallest number, and the LCM can never be smaller than the biggest.

Likely next in this chapter

Bells toll every 9, 12 and 15 seconds and start together. How many times do they toll together in 30 minutes, counting the start?

LCM = 180 s = 3 minutes. 30 ÷ 3 = 10, plus the start: 11 times.

HCF is 12, LCM is 336 and one number is 48. The other?

12 × 336 ÷ 48 = 84.

LCM of 2/3, 4/9 and 5/6?

LCM(2, 4, 5) = 20; HCF(3, 9, 6) = 3. Answer 20/3.

Chapter 10 · NUMBER SYSTEM · 2021 Q93 = 2025 Q71

Number facts: consecutive numbers, digits and factors

Some questions test what you know about numbers themselves: consecutive numbers, the digits of a two-digit number, and how many factors a number has. The examiner repeated one of these word for word, so it is worth learning the shortcut.

10.1Consecutive numbers and the difference of squares

What is the sum of two consecutive even numbers, the difference of whose squares is 84? 2021 Q93 2025 Q71 The long way: (n + 2)² − n² = 4n + 4 = 84, so n = 20, the numbers are 20 and 22, and the sum is 42.

The shortcut comes from a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b). For consecutive numbers the difference is 1, so the difference of squares equals the sum. For consecutive even or odd numbers the difference is 2, so the difference of squares is twice the sum. Here 84 ÷ 2 = 42 at once.

  • Three consecutive numbers (or even, or odd numbers) add up to 3 × the middle one. Sum 138 of three consecutive even numbers: the middle is 46, so they are 44, 46, 48.
  • Sum of the first n natural numbers = n(n + 1) ÷ 2. Sum of the first n odd numbers = n².

10.2Two-digit numbers and their reverses

A two-digit number with digits a and b is 10a + b, not ab. Its reverse is 10b + a. So:

  • number − reverse = 9 × (a − b)
  • number + reverse = 11 × (a + b)

Example: a number is 4 times the sum of its digits, and adding 27 reverses its digits. 10a + b = 4(a + b) gives b = 2a. Adding 27 reverses it, so 9(b − a) = 27 and b − a = 3. Then a = 3, b = 6, and the number is 36.

10.3How many factors

Write the number as prime powers, add 1 to each power, and multiply. 72 = 2³ × 3², so it has (3 + 1)(2 + 1) = 12 factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, 18, 24, 36, 72.

Decimals, recurring decimals and roots

2018 and 2021 each opened the maths section with decimal questions: a product in standard form, a sum of recurring decimals and a square root. They are quick marks if you know three small rules.

11.1Multiplying decimals: count the places

Multiply the numbers as if there were no points, then put back as many decimal places as the two numbers had together.

0.04 × 0.0162 = ? 2018 Q54 4 × 162 = 648. Decimal places: 2 + 4 = 6, so 0.000648 = 6.48 × 10⁻⁴. In 2021 it came again with new digits: 0.03 × 0.0165 = 0.000495 = 4.95 × 10⁻⁴ 2021 Q54. Standard form means one digit before the point, times a power of 10; count how many places the point moved to the right to get the negative power.

11.2Recurring decimals to fractions

A bar over digits means they repeat forever: 0.444… is written 0.4 with a bar.

  • All digits repeat: put the repeating digits over as many 9s. 0.444… = 4/9; 0.3737… = 37/99.
  • Some digits do not repeat: (all the digits − the non-repeating part) over as many 9s as repeating digits, followed by as many 0s as non-repeating digits. 0.3666… = (36 − 3)/90 = 33/90 = 11/30.

0.6868… + 0.7373… = ? 2021 Q56 68/99 + 73/99 = 141/99 = 1 42/99 = 1.4242… Add recurring decimals as fractions, never digit by digit.

11.3Square roots of decimals

Pair the decimal places. √1.5625: 15625 = 125², and 4 decimal places become 2, so 1.25 2021 Q63. √0.000064: 64 = 8², and 6 places become 3: 0.008. A decimal with an odd number of places is not a perfect square as it stands: √0.0009 = 0.03, but √0.009 is not a simple decimal.

The traps

  • 12 = 2 × 6 is not a valid split; the parts must be co-prime (3 × 4).
  • In successive division the smallest answer may not be an option; add the product of the divisors (94 → 214).
  • "Between 23 and 100" counts from the first multiple inside, and the "+ 1" is easy to forget.
  • HCF × LCM = product works for two numbers only.
  • A two-digit number is 10a + b, not a × b.
  • If the power divides by 4, the last digit is the 4th in its cycle, not the first.
