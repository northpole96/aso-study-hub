Probability: the words and the one formula

Probability is the maths of chance. It gave 10 questions in 2018 and 9 in 2021, so it is the biggest block in this guide. Every one of them uses the same formula. The skill is counting correctly.

1.1The words

  • An experiment is something we do whose result is not fixed: toss a coin, throw a die, pick a marble from a bag.
  • An outcome is one possible result: heads, or the number 4, or the red marble.
  • The sample space is the list of all possible outcomes. Tossing one coin: {H, T}. Throwing one die: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}.
  • An event is the result we are interested in: "an even number", "a prime", "a vowel".
  • Favourable outcomes are the outcomes that make the event happen.
  • A die is the cube with 1 to 6 dots. More than one are dice.
  • All outcomes are taken as equally likely (the coin is fair, the die is fair, the pick is at random).

The formula

P(event) = number of favourable outcomes ÷ total number of outcomes. Count both with care, then write the fraction.

1.2What the answer can be

A probability is always between 0 and 1. 0 means it cannot happen (a 7 on one die). 1 means it must happen (a number from 1 to 6 on one die). You can write it as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage. If you get 1.2 or −0.3, you have made a mistake.

1.3The event and its opposite 2018 Q79

If p is the probability that the event happens and q the probability that it does not, then p + q = 1. This is the answer to 2018 Q79 (option B). It gives the commonest shortcut in probability: P(not E) = 1 − P(E).

2021 Q79 A batsman hits a boundary 15 times in 60 balls. P(boundary) = 15 ÷ 60 = 0.25. P(no boundary) = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75.

  • 2018 Q84 An even number from 1 to 100: 50 even numbers out of 100, 1/2.

1.6The calendar question 2021 Q80

A year has 365 days = 52 weeks (364 days) + 1 extra day. Each of the 52 weeks has one Sunday, so the question is whether the one extra day is a Sunday. It could be any of the 7 days, so the probability of 53 Sundays is 1/7. In a leap year there are 2 extra days, so 2 of the 7 possible pairs contain a Sunday: 2/7.

1.7Two numbers picked together 2021 Q85

x is chosen from 1, 2, 3 and y from 1, 4, 9. Find P(xy < 9). List every pair, 3 × 3 = 9 pairs: (1,1)=1, (1,4)=4, (1,9)=9, (2,1)=2, (2,4)=8, (2,9)=18, (3,1)=3, (3,4)=12, (3,9)=27. Products below 9: 1, 4, 2, 8, 3, so 5 pairs. Answer 5/9.

Traps in the counting

  • Count 1 as not prime and 0 as even.
  • "More than 3" on a die is 4, 5, 6 (not 3). "At least 3" includes 3.
  • If the question says "numbers from 1 to 25", count both ends.

1.8Past questions of this chapter, word for word

2018 Q79

Q. For an event, if p is the probability of the happening of the event and q is the probability of not happening, then which of the following is true?

(A) p > q (B) p + q = 1 (C) p < q (D) p + q = 0

Answer: B.

Think of one trial. Either the event happens (chance p) or it does not (chance q). There is no third thing that can happen, so together they must cover every possibility, and every possibility together has probability 1.

So p + q = 1. The other options fail: p > q or p < q depends on the event, and p + q = 0 would mean nothing can happen.

Answer: 1/2.

Step 2: favourable outcomes are the even numbers 2, 4, …, 100. They are every second number, so 100 ÷ 2 = 50 of them.

Step 3: probability = 50 ÷ 100 = 1/2.

Q. Find the probability that a non-leap year has 53 Sundays:

Coins and dice: count with a table

Coins and dice came in both papers, so we make a little table once and read every answer from it.

2.1Coins

One coin has 2 outcomes. Two coins have 2 × 2 = 4. Three coins have 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. n coins have 2ⁿ outcomes.

The number of ways to get a number of heads follows a pattern. For 4 coins (16 outcomes in all):

Heads01234
Ways14641

So 4 coins, exactly 2 heads: 6/16 = 3/8 2021 Q90. The ways come from "4 choose 2" = 6 (explained in chapter 3). For 3 coins the row is 1, 3, 3, 1.

  • 2018 Q81 Three coins, all heads: 1 way of 8 = 1/8.
  • Three coins, at least one head: 1 − P(no head) = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8.
  • Three coins, exactly 2 heads: 3/8. At least 2 heads: (3 + 1)/8 = 1/2.

2.2One die

Six equally likely outcomes. 2021 Q92 More than 3 means 4, 5, 6: 3 of 6 = 1/2. An even number: 1/2. A number divisible by 3 (3, 6): 1/3.

2.3Two dice: draw the table

There are 6 × 6 = 36 outcomes. The figure shows them all, each cell holding the total of the two dice. Read the answer by counting the cells.

FIG_003Draw the 36-cell table once; every dice question is a count of cells.

  • 2018 Q92 Total 5: (1,4) (2,3) (3,2) (4,1), 4 cells of 36 = 1/9.
  • 2018 Q80 Total 6: (1,5) (2,4) (3,3) (4,2) (5,1), 5 cells = 5/36.
  • Total 7: 6 cells = 1/6, the likeliest total.
  • Total 9: (3,6) (4,5) (5,4) (6,3) = 4/36 = 1/9.
  • A doublet (same number on both): (1,1)…(6,6) = 6/36 = 1/6.
  • Total at least 10: totals 10, 11, 12 have 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 ways: 1/6.
  • Total at most 4: totals 2, 3, 4 have 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 ways: 1/6.

The "ways for each total" pattern, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, is worth learning. It saves drawing the table.

Traps with dice

  • (2,3) and (3,2) are different outcomes when two dice are thrown, so a total of 5 has 4 ways, not 2.
  • "Thrown together" or "thrown once" does not change the count. Still 36.
  • 2018 Q80 had "None of these" as option D, and 5/36 as A. Do not pick D by habit.

Bags, socks, balls and cards: choosing without looking

When several things are drawn from a bag, listing is too slow. We use combinations, the number of ways to choose a few from many when the order does not matter.

3.1Combinations: nCr

nCr means "the number of ways to choose r things from n". Read it as "n choose r".

The formula

nCr = n × (n − 1) × … (r terms) ÷ r! where r! (r factorial) means r × (r − 1) × … × 1. So 5C2 = (5 × 4) ÷ (2 × 1) = 10, and 9C2 = (9 × 8) ÷ 2 = 36.

  • nCr = nC(n − r). 15C11 is the same as 15C4 (choose the 4 to leave out) = (15 × 14 × 13 × 12) ÷ 24 = 1,365 2025 Q88.
  • nC0 = nC(n) = 1. nC1 = n.
  • 14C5 = (14 × 13 × 12 × 11 × 10) ÷ 120 = 2,002.

3.2Bag type 1: two items, same colour 2018 Q91

A bag has 5 brown and 4 white socks. Two are pulled out. Find P(same colour).

  • Step 1: total ways to pick 2 of the 9 socks: 9C2 = 36.
  • Step 2: both brown: 5C2 = 10. Both white: 4C2 = 6. Same colour: 10 + 6 = 16.
  • Step 3: P = 16 ÷ 36 = 4/9.

The word "or" (both brown or both white) means we add the two counts.

3.3Bag type 2: some of each colour 2018 Q93

A bag has 8 red and 6 blue balls. Five are drawn. Find P(3 red and 2 blue).

  • Total ways: 14C5 = 2,002.
  • Favourable: choose 3 of the 8 reds (8C3 = 56) and 2 of the 6 blues (6C2 = 15). The word "and" means we multiply: 56 × 15 = 840.
  • P = 840 ÷ 2,002 = 60/143 (divide top and bottom by 14).

Add for "or", multiply for "and"

Different cases (both brown or both white) are added. Different choices that happen together (3 reds and 2 blues) are multiplied.

3.4Bag type 3: two draws one after another

Without replacement means the first item is not put back, so the bag has one less. A bag has 5 red and 3 blue marbles. Two are drawn. P(both red)?

  • By choosing: 5C2 ÷ 8C2 = 10 ÷ 28 = 5/14.
  • By steps: P(first red) = 5/8; then 4 reds are left among 7: 4/7. Multiply: 5/8 × 4/7 = 20/56 = 5/14. Same.

With replacement the item goes back, so the bag is the same for the second draw: P(both red) = 5/8 × 5/8 = 25/64.

3.5Cards

A pack has 52 cards: 4 suits (spades and clubs are black, hearts and diamonds are red), 13 cards in each suit (A, 2 to 10, J, Q, K). The face cards are J, Q, K: 12 in all. There are 4 aces, 4 kings and 26 red cards.

  • One card drawn. P(a heart) = 13/52 = 1/4. P(a king) = 4/52 = 1/13. P(a face card) = 12/52 = 3/13. P(a red card) = 1/2.
  • P(a king or a heart) = (13 + 4 − 1) ÷ 52 = 16/52 = 4/13. We take away 1 because the king of hearts was counted twice.

Two events: AND, OR and independent events

Some questions give the probability of two events A and B and ask for the chance of both, or either.

4.1The words

  • A ∪ B (read "A or B") is the event that at least one of them happens.
  • A ∩ B (read "A and B") is the event that both happen.
  • B′ (read "B not") means B does not happen. P(B′) = 1 − P(B).
  • Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot happen together (one die: "a 2" and "a 5"). Then P(A and B) = 0.
  • Two events are independent if one does not change the chance of the other (two separate coin tosses).

4.2The two rules

Addition and multiplication

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B). We take off the "both" part because adding counted it twice. If A and B are mutually exclusive, the last part is 0.

If A and B are independent: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).

4.3Worked: find P(B) 2018 Q90

A and B are independent. P(A) = 0.3 and P(A or B) = 0.8. Find P(B).

  • Let P(B) = p. Then P(A and B) = 0.3 × p.
  • P(A or B) = 0.3 + p − 0.3p = 0.3 + 0.7p.
  • Set it equal to 0.8: 0.7p = 0.5, so p = 5/7. Answer 5/7.

4.4The twisted twin 2021 Q91

The next paper asked almost the same, but with A or B′: P(A) = 0.3, P(A ∪ B′) = 0.8, A and B independent. Then A and B′ are also independent. Let P(B′) = q.

  • P(A or B′) = 0.3 + q − 0.3q = 0.3 + 0.7q = 0.8, so q = 5/7.
  • P(B) = 1 − q = 1 − 5/7 = 2/7.

So the twist is just one extra step at the end: from P(B′) to P(B). Read whether the question uses B or B′.

4.5More checks to be able to do

Let P(A) = 0.4 and P(B) = 0.5, independent.

  • P(A and B) = 0.4 × 0.5 = 0.2.
  • P(A or B) = 0.4 + 0.5 − 0.2 = 0.7.
  • P(neither) = 1 − 0.7 = 0.3. (Or 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.3.)
  • P(exactly one) = 0.7 − 0.2 = 0.5.

If instead they are mutually exclusive: P(A or B) = 0.4 + 0.5 = 0.9 and P(A and B) = 0.

Do not mix them up

Independent and mutually exclusive are different. Independent events can both happen (P(A and B) is not 0). Mutually exclusive events can never both happen. If two events with non-zero probabilities are mutually exclusive, they are not independent.

Step 1: let P(B) = p. For independent events P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) = 0.3p.

Step 2: use the addition rule P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B): 0.8 = 0.3 + p − 0.3p.

Step 3: 0.8 − 0.3 = p(1 − 0.3), so 0.5 = 0.7p.

Step 4: p = 0.5 ÷ 0.7 = 5/7.

Average (mean): the most asked statistics idea

We start with words, because the whole block is built from them.

  • Data is just the list of numbers we are given: marks of five students, ages in a family, weights of boys.
  • A variate (or observation, or item) is one number of the list. If the list is 12, 15, 18, then 15 is one variate.
  • Frequency tells how many times a value occurs.
  • The mean (also called the average) is what each item would be if the total were shared equally.

5.1The one formula

Add all the items and divide by how many there are.

Mean

Mean = total ÷ count. The other way round, which you will use more: total = mean × count.

Example: marks 12, 15, 18, 20, 25. Total = 90. Count = 5. Mean = 90 ÷ 5 = 18.

Nearly every exam question is solved by moving between the mean and the total. So the first habit is: turn each average into a total.

5.2Type 1: one item is missing 2018 Q96

The mean of 7 variates is 12. Six of them are 5, 13, 9, 17, 14 and 10. Find the 7th.

  • Step 1: turn the average into a total. 12 × 7 = 84.
  • Step 2: add the six known items: 5 + 13 + 9 + 17 + 14 + 10 = 68.
  • Step 3: the 7th item is what is left: 84 − 68 = 16.

Note that 12 is the mean. It could not be the sum, because the six known items alone already add to 68.

5.3Type 2: two groups joined into one 2018 Q95 2025 Q68

This is the one type that came in all three years. A class of 100 students has 70 boys with an average of 75 marks. The average of the whole class is 72. Find the average of the girls.

  • Step 1: the whole class total = 100 × 72 = 7,200.
  • Step 2: the boys' total = 70 × 75 = 5,250.
  • Step 3: the girls' total = 7,200 − 5,250 = 1,950. There are 100 − 70 = 30 girls.
  • Step 4: the girls' average = 1,950 ÷ 30 = 65.

The 2025 version went the other way round. 16 boys average 50.25 kg and the other 8 boys average 45.15 kg. The average of all 24 is (16 × 50.25 + 8 × 45.15) ÷ 24 = (804 + 361.2) ÷ 24 = 48.55 kg.

A shortcut for two groups

The combined mean always lies between the two group means, and closer to the bigger group. For 16 boys and 8 boys the sizes are 2: 1. The gap between the means is 50.25 − 45.15 = 5.1. The mean is nearer the bigger group (the 16 boys), so it sits one part in three of the gap below 50.25. 5.1 ÷ 3 = 1.7, and 50.25 − 1.7 = 48.55. Same answer, no big multiplication.

5.4Type 3: parts of a list 2021 Q95

The average of 30 numbers is 12. The average of the first 20 is 11 and of the next 9 is 10. Find the last number.

  • Total of 30 numbers = 30 × 12 = 360.
  • First 20 = 20 × 11 = 220. Next 9 = 9 × 10 = 90. Together 310.
  • 20 + 9 = 29 numbers are used, so one number is left: 360 − 310 = 50.

5.5Type 4: pairs of averages 2021 Q96

The average of A and B is 20, of B and C is 19, of C and A is 21. Find A.

  • Turn each average into a total of two: A + B = 40, B + C = 38, C + A = 42.
  • Add all three: 2(A + B + C) = 120, so A + B + C = 60.
  • A = 60 − (B + C) = 60 − 38 = 22.

The same trick finds B (60 − 42 = 18) and C (60 − 40 = 20). Check: B + C = 18 + 20 = 38. ✓

5.6Type 5: the mean of a pattern 2021 Q98

When numbers go up by equal steps, the mean is simply the middle one, which is also the average of the first and the last.

  • The first 5 multiples of 7 are 7, 14, 21, 28, 35. The middle is 21, so the mean is 21. (Check: total 105 ÷ 5 = 21.)
  • The mean of the first n natural numbers is (n + 1) ÷ 2. For 1 to 9 it is 5.
  • The mean of the first n even numbers (2, 4, 6…) is n + 1. For the first 10 it is 11.
  • The mean of the first n odd numbers (1, 3, 5…) is n. For the first 8 it is 8.

5.7Type 6: the sum of deviations 2021 Q97 2021 Q99

A deviation is how far an item is from the mean: item − mean. Take the list 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 14. The total is 42 and the mean is 7. The deviations are −4, −3, −1, 0, 1, 7. Add them: 0.

Always zero

The sum of deviations from the mean is always 0, for any list. The items below the mean cancel the items above it. Both 2021 questions (Q97 and Q99, which asked it for "P values") have the answer 0. If you see this question, do not calculate; write 0.

5.8Type 7: what happens when the list changes

  • Add a number to every item: the mean goes up by that number. (Marks 10, 20, 30 have mean 20. Add 5 to each, the mean is 25.)
  • Multiply every item: the mean is multiplied too.
  • One item is replaced: the total changes by the difference, so the mean changes by (difference ÷ count). Ten numbers have a mean of 20. One 15 is replaced by 45. The total rises by 30, so the mean rises by 30 ÷ 10 = 3, to 23.
  • A new item joins: five numbers average 20 (total 100). A sixth number, 32, joins. The new mean is 132 ÷ 6 = 22.
  • An item leaves: five numbers average 20 and one of them is 30. The other four total 100 − 30 = 70, so they average 17.5.

Traps in mean questions

  • Do not average two averages by just adding and halving. 50 boys at 80 and 10 girls at 60 do not average 70. The mean is (4,000 + 600) ÷ 60 = 76.67.
  • Count the items carefully. "From 5 to 25" has 21 whole numbers, not 20.
  • The question may say "average" or "mean". They are the same word here.

Median, mode and quartiles

The mean is not the only "middle". Two more are asked, and each has its own job.

  • The median is the middle item when the list is put in order.
  • The mode is the item that occurs most often.
  • A quartile cuts the ordered list into four equal parts. Q1 (lower quartile) has a quarter of the items below it. Q3 (upper quartile) has a quarter above it. The median is Q2.

FIG_001One odd large value drags the mean away; median and mode stay put.

6.1Finding the median

  • Step 1: arrange the numbers in order, smallest to largest. Skipping this is the one common mistake.
  • Step 2: if the count is odd, the median is the middle item, the ((n + 1) ÷ 2)th one.
  • Step 3: if the count is even, there are two middle items. Take their average.

Examples:

  • 7, 3, 9, 5, 11 → in order 3, 5, 7, 9, 11. Five items, the 3rd is the middle: 7.
  • 4, 8, 6, 10 → in order 4, 6, 8, 10. Four items, the middle two are 6 and 8: median = (6 + 8) ÷ 2 = 7.

6.2Finding the mode

Count how often each value occurs. The most frequent one is the mode. In 2, 3, 3, 5, 7, 8, 14 the mode is 3. A list can have two modes (2, 2, 5, 5, 9) or no mode (all different).

6.3When to use which

The figure above shows why there are three measures. The big item 14 pulls the mean up to 6, but the median (5) and the mode (3) do not move. So for things with a few huge values, like house prices or salaries, the median describes the "typical" better than the mean.

The link between the three

For a roughly smooth, one-humped spread: Mode = 3 × Median − 2 × Mean. If the mean is 20 and the median is 21, the mode is 63 − 40 = 23. You can use it to find any one of the three when the other two are given.

  • Step 1: in order: 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 17, 19. Seven items; the median is 13.
  • Step 2: the lower half is the items below the median: 7, 9, 11.
  • Step 3: Q1 is the median of that half: 9. (The upper half is 15, 17, 19, so Q3 = 17.)
  • The interquartile range is Q3 − Q1 = 17 − 9 = 8.

Grouped data: mean, median and mode from a table

When there are many numbers, they are shown in a frequency table. Instead of listing 100 marks we write how many students fall in each band. New words:

  • A class (or class interval) is one band, like 20–30.
  • The frequency (f) is how many items fall in that class.
  • The class width (h) is the band's size: 30 − 20 = 10.
  • The class mark (x) is the middle of the class: for 20–30 it is 25.
  • The cumulative frequency (cf) is the running total of frequencies.
  • N is the total of all the frequencies.

The question we will work all through (2021 Q100):

Frequency f81636346
Cumulative cf8246094100

N = 8 + 16 + 36 + 34 + 6 = 100.

FIG_002The median sits in the class where the running total first passes N/2.

7.1The median of grouped data

  • Step 1: find N ÷ 2 = 50.
  • Step 2: look down the cumulative column for the first cf that is 50 or more. That is 60, so the median class is 20–30.
  • Step 3: use the formula.

Median (grouped)

Median = L + [(N/2 − cf) ÷ f] × h. Here L is the lower limit of the median class (20), cf is the cumulative frequency of the class before it (24), f is the frequency of the median class (36), and h is the class width (10).

Median = 20 + [(50 − 24) ÷ 36] × 10 = 20 + 7.22 = 27.22. That is option A of the 2021 paper. The idea: we need 50 items, the classes before this one gave only 24, so we take 26 more out of the 36 in the median class, and 26/36 of the way along its width.

7.2The mean of grouped data

Take every item in a class to be at its class mark. The class marks here are 5, 15, 25, 35, 45.

  • Multiply each class mark by its frequency: 40, 240, 900, 1,190, 270. Total Σfx = 2,640.
  • Mean = Σfx ÷ N = 2,640 ÷ 100 = 26.4.

A shorter way (the assumed mean method) is useful when the numbers are big. Pick a middle class mark as the guess A = 25. Work with the deviations d = x − 25: −20, −10, 0, 10, 20. Then fd = −160, −160, 0, 340, 120, total 140. Mean = A + Σfd ÷ N = 25 + 1.4 = 26.4. Same answer.

7.3The mode of grouped data

The modal class is the class with the highest frequency: 20–30 (f = 36). Its neighbours have frequency 16 (before) and 34 (after).

Mode (grouped)

Mode = L + [(f1 − f0) ÷ (2f1 − f0 − f2)] × h. Here f1 is the modal class frequency (36), f0 is the one before it (16), f2 is the one after it (34).

Mode = 20 + [(36 − 16) ÷ (72 − 16 − 34)] × 10 = 20 + (20 ÷ 22) × 10 = 29.09. Check with the link from the last chapter: 3 × 27.22 − 2 × 26.4 = 28.86, close to 29.09, as it should be.

Traps in grouped questions

  • If the table is given as 10–19, 20–29, 30–39, the gaps hide the real limits. Subtract 0.5 from every lower limit and add 0.5 to every upper limit before using L. (So 20–29 becomes 19.5–29.5.)
  • cf in the median formula is the one before the median class, not the one at it.
  • In a table of single values (not classes), the mode is the value with the highest frequency. The answer is that value, not the frequency.

Geometric mean, harmonic mean

The ordinary mean adds. Two other means are asked, and both are used when the numbers are connected by multiplying or by rates. The question slot for these was Q94 in both papers.

8.1Geometric mean (GM)

Multiply the n numbers and take the nth root.

Geometric mean

GM = the nth root of (x1 × x2 × … × xn). For two numbers it is √(a × b).

  • 2021 Q94 GM of 4 and 16: √(4 × 16) = √64 = 8.
  • GM of 2 and 8 is √16 = 4. GM of 2, 4, 8 is the cube root of 64 = 4.
  • Notice that the GM of two numbers is always in the middle of them in a multiplying sense: 4 × 2 = 8 and 8 × 2 = 16. A GM question therefore usually has numbers that make a clean square or cube.

8.2The long one: powers of 3 2018 Q94

Find the GM of 3, 3², 3³, …, 3ⁿ.

  • Step 1: multiply: the powers add up. 3¹ × 3² × … × 3ⁿ = 3^(1 + 2 + … + n) = 3^(n(n + 1)/2).
  • Step 2: take the nth root, which divides the power by n: 3^((n + 1)/2).
  • Answer: 3^((n + 1)/2). The power is just the mean of 1, 2, …, n, which is (n + 1)/2. So the shortcut for any list of powers of one number is: the GM is that number raised to the mean of the powers.

8.3Harmonic mean (HM)

The HM is for rates (speed, price per item). It is the number of items divided by the sum of their reciprocals (1 ÷ each).

Harmonic mean

HM = n ÷ (1/x1 + 1/x2 + … + 1/xn). For two numbers it is 2ab ÷ (a + b).

  • HM of 40 and 60 = 2 × 40 × 60 ÷ 100 = 48. This is why a trip at 40 km/h one way and 60 km/h back averages 48 km/h, not 50.
  • The three means always go in this order: AM ≥ GM ≥ HM. For 40 and 60: 50, 48.99, 48. For two numbers, AM × HM = GM²: 50 × 48 = 2,400 = 40 × 60. ✓

Chapter 09 · STATISTICS · 2018 Q99 Q100 · 2021 Q97 Q99

Spread: range, variance and standard deviation

The mean tells where the middle is. Spread (also called dispersion) tells how far the numbers wander from it. Two classes can both average 50 marks, but one has everyone near 50 and the other has 20s and 80s.

9.1The measures, from simplest

  • Range = largest − smallest. For 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 17, 19 it is 12.
  • Mean deviation is the average of the deviations, ignoring minus signs. (We need to ignore them, because the true sum of deviations is always 0.)
  • Variance is the average of the squared deviations. Squaring removes the minus signs another way.
  • Standard deviation (SD) is the square root of the variance. It is back in the units of the data.

Variance and SD

Variance = (sum of (x − mean)²) ÷ n. SD = √variance.

9.2A worked example

Data: 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9.

  • Step 1: the mean = 40 ÷ 8 = 5.
  • Step 2: deviations: −3, −1, −1, −1, 0, 0, 2, 4.
  • Step 3: square them: 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16. Total 32.
  • Step 4: variance = 32 ÷ 8 = 4. SD = √4 = 2.

9.3Variance of the first n natural numbers 2018 Q99

The answer to learn is (n² − 1) ÷ 12. For 1, 2, 3, 4, 5: mean 3, squared deviations 4, 1, 0, 1, 4 = 10, variance 10 ÷ 5 = 2. And (25 − 1) ÷ 12 = 2. ✓ The SD is the square root of this, √((n² − 1)/12). For 1 to 7 the variance is 48 ÷ 12 = 4 and the SD is 2.

9.4What happens to the spread when the data changes

  • Add the same number to every item: the whole list slides along. The spread does not change. Variance and SD stay the same.
  • Multiply every item by k: the SD is multiplied by k, and the variance by k².

Coefficient of variation

CV = (SD ÷ mean) × 100. It lets you compare two lists of different sizes. A smaller CV means the list is steadier. A list with mean 50 and SD 5 has CV 10%.
