OPSC ASO · Paper I · Topic: Indian Constitution

FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS

Part III of the Constitution · Articles 12 to 35, in simple words.

· · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · · ·

Fundamental Rights are the basic freedoms that every person in India has. They sit in Part III of the Constitution, from Article 12 to Article 35. They are called "fundamental" because they are guaranteed by the Constitution itself, and because a court can enforce them. If the government breaks one, you can go straight to the Supreme Court or a High Court.

The idea was borrowed from the American Bill of Rights. They are often called the "Magna Carta of India". The Constitution lists six groups of rights. A seventh, the Right to Property, was removed in 1978. Remember the six groups first; then each article falls into place under its group.

Two limits to remember. The rights are not unlimited: the State may put "reasonable restrictions" on them (for example, for public order). And Parliament can amend them, but not in a way that destroys the "basic structure" of the Constitution (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).

01 · ARTICLES 12–13

General: who must obey, and what happens to bad laws

Before the rights begin, two articles explain who is bound by them and what happens to a law that breaks them.

ART 12
Definition of "State"
"State" includes the Government and Parliament of India, the Government and Legislature of each State, and all local and other authorities. Fundamental rights are mostly rights against the State, not against private persons. (A few, like Art 17 and 23, work against private persons too.)
ART 13
Laws inconsistent with fundamental rights are void
Any law that breaks a fundamental right is void to that extent. This gives courts the power of judicial review. Old laws made before 1950 and new laws are both covered. "Law" includes ordinances, rules, customs and usage.
02 · ARTICLES 14–18

Right to Equality

Everyone is equal before the law. The State cannot treat people differently because of caste, religion or sex, and it must give everyone a fair chance at public jobs. Special help for weaker groups is allowed, because real equality sometimes needs it.

ART 14
Equality before law and equal protection of laws
"Equality before law" is a British idea (no one is above the law). "Equal protection of laws" is American (equals are treated alike). Reasonable classification is allowed. Applies to all persons, citizens and non-citizens.
ART 15
No discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth
Applies to citizens only. Exceptions: 15(3) special laws for women and children; 15(4) for socially and educationally backward classes, SCs and STs (added by the 1st Amendment, 1951); 15(5) reservation in educational institutions (93rd Amendment, 2005); 15(6) for economically weaker sections, up to 10% (103rd Amendment, 2019).
ART 16
Equality of opportunity in public employment
Citizens only. 16(4) allows reservation for backward classes not enough represented; 16(4A) reservation in promotion for SC/ST; 16(6) 10% for economically weaker sections. The Mandal case (Indra Sawhney, 1992) set the 50% ceiling and excluded the "creamy layer".
ART 17
Abolition of untouchability
Untouchability is abolished and its practice is a punishable offence. The law is the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955. Works against private persons also.
ART 18
Abolition of titles
The State cannot give titles (like Sir or Rai Bahadur). Military and academic distinctions are allowed. Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are not titles and cannot be used as a prefix or suffix (Balaji Raghavan, 1996). A citizen cannot accept a foreign title.
03 · ARTICLES 19–22

Right to Freedom

This is the largest group. Article 19 lists six freedoms for citizens. Articles 20 to 22 protect a person who is accused, arrested or deprived of liberty. Article 21 is the most widely used article in the whole Constitution.

ART 19
Six freedoms (citizens only)
19(1)(a) speech and expression; (b) assemble peacefully without arms; (c) form associations, unions or cooperative societies (cooperative societies added by the 97th Amendment, 2011); (d) move freely throughout India; (e) reside and settle in any part of India; (g) practise any profession, trade or business. The seventh, (f) property, was deleted in 1978. These can be limited by "reasonable restrictions" (19(2)–19(6)) and can be suspended in an Emergency only on the ground of war or external aggression.
ART 20
Protection in respect of conviction for offences
Three protections: (1) no ex post facto law, meaning no punishment under a law made after the act; (2) no double jeopardy, meaning no second trial and punishment for the same offence; (3) no self-incrimination, meaning no one can be forced to be a witness against themselves. Cannot be suspended even in an Emergency.
ART 21
Protection of life and personal liberty
"No person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law." For all persons. In Maneka Gandhi (1978) the court said the procedure must be fair, just and reasonable. It now includes privacy (Puttaswamy, 2017), dignity, clean environment, speedy trial, free legal aid and more. Cannot be suspended in an Emergency.
ART 21A
Right to education
Free and compulsory education for children aged 6 to 14. Added by the 86th Amendment, 2002. The law behind it is the RTE Act, 2009 (in force from 1 April 2010).
ART 22
Protection against arrest and detention
A person arrested must be told the grounds, can consult a lawyer, and must be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours. These rights do not apply to enemy aliens or to people held under preventive detention laws. Preventive detention beyond 3 months needs an Advisory Board.
04 · ARTICLES 23–24

Right against Exploitation

These two articles protect the weak from being used unfairly. Both work against private persons as well as the State.

ART 23
Prohibition of traffic in human beings and forced labour
Bans human trafficking, begar (unpaid forced work) and similar forced labour. The State may ask for compulsory public service, such as national service, but cannot discriminate in doing so. Applies to all persons.
ART 24
No child labour in hazardous work
No child below 14 years may be employed in a factory, mine or other hazardous job.
05 · ARTICLES 25–28

Right to Freedom of Religion

India is a secular State: it has no State religion and treats all faiths alike. These articles let every person follow, spread and manage their religion, within limits of public order, morality and health.

ART 25
Freedom of conscience and free profession, practice and propagation of religion
All persons. Wearing and carrying of the kirpan is part of the Sikh religion. The State may regulate economic, financial or political activity linked to religion, and may open Hindu religious institutions to all Hindus.
ART 26
Freedom to manage religious affairs
Every religious group may set up institutions, manage its own affairs, and own and run property in line with law.
ART 27
No tax for promotion of any religion
No person can be forced to pay a tax whose money goes to support a particular religion. (Fees for services are different from taxes.)
ART 28
Freedom from religious instruction in some institutions
No religious instruction in fully State-funded schools. In State-aided or recognised ones, no one can be forced to attend it. Private institutions may teach religion.
06 · ARTICLES 29–30

Cultural and Educational Rights

These protect minorities, whether religious or linguistic, so that their language, script and culture do not disappear.

ART 29
Protection of interests of minorities
Any group of citizens with its own language, script or culture may keep it. No citizen can be refused admission to a State-run or State-aided institution only on grounds of religion, race, caste or language. Covers minorities and the majority (it says "any section of citizens").
ART 30
Right of minorities to establish and run educational institutions
Religious and linguistic minorities may set up and manage educational institutions of their choice. The State cannot discriminate against them when giving aid. The 44th Amendment protects this when property is acquired by the State.
07 · ARTICLE 32

Right to Constitutional Remedies

A right is only as good as the way to enforce it. Article 32 lets a person go directly to the Supreme Court when a fundamental right is violated. Dr Ambedkar called it the "heart and soul" of the Constitution. It is itself a fundamental right. High Courts have the same power under Article 226, which is wider because it also covers ordinary legal rights.

ART 32
Supreme Court can issue five writs
A writ is a written order from a court. Parliament may give the same power to other courts. Can be suspended only during an Emergency.
HABEAS CORPUS
"You must have the body"
Orders that a detained person be produced before the court. Used against illegal detention.
MANDAMUS
"We command"
Orders a public official or body to do its public duty. Cannot be issued against the President or Governor, or a private person.
PROHIBITION
"To forbid"
A higher court tells a lower court or tribunal to stop a case that is outside its power. Issued while the case is going on.
CERTIORARI
"To be certified"
A higher court cancels an order already passed by a lower court or tribunal that acted without power. Issued after the order.
QUO WARRANTO
"By what authority"
Asks a person holding a public office by what right they hold it. Removes someone who holds a public office illegally.
08 · ARTICLES 31, 33, 34, 35

The last articles of Part III

ART 31
Right to property: deleted
Removed by the 44th Amendment, 1978. Property is now a legal right under Article 300A (Part XII), not a fundamental right. A person whose property is taken now goes to the High Court, not under Art 32.
ART 33
Rights of armed forces and police
Parliament may limit or cancel these rights for the armed forces, police and similar forces so that they can do their duty and keep discipline.
ART 34
Martial law
Limits fundamental rights while martial law is in force in any area. Parliament may protect officials for acts done to restore order.
ART 35
Power to make laws for fundamental rights
Only Parliament (not State legislatures) can make laws on some matters, such as punishing offences against fundamental rights.

Trap pairs for the exam

  • Citizens only: Art 15, 16, 19, 29, 30. All persons: Art 14, 20, 21, 21A, 22, 23, 24, 25–28.
  • Not suspended in an Emergency: Art 20 and 21. Art 19 is suspended only for war or external aggression. (Others by Presidential order.)
  • Right to property: deleted from Art 31 and 19(1)(f) in 1978; now Art 300A.
  • Right to education: Art 21A (6–14, a fundamental right) is not the same as DPSP Art 45 (early childhood care, under 6 years) or Art 51A(k) (parents' duty).
  • Art 17 and 23 work against private persons too; most others only against the State.
  • Prohibition comes during the case; certiorari after the order.
  • Fundamental duties are Art 51A (Part IVA), not in Part III.

Cases and amendments to link

  • Maneka Gandhi (1978): Art 21 needs a fair procedure; Arts 14, 19, 21 work together.
  • Puttaswamy (2017): privacy is a fundamental right under Art 21. Shreya Singhal (2015): Section 66A of the IT Act struck down under Art 19(1)(a).
