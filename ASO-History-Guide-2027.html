Chapter 00 · READ FIRST · 5 minutes

Start here

The official syllabus line for this topic is "(c) History of British Rules in India and Freedom Struggle". So the period is about 1600 to 1950: how the British came, how they ruled, how Indians answered, and how the country became free. Ancient and medieval history are not in the syllabus. This guide covers the whole period in 14 chapters of fixed knowledge, then one chapter on history in the 2025 and 2026 news, then a last-week page.

Modern History is the biggest block of fixed facts in the paper. It had 14 questions in 2018, 10 in 2021 and 16 in 2025, the most ever. Because the facts never change, a student who learns them well can score almost full marks here.

Chapter 02 · PART A · STATIC

How the British took India (1600–1856)

2.1The Europeans come

The Portuguese came first (Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498). They were followed by the Dutch, the English, the Danish and the French.

CompanyYearMain base
Portuguese1498Goa, Daman, Diu
English East India Company1600Surat, then Madras (1639), Bombay (1668), Calcutta (1690)
Dutch1602Pulicat, Chinsura
Danish1616Tranquebar, Serampore
French1664Pondicherry (1674), Chandernagore

The English company won because it had a navy, money from trade and clever commanders. The Mughal Empire was also growing weak after Aurangzeb (1707).

2.2Bengal: Plassey and Buxar

  • Plassey, 1757. Robert Clive beat the Nawab Siraj-ud-Daulah, because Mir Jafar, the Nawab's commander, changed sides. Mir Jafar became Nawab. This gave the Company control of Bengal. The "Black Hole of Calcutta" (1756) was the excuse.
  • Buxar, 1764. Hector Munro beat three rulers together: Mir Qasim of Bengal, Shuja-ud-Daula of Awadh and the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II.
  • Treaty of Allahabad, 1765: Clive gave Awadh back, but took from Shah Alam the Diwani, the right to collect taxes in Bengal, Bihar and Odisha. Bengal then had "dual government" (Company collected tax, the Nawab kept law and order) until 1772.

2.3The French: the Carnatic Wars

Three wars in South India (1746–63). The Battle of Wandiwash (1760) (Eyre Coote beat Lally) ended the French challenge. The Treaty of Paris (1763) left the French only a few trading towns. 2018 Q26

2.4Mysore: Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan

Tipu is remembered for his rocket artillery, a "Tree of Liberty" planted at Seringapatam and a modern navy and trade plan.

Deogaon and Argaon

Argaon was a battle of the second Maratha war. Deogaon was the treaty. Odisha was given up by the Treaty of Deogaon, not Argaon. 2018 Q27

2.6Punjab and Sindh

  • Maharaja Ranjit Singh (died 1839) built the Sikh state with a strong army. By the Treaty of Amritsar (1809) he agreed to keep to the Sutlej.
  • First Anglo-Sikh war (1845–46): Treaty of Lahore (1846). Gulab Singh was made ruler of Jammu and Kashmir by the Treaty of Amritsar (1846) for ₹75 lakh. 2021 Q21
  • Second war (1848–49): Punjab annexed by Dalhousie in 1849. Boy king Duleep Singh was sent to England. The Koh-i-Noor diamond went with him. The Bhangi clan (misl) had ruled Amritsar, Lahore and part of Punjab. 2021 Q25
  • Sindh: taken by Charles Napier in 1843 (the Amirs of Sindh were beaten at Miani).

2.7Other conquests

  • Nepal: Anglo-Nepal war (1814–16). Treaty of Sugauli (1816).
  • Burma: First war (1824–26), Treaty of Yandabo. Second war 1852 (Lower Burma). Third war 1885–86 (all Burma).
  • Afghanistan: First war (1839–42), a disaster for the British. Treaty of Gandamak (1879) after the second war.

2.8Two tricks for taking states

Subsidiary Alliance (Wellesley, 1798). A ruler agreed to: keep a British force in his state and pay for it; keep a British Resident; give up foreign relations; and not employ other Europeans. In return he was "protected". Hyderabad (1798) was the first to accept. Others: Mysore (1799), Tanjore, Awadh (1801), Peshwa (1802), Scindia and Bhonsle (1803–04).

Doctrine of Lapse (Dalhousie, 1848–56). If a ruler died without a natural son, the state "lapsed" to the British. Taken this way: Satara (1848), Jaitpur and Sambalpur (1849), Jhansi (1853), Nagpur (1854), Udaipur. Awadh was taken in 1856 for "bad government" and Wajid Ali Shah was sent to Calcutta. Many rulers lost their thrones, which was a main reason for the 1857 revolt. 2021 Q21, Q22

Rulers and their states

Gulab Singh: Jammu. Duleep Singh: Punjab. Wajid Ali Shah: Awadh. Krishnaraja Wodeyar: Mysore. The Nizam (Asaf Jah): Hyderabad. Govinda Chandra: Cachar. Shah Shuja and Zaman Shah ruled Afghanistan, so pairing them with Rohilkhand or Peshawar is wrong. 2021 Q21, Q22

2.9Law and courts

Under Cornwallis the courts were set up in a ladder. Lowest to highest: Munsif's Court → Sadr Amin's Court → Zilla (district) Court → Sadr Diwani Adalat. 2021 Q24

First Law Member

In 2025 a statement said the first Law Member was Lord Amherst. That is false. The first Law Member (added by the Charter Act of 1833) was Thomas Babington Macaulay. 2025 Q45

First Governor-General of Bengal, of India and Viceroy

First Governor-General of Bengal: Warren Hastings. First Governor-General of India: William Bentinck. First Viceroy (and also Governor-General): Lord Canning. A 2025 option mixed these. 2025 Q45

3.2The Ilbert Bill row

The Bill of 1883, named after the Law Member Courtenay Ilbert, would have let Indian judges try Europeans in the districts. Europeans protested loudly and the Bill was watered down. Indians learned how effective organised protest is, and the Congress was formed two years later. 2018 Q86, 2025 Q10

3.3The services

  • Civil Services. Cornwallis made them well-paid and separate from trade. Wellesley founded the Fort William College (1800). Haileybury (1806) trained the Company's men in England. The Charter Act of 1853 brought an open exam. Satyendranath Tagore was the first Indian in the ICS (1863). The exam was held only in London until 1922. The Lee Commission (1924) recommended 40% Indians.
  • Army. The Company's three armies of Bengal, Madras and Bombay. After 1857 the proportion of Europeans was raised and artillery was kept in British hands. The Peel Commission (1858) reorganised the Army. "Martial races" were preferred.
  • Police. Cornwallis set up the thana system. The Police Act of 1861 made the modern force.
  • Judiciary. Hastings began the courts. The Supreme Court (1774), Sadr Adalats, High Courts Act 1861 (High Courts at Calcutta, Bombay, Madras). Macaulay's Indian Penal Code (1860), Civil Procedure Code (1859), Criminal Procedure Code (1861) and Evidence Act (1872). The first Law Commission (1834) was headed by Macaulay.

Land, trade and money: the economic story

5.1Land revenue systems

The British needed tax from land. They tried three ways.

SystemWho startedWhereWho paid the tax
Permanent Settlement (Zamindari)Cornwallis, 1793Bengal, Bihar, Odisha, VaranasiThe zamindar. Amount fixed for ever. Zamindars could lose the land if they did not pay on the "sunset" day
RyotwariThomas Munro (Madras, 1820); Bombay tooMadras and Bombay 2025 Q88The farmer (ryot) directly. Revised every 20 to 30 years
MahalwariHolt Mackenzie, 1822 (Regulation VII)North-Western Provinces, Punjab, Central IndiaThe whole village (mahal) together

In all three the peasant paid heavily. About half the produce went as tax. Under the Permanent Settlement, zamindars grew rich while tenants were crushed.

5.2Drain of wealth and de-industrialisation

  • Drain of wealth: India sent a large part of its income to Britain without return: salaries, pensions, interest on debt and profits. Dadabhai Naoroji gave this theory in his book Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (1901). R. C. Dutt wrote The Economic History of India (1901). M. G. Ranade and G. Subramania Iyer also wrote on it.
  • De-industrialisation: Cheap British cloth from the factories of Lancashire was allowed into India nearly free, while Indian cloth was taxed in Britain. Weavers and craftsmen lost work and went back to farming. Nationalist writers hold that colonial policy caused it, and that is the standard answer. 2021 Q27
  • Commercialisation of agriculture: farmers were forced to grow cash crops such as indigo, cotton, jute, opium and tea for the British market.

5.4Railways, telegraph, irrigation

  • First railway: Bombay to Thane, 16 April 1853 (Dalhousie). First line in the north: Howrah to Hooghly (1854). Rail laid so that raw cotton could go to ports and British goods to the interior.
  • Telegraph: 1851 (Calcutta to Diamond Harbour) and a wide network by 1854. Post Office Act, 1854.
  • Irrigation: Ganga Canal (1854), Punjab canal colonies.
  • Money: the Reserve Bank of India was set up on 1 April 1935, from the Hilton Young Commission (1926) idea and under the Act of 1934.

5.5Industry and early Indian business

  • Tata Iron and Steel Company at Jamshedpur, 1907 (Jamsetji Tata). First Indian cotton mill: Bombay 1854 (Cowasji Davar). First jute mill at Rishra, near Calcutta, in 1855 (British owned).
  • The Swadeshi movement (1905) gave Indian industries a push: Bengal Chemicals (1893 by P. C. Ray), Tata, Bank of India.
  • Discriminating protection policy came in the 1920s. The Fiscal Commission of 1921–22.
  • The Bombay Plan (1944) by industrialists, a plan for post-war growth.

5.6The plantation and the indigo story

European planters forced peasants in Bengal and Bihar to grow indigo at fixed low prices. The Indigo Revolt of 1859–60 in Bengal, led by Digambar Biswas and Bishnucharan Biswas, was helped by Harish Chandra Mukherjee's Hindoo Patriot and by Dinabandhu Mitra's play Nil Darpan (1860). The Government set up the Indigo Commission. In Bihar the Champaran Satyagraha (1917) was against the tinkathia system of 3 out of 20 parts to be sown with indigo. It was Gandhi's first big movement in India.

7.3The Revolt of 1857

Spark. The new Enfield rifle had cartridges that were said to be greased with cow and pig fat. Both Hindu and Muslim soldiers feared for their religion.

Deeper causes.

  • Political: states taken by the Doctrine of Lapse, Awadh annexed in 1856, pensions ended (Nana Saheb), the Mughal emperor's title to end after Bahadur Shah.
  • Economic: heavy land tax, ruin of crafts, loss of work.
  • Social: fear of conversion, laws against sati and for widow remarriage seen as meddling.
  • Military: Indian soldiers were low paid and badly treated. The General Service Enlistment Act (1856) required service overseas.

Result.

  • The Government of India Act, 1858 ended Company rule. The Crown took over.
  • Queen Victoria's Proclamation was read at Allahabad on 1 November 1858. It promised no more annexation, respect for religion and equal jobs. It is called the "Magna Carta of Indian liberties".

  • R. C. Majumdar said it was "neither first, nor national, nor a war of independence".

8.3The Congress is founded (1885)

  • A. O. Hume, a retired British civil servant, helped to form the Indian National Congress. The first session was held at Bombay (Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College), 28 to 31 December 1885, with W. C. Bonnerjee as president and 72 delegates.

Provinces, war, Quit India and Independence, 1935–1950

10.1Provincial elections and ministries (1937–39)

  • Under the Act of 1935, elections were held in 1937 in 11 provinces. The Congress won in 8 and formed ministries in 7 or 8 (Madras, Bihar, Orissa, UP, CP, Bombay, NWFP, Assam). The League did poorly.
  • In Odisha Biswanath Das formed the first Congress ministry (1937) and Harekrushna Mahtab later (1946).
  • The Muslim League said the Congress rule was "Hindu raj". It marked 22 December 1939 as a "Day of Deliverance" after the Congress ministries resigned (October–November 1939) over India being dragged into the war.

10.2Congress politics, 1938–40

  • Subhas Chandra Bose was Congress president in 1938 (Haripura) and re-elected in 1939 at Tripuri, defeating Gandhi's choice, Pattabhi Sitaramayya. He resigned and founded the Forward Bloc.
  • Lahore Resolution (Pakistan Resolution), 23 March 1940: moved by A. K. Fazlul Huq, in the presidency of Jinnah. Demanded "independent states" for Muslim-majority areas.
  • August Offer (8 August 1940): Linlithgow. Rejected.
  • Individual Satyagraha (October 1940): first satyagrahi Vinoba Bhave, second Nehru. Called also the "Delhi Chalo" movement.

10.3Cripps Mission and Quit India (1942)

  • Cripps Mission (March 1942): offered dominion status after the war, with the right to leave. Rejected. Gandhi called it "a post-dated cheque on a crashing bank".
  • Quit India Movement: the Wardha Congress Working Committee resolution (14 July); the Bombay (Gowalia Tank) session of 8 August 1942 adopted it. Gandhi gave the call "Do or Die" ("Karenge ya Marenge"). All top leaders were arrested on 9 August. Aruna Asaf Ali hoisted the flag at Gowalia Tank. Usha Mehta ran the Congress Radio (secret). Jayaprakash Narayan escaped from Hazaribagh jail. Parallel governments: Ballia (Chittu Pandey), Tamluk (Midnapore, Jatiya Sarkar, Matangini Hazra), Satara (Prati Sarkar, Y. B. Chavan, Nana Patil). In Odisha: Eram (Balasore), Nilagiri, Koraput.
  • Jinnah and the League, the Communists, Hindu Mahasabha and princes kept away or opposed it.
  • The British crushed it with severe force. About 940 were killed (official figure) in firing.

10.4Subhas Bose and the INA

  • Bose escaped from house arrest in Calcutta in January 1941, via Afghanistan, to Germany. He raised the Indian Legion in Germany. In 1943 he travelled by submarine to Japanese-held Asia.
  • The first Indian National Army was formed by Mohan Singh and Rash Behari Bose (Indian Independence League) with prisoners of war in 1942. Bose took command on 4 July 1943 in Singapore.
  • Provisional Government of Free India (Azad Hind Government): 21 October 1943, Singapore 2018 Q33. Capital later at Rangoon. Japan handed the Andaman and Nicobar islands to it.
  • Brigades: Gandhi, Azad, Nehru and the women's Rani Jhansi Regiment (Captain Lakshmi Sahgal). Slogans: "Dilli Chalo", "Jai Hind", "Give me blood and I will give you freedom".
  • The INA reached Imphal and Kohima (1944) but failed. The INA trials at the Red Fort (1945–46): Shah Nawaz Khan, P. K. Sahgal and Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon, defended by Nehru and Bhulabhai Desai. The trials caused huge protest.
  • Bose is believed to have died in an air crash at Taihoku (Taiwan) on 18 August 1945 CHECK disputed.
