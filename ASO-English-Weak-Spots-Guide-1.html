Chapter 01 · WEAK SPOT · Q3, Q19

1. Infinitives: "to" or no "to"

An infinitive is the base form of a verb, usually with the word to in front: to make, to learn, to go. Two questions in Mock 07 were about the small word "to". In Q3 you kept a wrong form, and in Q19 you added a "to" that must not be there.

1.1The negative infinitive: not to + verb

When you want to say "do not" about an infinitive, the word not goes before to. We say "asked him not to make noise", never "asked him to not make noise". The reason is simple. In "to make", the word "to" belongs to the verb like a hat. The word "not" stands in front of the whole thing and cancels it.

✗ WrongThe teacher asked the students to not make noise.
✓ RightThe teacher asked the students not to make noise.
✗ WrongShe told me to not worry.
✓ RightShe told me not to worry.

Your question (Mock 07 Q3)

The sentence was: "Before the examination began, the teacher politely asked all the students (to not make) any noise in the library." You chose No improvement. The right answer was not to make. The trap: "to not make" sounds natural in everyday speech, so it feels fine. The exam follows the textbook rule, which is "not to".

1.2The bare infinitive: no "to" after some verbs

After a few verbs, the next verb comes without "to". This is called the bare infinitive. The main ones are:

  • make someone do: The teacher made the students learn.
  • let someone do: Her father let her go.
  • see, hear, watch, notice someone do: I saw him leave.
  • modal verbs (can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would): He must go.
  • had better, would rather: You had better go. I would rather stay.

The reason is old English habit, so do not look for logic. Just remember the short list: make, let, see, hear + modals.

Your question (Mock 07 Q19)

"To prepare for the annual day programme, the teacher made all the students ____ the long poem by heart." You chose to learn. The right answer was learn. The trap: most verbs ("asked", "told", "wanted", "allowed") do take "to", so "to learn" looks safe. But made is on the bare-infinitive list.

1.3The passive brings the "to" back

When "make" or "see" is turned into the passive, the "to" returns. This is a favourite exam trap.

✓ RightThe teacher made the students learn the poem. (active, no to)
✓ RightThe students were made to learn the poem. (passive, to returns)
✓ RightI saw him enter. / He was seen to enter.

1.4Which verbs take what

enjoy, avoid, finish, deny, mind, suggest-ing: learningHe denied breaking it.

Chapter 02 · WEAK SPOT · Q21, Q24, Q25

2. Parts of speech: the job decides

A part of speech tells us what job a word does in a sentence. The same word can do different jobs in different sentences. So you can never name the part of speech by looking at the word alone. You must look at what it does here. Three of your questions (Q21, Q24, Q25) were about this one idea. The 2021 paper had seven direct questions of this kind 2021 Q17–22, so expect one or two again.

2.1The eight parts in one table

PartIts jobExample
Nounnames a person, place, thing or ideaschool, honesty
Pronounstands in place of a nounhe, everyone, who
Verbshows action or staterun, is
Adjectivedescribes a noun or pronouna difficult question
Adverbdescribes a verb, adjective or another adverbquite sure, runs fast
Prepositionshows the place or relation of a noun to something elseover the wall
Conjunctionjoins words or clausesand, but, until, because
Interjectiona cry of feelingAlas! Wow!

2.2Trap 1: an adverb can describe an adjective (Q21)

Many students think an adverb only describes a verb. It can also describe an adjective, as in "He is quite sure". The word "sure" is an adjective. The word "quite" tells us how sure. So "quite" is an adverb. The same is true for very, too, so, rather, extremely, fairly, almost.

✗ Wrong"Quite" is an adjective, because it comes before the adjective "sure".
✓ Right"Quite" is an adverb, because it tells how much (how sure).

Your question (Mock 07 Q21)

"After checking his notes twice, he is (quite) sure about the answer." You chose Adjective, the answer was Adverb. Test to use: ask "does this word describe a noun?" If it describes an adjective or a verb, it is an adverb.

2.3Trap 2: gerund, participle and infinitive (Q24)

Three verb forms do the work of other parts of speech.

FormLooks likeWorks asExample
Gerundverb + -inga nounSwimming is a good exercise.
Participleverb + -ing or -edan adjectivea drowning man; the broken window
Infinitiveto + verbnoun, adjective or adverbEat to live.

The test: can you replace the word with "it" or "this"? "Swimming is a good exercise" becomes "It is a good exercise". It works, so "swimming" is acting as a noun: a gerund. In "a drowning man", "drowning" describes the man, so it is a participle.

Your question (Mock 07 Q24, skipped)

"(Swimming) is a good exercise." The answer was Gerund. If you skip, you lose nothing, but this question is the same as 2021 Q20 and Q22. Learn the table above and you will answer it in five seconds.

2.4Trap 3: until is a conjunction when a clause follows (Q25)

Some words can be a preposition or a conjunction. The test is what comes after the word.

  • A noun or noun phrase after it: it is a preposition. "I will wait until Monday."
  • A full clause (a subject and a verb) after it: it is a conjunction. "I will wait until you come back."

The same split works for before, after, since, as and for.

✓ RightHe left before noon. (preposition: noon is a noun)
✓ RightHe left before I arrived. (conjunction: "I arrived" is a clause)

Your question (Mock 07 Q25)

"I will wait (until) you come back." You chose Preposition; the answer was Conjunction, because "you come back" is a clause.

Check yourself and likely next

Name the part of speech: "She is (very) kind."

Adverb. It describes the adjective "kind".

Name the part of speech: "A (running) horse is a lovely sight."

Participle. It describes the noun "horse".

Name the part of speech: "(Running) keeps you fit."

Gerund. It is the subject, so it works as a noun.

Name the part of speech: "I stayed home (because) it was raining."

Conjunction. A clause follows.

Likely next: "He worked (hard) all day." (adjective or adverb?)

Adverb here, because it describes the verb "worked". In "a hard task" it would be an adjective. The same word can do both jobs.

Likely next: "The (lovely) girl smiled." Part of speech?

Adjective. A word ending in -ly is not always an adverb: lovely, friendly, costly, lonely are adjectives.

Chapter 03 · WEAK SPOT · Q33, Q35

3. If-sentences: the "if" part never takes will

A conditional sentence has two parts: the if part (the condition) and the result part. Which verb forms we use depends on how real the condition is. Two of your questions were about this (Q33, skipped, and Q35).

3.1The four types

TypeMeaningIf partResult partExample
Zeroalways truesimple presentsimple presentIf you heat ice, it melts.
Firstreal, possible in the futuresimple presentwill + verbIf it rains, we will stay home.
Secondunreal or unlikely nowsimple pastwould + verbIf I knew the answer, I would tell you.
Thirdimpossible, it is pasthad + V3would have + V3If she had known, she would have come.

3.2The two big rules

Rule A: the "if" part never takes will or would in types 1 and 2. We do not say "if it will rain". The word "will" belongs only to the result part. This is why Q33 had its error in the first segment: "If it will rain tomorrow" is wrong; it should be "If it rains tomorrow". The same is true for "when, as soon as, before, after, until" with a future meaning.

✗ WrongIf it will rain tomorrow, we shall cancel the picnic.
✓ RightIf it rains tomorrow, we shall cancel the picnic.
✗ WrongI will call you when I will reach home.
✓ RightI will call you when I reach home.

Rule B: the verb tells you the type. Look at the result part first. If it has "would", the "if" part must be in the simple past (type 2). If it has "would have", the "if" part must be in the past perfect (type 3).

Your question (Mock 07 Q35)

"If I ____ the answer, I would tell you." You chose had known; the answer was knew. The result part says "would tell" (not "would have told"), so this is type 2 and the if part is the simple past: knew. "Had known" would need "would have told". The trap: "had known" feels more correct because it sounds more "past". But the tense of the result decides.

3.3Was or were?

In type 2, use were for every person: "If I were you", "If he were here". This is the form the examiner expects. The 2025 paper asked conditionals in the same family ("Had she known", "If I had been you").

A note on Q33

In Q33 you skipped. Skipping costs nothing, but you can answer it: look at each segment for a will/would in the "if" part. [If it will rain] is the only one. Always scan the if part first.

Check yourself and likely next

Fill in: "If he ____ harder, he would pass." (works / worked / had worked)

worked. Result has "would pass", so type 2: simple past.

Fill in: "If I ____ rich, I would buy a car." (am / was / were)

Fill in: "If she had left early, she ____ the train." (will catch / would catch / would have caught)

would have caught. The if part is past perfect, so type 3.

Fix the error: "If it will be sunny, we will go out."

If it is sunny, we will go out. No will in the if part.

Likely next: "Had she known, she would have been there." This is type 3 with "if" dropped. Which word moves?

"Had" moves to the front. "Had she known" = "If she had known".

Chapter 04 · WEAK SPOT · Q31

4. Since and for

Since and for both talk about time, and both go with the present perfect: "I have lived here since 2020" or "I have lived here for five years". The difference is simple.

WordGoes withExample
sincea point of time (a moment when it began)since 2020, since Monday, since morning, since I was ten
fora length of time (how long)for five years, for two hours, for a week

A good trick: if you can put a calendar or clock word after it, use since. If you can put a number of units, use for.

✗ WrongI have lived here since five years.
✓ RightI have lived here for five years.
✓ RightI have lived here since 2020.
✗ WrongShe is teaching here since 2015.
✓ RightShe has been teaching here since 2015.

4.1Why "since" needs the present perfect

"Since" says the action began in the past and is still going on. So the verb must connect past to present: has / have + V3, or has / have been + -ing. We cannot say "is teaching since" because "is teaching" has no link to the past.

Your question (Mock 07 Q31)

"Which of the following sentences is NOT correct?" You chose the sentence "I have lived here for five years", which is correct. The wrong one was "I have lived here since five years". The question asked for the wrong sentence, and you may have read it as "which is correct". Read the stem twice when it says NOT, INCORRECT or EXCEPT. Circle the word NOT with your pencil.

Check yourself and likely next

Fill in: "He has been absent ____ Monday." (for / since / from)

since. Monday is a point of time.

Fill in: "They have known each other ____ ten years." (for / since / from)

for. Ten years is a length.

Which is wrong: "I have been waiting since two hours."

It is wrong. It should be "for two hours".

Likely next: "It is five years ____ I saw him." (since / for / from)

since. The pattern "It is + length of time + since + past" is common.

Chapter 05 · WEAK SPOT · Q32

5. Uncountable nouns: furniture and its family

A countable noun can be counted one by one: one pen, two pens. An uncountable noun is seen as a mass and cannot be counted with numbers: water, rice, advice. Uncountable nouns have no plural (no -s) and take a singular verb. They never take "a" or "an".

5.1The ones English learners get wrong

Some of these nouns are countable in other languages, including Odia and Hindi, which is why they fool us. In English they are uncountable:

WordRightWrong
furnitureThe furniture is old.furnitures, furniture are
informationThis information is useful.informations
adviceHis advice was good.advices, an advice
luggage / baggageMy luggage has arrived.luggages
newsThe news is good.The news are
knowledge, equipment, evidence, sceneryEquipment is costly.equipments
hair, bread, rice, money, homework, workHer hair is long.hairs (for the whole head)

5.2How to say "one" or "some" of them

We use a measure word: a piece of furniture, a piece of advice, an item of news, a bit of information, two pieces of luggage. We use much (not many), and some or any.

✗ WrongI need an advice and many informations.
✓ RightI need some advice and a lot of information.
✗ WrongShe bought many furnitures.
✓ RightShe bought a lot of furniture.

Your question (Mock 07 Q32)

"Choose the sentence that is free from error." You chose "The furniture in the room are old." The right answer was "The furniture in the room is old." The trap: the word "room" and the idea of many chairs and tables make "are" feel right. Furniture is one mass noun, so the verb is singular.

Check yourself and likely next

Fix it: "The informations are very useful."

The information is very useful.

Fill in: "The news ____ shocking." (was / were)

was. News is singular in form and meaning.

Fix it: "Give me an advice."

Give me some advice. (or: a piece of advice)

Fill in: "How ____ luggage do you have?" (many / much)

much. Luggage is uncountable.

Likely next: "Mathematics ____ my favourite subject." (is / are)

is. Names of subjects ending in -s (mathematics, physics, economics) are singular. Also news, measles, politics.

Chapter 06 · WEAK SPOT · Q39, Q86, Q87, Q88

6. Agreement: four traps with the verb

Subject–verb agreement means the verb must match its subject: a singular subject takes a singular verb (is, was, has), a plural subject takes a plural verb (are, were, have). Four of your wrong answers were here, so this is the most important chapter. The basic rule is easy. The trouble is finding the real subject. In each of your four questions, another word stood right next to the verb and fooled you.

6.1The method: find the subject first

1. Cross out any phrase that starts with of, along with, together with, as well as, with, in addition to. 2. What is left is the real subject. 3. Decide singular or plural for that word alone. 4. Choose the verb to match.

6.2Trap 1: "along with" does not make a plural (Q86)

Words like along with, together with, as well as, with, besides only add extra information. They do not change the subject. The verb agrees with the first noun.

✗ WrongThe captain, along with his players, were welcomed at the airport.
✓ RightThe captain, along with his players, was welcomed at the airport. 2021 Q83–92

Cross out "along with his players" and you see: "The captain ___ welcomed." So was.

Your question (Mock 07 Q86)

You chose are. The right answer was was. This family was asked in both 2021 and 2025 ("as well as"), so it is likely to come again.

6.3Trap 2: a plural noun after "of" is not the subject (Q87)

In "a collection of old coins", the subject is collection. The words "of old coins" only describe it. This is the oldest trap in grammar.

✗ WrongA rare collection of old coins are kept in the museum.
✓ RightA rare collection of old coins is kept in the museum.
✓ RightThe price of vegetables has risen.
✓ RightOne of the books is missing.

Your question (Mock 07 Q87)

You chose are because "coins" is plural and sits right before the verb. The subject is collection, so the verb is is.

6.4Trap 3: "a pair of" is one thing (Q88)

In "a pair of trousers", the subject is pair. So the verb is singular: "A pair of trousers is lying on the bed." Without "a pair of", words like trousers, scissors, spectacles, pants, shorts are always plural: "The trousers are on the bed."

PhraseVerb
A pair of scissorsis on the table
The scissorsare on the table
My spectaclesare broken
A pair of my spectaclesis broken

Your question (Mock 07 Q88)

You chose are. The right answer was is, because the question said "A pair of trousers".

6.5Trap 4: two nouns that make one idea (Q39)

When and joins two words that together name one thing, the verb is singular. Examples: bread and butter, rice and curry, fish and chips, curd and rice. When and joins two separate persons or things, the verb is plural: "The doctor and the nurse are in the room."

✓ RightRice and curry is his favourite meal. (one dish)
✓ RightBread and butter is my usual breakfast. (one meal)
✓ RightRama and Shyama are friends. (two people)

Rice and curry

The key says "is", because rice and curry is one meal. But many speakers say "are", so this question can be argued. Treat it as a low-priority item. The safe habit is: if the two nouns clearly make one dish or idea, use is.

Chapter 07 · WEAK SPOT · Q74

7. Articles: meals have no article

Articles are a, an and the. The best rule list is short. Use a/an for "any one" of something countable, use the for something known or one of a kind, and use no article in a group of places listed below. In Q74 you added "the" where no article is needed.

7.1No article before meals

Say breakfast, lunch, dinner, supper without an article when you mean the meal in a general sense.

✗ WrongWe have the lunch at one o'clock.
✓ RightWe have lunch at one o'clock.
✓ RightWhat did you have for breakfast?

The reason: a meal here is like an activity (as "we play cricket"), not a particular thing. But when you point to one particular meal, an article returns: "The lunch she cooked was delicious." or "It was a heavy lunch."

Your question (Mock 07 Q74)

"In our hostel, the bell rings loudly at noon, and we have ____ lunch at one o'clock in the big dining hall." You chose the; the answer was No article. The trap: the sentence is full of specific words (the bell, the big dining hall), which pulls "the" in. But "have lunch" is the habit of eating the meal.

7.2The other no-article places

Use no article beforeExample
mealshave lunch, after dinner
games and sportsplay cricket, play hockey
languagesspeak Odia, learn English
names of people, cities, statesOdisha, Cuttack, Rama
single peaks and lakesMount Everest, Chilika Lake
general plural or mass nounsI like dogs. Time is money.
go to school, college, hospital, church, prison, bed (main purpose)He goes to school.
abstract nouns in a general senseHonesty is the best policy.

7.3Where "the" is a must

Rivers, seas, oceans, mountain ranges, deserts and island groups: the Mahanadi, the Bay of Bengal, the Himalayas, the Thar. Also superlatives (the best), musical instruments (the violin) and one-of-a-kind things (the sun). Note the pair: "Mount Everest" (no article) but "the Himalayas" (with).

Check yourself and likely next

Fill in: "She plays ____ hockey for the state." (the / a / no article)

No article. Games take no article.

Fill in: "He is the best player in ____ team." (the / a / no article)

the. A specific team; "the best" also needs the.

Fill in: "____ Mahanadi flows through Cuttack." (The / A / No article)

The. Rivers take "the".

Fill in: "____ Chilika Lake is in Odisha." (The / No article)

No article. A single lake takes none.

Likely next: "He goes to ____ hospital every Sunday to visit a friend." (the / no article)

the. He goes as a visitor, not as a patient, so the article returns.

Chapter 08 · WEAK SPOT · Q58, Q59

8. Prepositions: envious of, in the hall, on Monday

A preposition is a small word that links a noun to the rest of the sentence: of, in, on, at, for, to. They have no logic you can derive. They are habits of English, and you learn them in pairs. Q58 and Q59 were about pairs you already half know.

8.1Envious of: adjective + preposition (Q58)

Many adjectives go with one fixed preposition. Learn the pair as one word.

AdjectiveTakesExample
envious, jealous, afraid, proud, fond, capable, aware, shortofShe is envious of her friend's success.
good, bad, quick, clever, weak, surprised, angry (at a thing)atHe is good at maths.
angry (with a person), pleased, satisfied, bored, busywithThe shopkeeper was angry with the boy.
different, free, safefromYour idea is different from mine.
similar, kind, rude, polite, loyal, marriedtoShe is married to a doctor.
eligible, fit, famous, responsible, sorryforHe is famous for his honesty.

Your question (Mock 07 Q58)

"She was secretly envious ____ her friend's success." You chose about; the answer was of. "About" is common after "worried, anxious, excited", so it looked right. Remember: envious of, jealous of, proud of, afraid of.

8.2In, on, at: the three place and time words (Q59, skipped)

The question was: "The meeting was held ____ the school hall ____ Monday. The principal congratulated the winners ____ their success." The right option was in, on, on. Take them one by one:

BlankRuleAnswer

The time rule in one line: at a clock time (at 6 o'clock), on a day or date (on Monday, on 15 August), in a month, year or season (in June, in 1999, in winter).

congratulate on, depend on, rely on, insist on, focus on, agree on (a plan), but agree with (a person) and agree to (a proposal).

Q31read NOT as "correct"Underline NOT. Since = point of time; for = length of time.
Q32"The furniture are old"Furniture, information, advice, luggage, news: singular, no -s.
Q33skipped "If it will rain"The if part never takes will in type 1.
Q35chose "had known" for "would tell"Look at the result part: would = if part in simple past.
Q39"Rice and curry are"Two nouns, one dish = singular. CHECK debatable, some accept are
Q58"envious about"Envious of, jealous of, proud of, afraid of.
Q59skipped in/on/onin a place, on a day, congratulate on.
Q74"have the lunch"No article before meals.
Q86"The captain along with his players are"Along with / as well as do not change the subject.
Q87"A collection of old coins are"The subject is the collection, not the "coins".
Q88"A pair of trousers are"A pair of is singular. Without "pair", trousers are plural.

9.2The four habits that would have saved most of these marks

  • Underline NOT, INCORRECT and EXCEPT in the question.
  • In every agreement question, cross out the "of / along with / as well as" phrase and find the real subject.
  • In every "if" question, read the result part first.
  • When two options both look right, ask which small rule the examiner is testing. It is almost always one of the eight in this guide.
