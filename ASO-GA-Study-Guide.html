1 · Indian Polity 13–16 questions every paper

Polity is the most reliable block in the paper. The questions test the text of the Constitution (which Article, which Schedule, which amendment) and how institutions work (who appoints whom, who can be removed and how). Learn this section like a map. Once you know where each thing sits, you can reason out most questions even when you don't remember them exactly.

1.1 Making of the Constitution

The Constituent Assembly was formed under the Cabinet Mission Plan (1946). It first met on 9 December 1946, with Sachchidananda Sinha as temporary chairman, and then elected Dr Rajendra Prasad as its permanent President. On 13 December 1946 Nehru moved the Objectives Resolution, which later became the Preamble. The Drafting Committee, chaired by Dr B.R. Ambedkar, was set up on 29 August 1947, and B.N. Rau was the constitutional adviser. The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 (now Constitution Day) and came fully into force on 26 January 1950. That date was chosen because on 26 January 1930 the Congress had celebrated "Purna Swaraj Day" 2025 Q54. The whole process took 2 years, 11 months and 18 days.

1.2 The Preamble

The Preamble declares India a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic and promises Justice (social, economic, political), Liberty, Equality and Fraternity. The words "Socialist", "Secular" and "Integrity" were added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) 2025 Q32. That is the only time the Preamble has been amended.

Is the Preamble part of the Constitution? In Berubari (1960) the Supreme Court said no. In Kesavananda Bharati (1973) it reversed itself and said yes, it is part of the Constitution and can be amended, as long as the basic structure isn't damaged. It is not enforceable in a court, but courts use it to interpret the Constitution 2018 Q34.

Famous descriptions of the Preamble (a favourite matching question):

  • Ernest Barker: "the keynote of the Constitution" 2025 Q35
  • K.M. Munshi: "the political horoscope of our sovereign democratic republic"
  • N.A. Palkhivala: "the identity card of the Constitution"
  • Thakurdas Bhargava: "the soul of the Constitution" (Ambedkar used the same phrase for Article 32)

1.4 Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12–35)

The six groups of rights, with their Article ranges, are asked directly 2025 Q82:

Article 19 originally had seven freedoms. The 44th Amendment (1978) removed 19(1)(f), the right to property, and Article 31, and moved property to Article 300A as a legal right 2021 Q39. Freedoms can be limited only by law made by the legislature ("reasonable restrictions", 19(2)–(6)) 2021 Q32.

Article 21 has grown the most through court rulings. Right to privacy: in K.S. Puttaswamy (2017), a nine-judge bench headed by CJI J.S. Khehar held unanimously that privacy is a Fundamental Right under Article 21. It is not absolute and can be restricted by fair law 2018 Q16. Other rights the courts have read into Article 21 include livelihood, a clean environment, speedy trial, shelter, dignity in death and, as the 2025 paper noted, maternity leave as part of reproductive rights 2025 Q6.

Writs (Art 32 for the Supreme Court, Art 226 for High Courts, and High Courts' power is wider):

  • Habeas corpus ("have the body"): produce the detained person in court.
  • Mandamus ("we command"): orders a public authority to perform a public duty it has failed to do 2018 Q38. It can't be issued against the President, Governors or private persons.
  • Prohibition: a higher court stops a lower court or tribunal while a case is still pending.
  • Certiorari: a higher court quashes an order already passed by a lower one.
  • Quo warranto ("by what authority?"): challenges someone's right to hold a public office.

Article 35A (inserted by a 1954 Presidential Order) protected J&K laws on permanent residents and their special rights 2018 Q35. It ended when Article 370 was effectively abrogated on 5 August 2019. J&K and Ladakh became separate Union Territories on 31 October 2019.

1.5 Directive Principles (Part IV, Articles 36–51) and Duties (51A)

The Directive Principles (DPSP) are the government's goals. They are not enforceable in court, but they are "fundamental in governance" (Art 37). The idea came from the Irish Constitution. The ones most often asked:

  • Art 39A: equal justice and free legal aid · Art 40: organise village panchayats · Art 44: Uniform Civil Code · Art 45: early childhood care (rewritten by the 86th Amendment) · Art 48: organise agriculture, prohibit cow slaughter · Art 48A: protect environment, forests and wildlife · Art 50: separate the judiciary from the executive · Art 51: promote international peace and security, the foreign-policy article 2025 Q78.
  • Added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) 2025 Q98: 39(f) healthy development of children, 39A free legal aid, 43A workers' participation in management, 48A environment. The 44th added 38(2) (reduce inequality of income). The 97th added 43B (cooperatives).

Fundamental Duties (Art 51A) were added in 1976 on the Swaran Singh Committee's recommendation, inspired by the USSR. There were 10, and the 86th Amendment (2002) added the 11th: parents must educate children aged 6–14. They aren't enforceable.

1.7 The Union Executive

President (Articles 52–62)

The President is the head of state and must be at least 35 years old. They are elected indirectly by an electoral college (Art 54) made up of the elected MPs of both Houses and the elected MLAs of all states, Delhi and Puducherry. The method (Art 55(3)) is proportional representation by single transferable vote, by secret ballot 2021 Q36. The term is five years. Removal is by impeachment (Art 61) for "violation of the Constitution", needing a two-thirds majority of total membership in each House. The President can pardon (Art 72), issue ordinances when Parliament isn't sitting (Art 123) and declare emergencies.

Under Art 87 the President makes a special address to both Houses at the start of the first session after each general election and at the start of the first session of each year 2018 Q66.

Vice-President (Articles 63–71)

The Vice-President is elected by all members of both Houses, both elected and nominated. State MLAs don't take part 2018 Q36. They are the ex-officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha and must be at least 35. Hook: the President's college excludes nominated members but includes MLAs. The Vice-President's college includes nominated members but excludes MLAs.

Prime Minister and Council of Ministers (Articles 74–75)

  • The President appoints the PM and, on the PM's advice, the other ministers. In practice the PM allocates portfolios.
  • The PM can belong to either House. Indira Gandhi (1966) and Manmohan Singh were in the Rajya Sabha, so "the PM must be a Lok Sabha member" is false 2025 Q18.
  • The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha (Art 75(3)). That's why only the Lok Sabha can pass a no-confidence motion.
  • A non-member can become a minister or the PM but must enter Parliament within six months.
  • The 91st Amendment (2003) capped the Council of Ministers at 15% of the Lok Sabha's total strength (for states, 15% of the Assembly, minimum 12) 2025 Q91. It also strengthened the anti-defection law.

Attorney General (Art 76): the government's chief law officer, appointed by the President, qualified to be a Supreme Court judge. They may speak in Parliament but can't vote. Watch out for decoys like "Article 1976" 2018 Q90.

1.8 Parliament

Lok Sabha: maximum 550 elected members (Art 81). It currently has 543, elected by First Past the Post in single-member constituencies 2025 Q83. Minimum age 25, term 5 years. Rajya Sabha: maximum 250 (238 elected + 12 nominated for art, literature, science and social service). It currently has 245. Members are elected by state MLAs using STV. It is permanent: one-third retire every two years and members serve six years. Minimum age 30.

Who is elected how:

  • FPTP (whoever gets the most votes wins): Lok Sabha and State Assemblies.
  • Proportional representation by STV: President, Vice-President, Rajya Sabha and State Legislative Councils.

The first Lok Sabha Speaker was G.V. Mavalankar 2018 Q79, followed by M.A. Ayyangar. Speaker-related facts that get asked:

  • The Speaker decides whether a bill is a Money Bill.
  • The Speaker presides over joint sittings.
  • The Speaker decides defection cases under the Tenth Schedule.

How bills pass

  • Ordinary bill: either House can start it. If the two Houses deadlock, the President can call a joint sitting (Art 108), presided over by the Speaker. There have been three: the Dowry Prohibition Bill (1961), the Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill (1978) and the POTA Bill (2002). No joint sitting is allowed for Money Bills or Constitution Amendment Bills.
  • Money Bill (Art 110): can only be introduced in the Lok Sabha, with the President's recommendation. The Rajya Sabha must return it within 14 days and can only recommend changes. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject those recommendations 2025 Q19. The President can't send it back for reconsideration.
  • Constitution Amendment Bill (Art 368): needs a special majority in each House separately. Some amendments, such as those affecting federal provisions, also need ratification by half the states.

Budget vocabulary

  • The Constitution calls the budget the "Annual Financial Statement" (Art 112).
  • Appropriation Bill (Art 114): authorises withdrawing money from the Consolidated Fund.
  • Finance Bill: contains the tax proposals.
  • Vote on Account (Art 116): an advance grant to cover spending (usually two months) until the budget is passed 2018 Q37.
  • Supplementary grant: when the sanctioned amount isn't enough.
  • Cut motions: policy cut (reduce to ₹1), economy cut, token cut (₹100).
  • The Consolidated Fund (Art 266) holds all receipts. The Contingency Fund (Art 267) is for emergencies and is held by the President. The Public Account holds money like provident funds.

Special powers of each House

  • Rajya Sabha only 2025 Q59: (1) authorise Parliament to make laws on a State List subject in the national interest (Art 249); (2) authorise the creation of new All India Services (Art 312) 2021 Q45. Both need a resolution passed by two-thirds of members present and voting. The Rajya Sabha can also approve an emergency proclamation alone if the Lok Sabha is dissolved.
  • Lok Sabha only: money bills, no-confidence motions, and the Council of Ministers' accountability.
  • Both together: electing the Vice-President, creating or abolishing Legislative Councils, changing state boundaries (Art 3, by simple law).

1.9 Judiciary

The Supreme Court (Art 124) has the CJI plus up to 33 other judges (34 in all). Judges are appointed by the President through the collegium system, which came from the Judges' cases of 1981, 1993 and 1998. The 99th Amendment (2014) tried to replace the collegium with a National Judicial Appointments Commission. It came into force on 13 April 2015 2021 Q37 and was struck down in October 2015 (Fourth Judges case). Supreme Court judges retire at 65, High Court judges at 62. Removal needs a special majority in both Houses for "proved misbehaviour or incapacity". No Supreme Court judge has ever been removed.

The Supreme Court has several jurisdictions:

  • Original: Centre–state disputes (Art 131).
  • Writ: Art 32.
  • Appellate.
  • Advisory: the President can ask its opinion (Art 143).

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) began in the early 1980s, associated with Justices P.N. Bhagwati and V.R. Krishna Iyer.

1.10 The States

The Governor is appointed by the President (Art 155) and holds office "during the pleasure of the President". The Sarkaria Commission recommended that Governors be eminent persons from outside the state, not active in politics, i.e. non-partisan 2025 Q89. The Governor can reserve state bills for the President's consideration (Art 200–201) and issue ordinances (Art 213). The Advocate General (Art 165) is the state's counterpart of the Attorney General.

Legislative Councils (Vidhan Parishad, Art 169):

  • Six states have one: Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra and Karnataka. Odisha doesn't.
  • Creating or abolishing one: the Assembly passes a special-majority resolution, then Parliament passes a law 2021 Q35.
  • Size: at most one-third of the Assembly, at least 40.
  • Minimum age for an MLC is 30 2025 Q33.
  • One-third retire every two years, and members serve six years.
  • Composition: one-third elected by MLAs, one-third by local bodies, one-twelfth by graduates, one-twelfth by teachers, and one-sixth nominated by the Governor.

Delhi became the National Capital Territory through the 69th Amendment (1991), which inserted Art 239AA and 239AB 2018 Q75.

1.11 Local Government

The Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957) recommended a three-tier Panchayati Raj system: village, block and district. Rajasthan launched it first, at Nagaur on 2 October 1959. Andhra Pradesh followed. Later committees:

  • Ashok Mehta (1977): a two-tier system.
  • G.V.K. Rao (1985).
  • L.M. Singhvi (1986): give panchayats constitutional status.

Note that the B.R. Mehta Committee was about panchayats, not civil services 2025 Q41.

The 73rd Amendment (1992) added Part IX and the 11th Schedule (29 subjects). It came into force on 24 April 1993, now National Panchayati Raj Day. The 74th Amendment (1992) added Part IXA and the 12th Schedule (18 subjects) for municipalities. It came into force on 1 June 1993 2018 Q40. Key features:

  • One-third of seats are reserved for women. Odisha and several other states reserve 50%.
  • Elections every five years.
  • State Election Commissions conduct local body elections, not the Election Commission of India 2025 Q34.
  • State Finance Commissions are set up every five years.

1.13 Statutory bodies (created by ordinary law)

National Human Rights Commission. This topic was asked in all three papers, so learn it fully.

  • Set up: under the Protection of Human Rights Act 1993.
  • Chairperson: a former Chief Justice of India. Since a 2019 amendment, a former Supreme Court judge can also be appointed 2018 Q65. The first chairperson was Justice Ranganath Mishra 2021 Q51.
  • Appointment: the President appoints the chair and members on the recommendation of a six-member committee: the PM (chair), the Lok Sabha Speaker, the Rajya Sabha Deputy Chairman, the Leaders of the Opposition in both Houses, and the Union Home Minister. The Lok Sabha's Deputy Speaker is not on it 2025 Q87.
  • Term: 3 years or age 70.
  • Powers: it can investigate and recommend, but its recommendations are not binding.
  • State Human Rights Commissions: appointed by the Governor.

Other statutory bodies:

  • Central Vigilance Commission: created in 1964 on the Santhanam Committee's recommendation, statutory since 2003.
  • Central Information Commission: under the RTI Act 2005.
  • Lokpal: under the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013. The first Lokpal was Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose (2019).
  • NITI Aayog: not even statutory. It was created by a cabinet resolution on 1 January 2015.

1.15 Emergency provisions (Part XVIII)

  • National Emergency (Art 352): grounds are war, external aggression or armed rebellion. Parliament must approve it within one month. It has been declared three times: 1962, 1971 and 1975 (the last on grounds of "internal disturbance").
  • President's Rule (Art 356): imposed when constitutional machinery fails in a state. Parliament must approve it within two months, and it can last up to three years. S.R. Bommai (1994) limited its misuse.
  • Financial Emergency (Art 360): has never been declared.

1.16 Laws often confused

Some well-known laws were passed by state legislatures, not Parliament 2018 Q77:

  • MCOCA (Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act, 1999) is a state law.
  • Central laws: AFSPA (1958), UAPA (1967), FEMA (1999, replaced FERA), POTA (2002, repealed 2004) and the RTI Act (2005).
  • The Preamble's description of India and the words added in 1976; who called it the "identity card".
  • Which Schedule covers languages (8th), the anti-defection law (10th) or tribal areas of the North-East (6th).
  • Article numbers: 32 (remedies), 21A (education), 44 (Uniform Civil Code), 50 (separation of judiciary), 51 (foreign policy), 72 (pardon), 110 (Money Bill), 112 (budget), 123 (ordinance), 280 (Finance Commission), 324 (Election Commission), 352/356/360 (emergencies), 368 (amendment).
  • The Vice-President's electoral college versus the President's; who presides over a joint sitting; the three joint sittings.
  • Rajya Sabha versus Lok Sabha exclusive powers; Money Bill procedure.
  • Selection committees for the NHRC, Lokpal, CBI Director and CEC: who sits on each.
  • Which committee recommended what (Balwant Rai Mehta, Ashok Mehta, Sarkaria, Punchhi, Swaran Singh).
  • Year and content of amendments: 42, 44, 61, 69, 73, 74, 86, 91, 101, 103 and 106.

2 · Governance, Civil Services & Planning rising: 3 → 8 → 9 questions

The syllabus head reads "Governance, transparency and accountability, e-governance, role of civil services in a democracy". Ordinary GK books cover it poorly, which makes it one of your best chances to score where others drop marks. You need four stories: how the civil services evolved, the reform committees, accountability tools, and the planning system.

2.1 How the civil services evolved

Under the East India Company, officials ("covenanted servants") got their jobs through patronage from the Company's directors. Cornwallis (1786–93) is called the "father of the civil services in India". He separated revenue and judicial work and paid officials well so they wouldn't need bribes. Wellesley founded Fort William College (1800) to train recruits. The Charter Act of 1853 ended patronage and introduced open competition. The Macaulay Committee (1854) designed the exam, first held in London in 1855. Satyendranath Tagore became the first Indian ICS officer in 1863.

Later commissions came in a sequence:

The IAS and IPS were created in 1947, and the Indian Forest Service in 1966. A new All India Service needs a Rajya Sabha resolution (two-thirds) followed by an Act of Parliament (Art 312) 2021 Q45 · 2025 Q59. The All India Services Act 1951 governs them.

2.4 Accountability: who controls the administration?

A bureaucracy has power, so democracies build several controls around it. Questions ask you to classify a tool:

  • Legislative control: question hour, zero hour, debates, motions, financial committees (Public Accounts Committee, Estimates Committee, Committee on Public Undertakings), CAG audit reports and budget voting. Audit belongs here, because the CAG reports to Parliament.
  • Executive control: political direction by ministers, the budget, civil service rules and, above all, appointment and removal of officials 2018 Q49. Also delegated legislation and staff agencies.
  • Judicial control: writs, judicial review and PIL.
  • Citizens' control: elections, public opinion and the media, pressure groups, advisory committees, RTI requests, social audit and citizens' charters. Public opinion is usually taken as the most effective of these, because it works continuously 2021 Q44.

2.5 Transparency tools

Right to Information Act, 2005

The RTI movement began with grassroots campaigns, especially the Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan (MKSS) in Rajasthan in the 1990s, which demanded to see village spending records. Tamil Nadu (1997) was among the first states to pass an RTI law. The central RTI Act was passed in June 2005 and came into force on 12 October 2005. The main provisions:

  • Any citizen can ask a Public Information Officer (PIO) for information.
  • The reply must come within 30 days, or 48 hours if a person's life or liberty is involved.
  • Appeals go first to a senior officer, then to the Central or State Information Commission.
  • Section 4 requires public bodies to publish information proactively.
  • Section 8 lists the exemptions (security, cabinet papers before decisions, privacy and others).

The 2019 amendment let the Centre fix the tenure and salaries of Information Commissioners. The second ARC's first report was "RTI: Master Key to Good Governance" (2006) 2021 Q41.

Citizens' Charter and Sevottam

The Citizens' Charter idea started in the UK in 1991 under John Major. A charter is a public promise of the service standards a department will meet. India adopted charters in 1997. Sevottam ("seva" + "uttam", excellent service) is the Department of Administrative Reforms and Public Grievances' quality management framework for public service delivery. It was recommended by the 2nd ARC and certified under BIS standard IS 15700:2005 2025 Q1. It has three modules: the Citizens' Charter, grievance redress, and service-delivery capability. Grievances are now filed online through CPGRAMS.

Anti-corruption framework

  • Santhanam Committee: appointed in 1962 (Home Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri) under K. Santhanam 2025 Q47. Its 1964 report described the modes of corruption 2021 Q42 and led to the Central Vigilance Commission (February 1964), which became statutory through the CVC Act 2003.
  • Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 (amended in 2018 to make bribe-giving an offence too).
  • Lokpal: the term was coined by L.M. Singhvi (1963), and the 1st ARC recommended a Lokpal and Lokayuktas in 1966. The Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013 came into force in January 2014. It has a chairperson and up to 8 members, half of them judicial. The first Lokpal was Justice P.C. Ghose (2019). Odisha passed its own Lokayukta Act in 2014.
  • Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014; Vohra Committee (1993) on the criminal–politician–bureaucrat nexus.

  • Sarkaria Commission (1983, report 1988): recommended non-partisan Governors from outside the state, an Inter-State Council (set up in 1990) and sparing use of Art 356 2025 Q89.

  • A Vice-Chairperson, a few full-time members and part-time members.
  • Up to four Union Ministers as ex-officio members.
  • A CEO.
  • A Governing Council of all Chief Ministers and Lieutenant Governors.

Its flagship outputs are the SDG India Index (2018), the National Multidimensional Poverty Index, the Aspirational Districts Programme (2018) and the Atal Innovation Mission. It is now responsible for assessing poverty 2021 Q48.

3 · Indian Economy 2–9 questions; a full syllabus head

The syllabus says "Indian Economy and issues relating to planning, growth, development, employment, poverty alleviation, resource mobilisation and inflation". Questions are conceptual and easy once you know the vocabulary: what the RBI does, what makes up the fiscal deficit, which committee did what. Planning is covered in section 2.8.

3.1 Measuring the economy

GDP (Gross Domestic Product) is the value of all final goods and services produced within the country in a year. GNP = GDP + net income from abroad (what Indians earn abroad minus what foreigners earn in India). NDP = GDP − depreciation. "Net" always means depreciation has been subtracted. National income is NNP at factor cost. GVA (Gross Value Added) measures output by sector: GDP = GVA + product taxes − product subsidies.

Nominal values use current prices. Real values use constant prices from a base year, so inflation is removed. That's why real GDP growth, or for living standards real per-capita income, is the right measure of economic growth 2018 Q52. India's GDP is compiled by the National Statistical Office (NSO) under MoSPI, and the base year was revised to 2022-23 in 2026. National Statistics Day is 29 June, the birth anniversary of P.C. Mahalanobis 2018 Q4.

Sector shares. Services contribute more than half of India's GVA (about 55%). Industry, which includes manufacturing, construction and mining, contributes about a quarter, and agriculture under a fifth 2025 Q21. Agriculture still employs the largest share of workers, around 45%. That mismatch (low output share, high employment) is the core problem of Indian development.

3.2 Government finance: budget and deficits

Government receipts are either revenue receipts, which don't create a liability (taxes, fees, dividends), or capital receipts, which create a liability or reduce assets (borrowing, loan recoveries, disinvestment). Spending is either revenue expenditure (salaries, interest, subsidies; creates no asset) or capital expenditure (roads, dams, loans given; creates assets).

Taxes

Direct taxes, such as income tax and corporate tax, are paid by the person they fall on. Indirect taxes, such as GST and customs, are passed on to consumers. The Goods and Services Tax (101st Amendment, 2016; launched 1 July 2017) merged many indirect taxes into one 2025 Q62: central excise, service tax, VAT, entry tax and others. It is a destination-based tax with CGST and SGST (and IGST on inter-state supplies), and it is run by the GST Council (Art 279A). Alcohol for drinking stays outside GST, and petroleum is outside "for now". In 2025 the GST rate structure was simplified into mainly two slabs (5% and 18%), plus a high rate for luxury and sin goods.

3.3 Money and banking

The Reserve Bank of India was set up on 1 April 1935 under the RBI Act 1934, on the Hilton Young Commission's recommendation, and nationalised in 1949. Its headquarters were in Calcutta at first and moved to Mumbai in 1937. Its functions 2025 Q68:

  • Monetary policy: controls inflation and money supply.
  • Currency issue: issues all notes except ₹1, which the Finance Ministry issues.
  • Banker to the government and banker's bank.
  • Lender of last resort.
  • Regulator of banks and NBFCs.
  • Custodian of foreign-exchange reserves.
  • Payment systems regulator.

It does not regulate stock exchanges. That is SEBI's job.

Monetary policy tools

Since 2016 the RBI has followed inflation targeting: 4% CPI inflation, with a band of 2–6%. Rates are set by a six-member Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), chaired by the Governor, with three RBI and three government-nominated members. The tools:

  • Repo rate: the rate at which the RBI lends to banks overnight against government securities. This is the main policy rate. Raising it makes loans costlier and cools inflation.
  • Reverse repo / Standing Deposit Facility (SDF): what the RBI pays banks for parking surplus money. The SDF became the floor of the corridor in 2022.
  • Marginal Standing Facility (MSF): emergency borrowing for banks at a rate above repo (the ceiling).
  • Bank Rate: the long-term lending rate, now aligned with the MSF.
  • CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio): the share of deposits banks must keep with the RBI as cash.
  • SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio): the share of deposits banks must keep as cash, gold or government securities.
  • Open Market Operations: buying or selling government bonds to add or remove liquidity.

The repo rate changes several times a year, and the 2018 paper asked for the current figure 2018 Q76. Note the latest repo rate, CRR and the name of the RBI Governor before the exam.

The Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (2016) created a time-bound process for resolving bad loans. Since 2017, several public sector bank mergers have reduced their number to 12.

3.5 Inflation

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. Demand-pull inflation happens when too much money chases too few goods. Cost-push inflation comes from rising input costs such as fuel and wages. Stagflation is high inflation together with stagnant growth. Deflation means falling prices, and disinflation means inflation is slowing but still positive.

3.6 Employment, poverty and alleviation

Unemployment is measured by the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS), run by the NSO since 2017-18 and monthly since 2025. Types of unemployment: disguised (more people on a farm than needed, so their marginal output is zero), seasonal, structural (skills don't match jobs), frictional (between jobs) and cyclical.

Poverty used to be measured by an income or consumption line (Tendulkar, Rangarajan). India now also uses the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), built on the global UNDP–Oxford (OPHI) method. NITI Aayog computes the national MPI from National Family Health Survey (NFHS) data 2025 Q63. It covers three equally weighted dimensions (health, education and standard of living) through 12 indicators. NITI Aayog reported that about 24.8 crore people escaped multidimensional poverty between 2013-14 and 2022-23.

3.7 External sector and resource mobilisation

The Balance of Payments has a current account (trade in goods and services, remittances, income) and a capital account (foreign investment, loans). India usually runs a trade deficit in goods, a surplus in services (software) and receives large remittances. It is the world's largest recipient. FDI (foreign direct investment) is long-term investment in businesses. FPI (foreign portfolio investment) is investment in shares and bonds. It is called "hot money" because it can leave quickly. FEMA (1999) replaced the stricter FERA (1973).

The government raises resources through taxes, borrowing (government securities, T-bills), disinvestment (selling shares in public companies, managed by DIPAM) and asset monetisation, for example the National Monetisation Pipeline (2021).

4 · Modern History & Freedom Struggle rising: 13 → 10 → 17 questions

The syllabus says "History of British Rule in India and Freedom Struggle", and the 2025 paper took that seriously with 17 questions. Questions come in five families: Governors-General and Viceroys with their measures, Acts, education and press, organisations and newspapers with their founders, and the national movement (Congress sessions, 1857, Gandhian movements). Learning history as a timeline helps, because many questions ask for a sequence or for the odd one out.

4.1 How the Company conquered India

  • Battle of Plassey (1757): Clive defeated Siraj-ud-Daulah of Bengal through Mir Jafar's betrayal. Battle of Buxar (1764): the British defeated the combined forces of Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula of Awadh and Mughal emperor Shah Alam II. The Treaty of Allahabad (1765) gave the Company the Diwani (revenue rights) of Bengal, Bihar and Odisha.
  • Carnatic Wars (1746–63) against the French: the Battle of Wandiwash (1760), where Eyre Coote defeated the French general Lally, ended the French challenge in India 2018 Q26. Pondicherry remained French until 1954.
  • Anglo-Mysore Wars (1767–99): Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan. Tipu died at Seringapatam in 1799 (4th war, under Wellesley).
  • Anglo-Maratha Wars: in the Treaty of Bassein (1802) the weak Peshwa Baji Rao II accepted a subsidiary alliance. Historians call it a treaty "signed with a cypher", meaning a nobody 2018 Q71. In the Second Anglo-Maratha War, Odisha was conquered in 1803 from the Bhonsles of Nagpur and formally ceded by the Treaty of Deogaon (17 December 1803). Argaon was a battle, not the treaty, which made it the trap in 2018 2018 Q27. The Marathas were finally crushed in 1818.
  • Anglo-Sikh Wars (1845–46 and 1848–49): Punjab was annexed in 1849 under Dalhousie. The boy king Duleep Singh was sent to England.
  • Subsidiary Alliance (Wellesley, 1798): Indian rulers paid for British troops stationed in their territory and accepted a British Resident. Hyderabad was the first to accept. Doctrine of Lapse (Dalhousie): a state was annexed if its ruler died without a natural heir. Satara (1848), Jhansi (1853) and Nagpur (1854) fell this way. Awadh was annexed in 1856 on grounds of "misgovernance".

5 · World Physical Geography ~5–10 questions

The syllabus asks only for physical geography: the Earth's interior, rocks, landforms, atmosphere, oceans and biomes. No economic geography (crops, trade) has appeared. Questions are mostly "Which is the largest or deepest?", "Which pair is wrong?" and composition-type matching.

5.1 Inside the Earth, rocks and earthquakes

The Earth has three layers: the crust (thin, rocky; continental crust is mostly granite, oceanic crust is basalt), the mantle (hot, slowly flowing rock; its upper part, the asthenosphere, carries the plates) and the core (iron–nickel; the outer core is liquid and the inner core solid). The boundary between crust and mantle is the Mohorovičić (Moho) discontinuity. The boundary between mantle and core is the Gutenberg discontinuity.

Rocks come in three families:

  • Igneous: formed when magma cools. Granite cools slowly underground and has large crystals 2018 Q42. Basalt cools fast at the surface; the Deccan Traps are basalt.
  • Sedimentary: formed in layers from deposits. Examples are sandstone, shale, limestone and coal. Only these contain fossils.
  • Metamorphic: changed by heat and pressure. Limestone becomes marble, shale becomes slate, sandstone becomes quartzite, and granite becomes gneiss.

Earthquakes are caused by endogenetic (internal) forces, mostly plate movement along faults. The point underground where the rock breaks is the focus (hypocentre). The point on the surface directly above it is the epicentre. A statement calling the epicentre "the point deep under the ground" is false 2018 Q43. Magnitude (energy released) is measured on the Richter scale, now the moment magnitude scale; each step means about 32 times more energy. Intensity (damage) is measured on the Modified Mercalli scale. Seismic waves come in three types:

  • P-waves: primary, fastest, travel through solids and liquids.
  • S-waves: secondary, travel only through solids. Their absence beyond a certain distance proved the outer core is liquid.
  • Surface (L) waves: the most destructive.

5.2 Plates, mountains and volcanoes

Plate boundaries come in three types. At divergent boundaries, plates move apart and new crust forms at mid-ocean ridges: the North American and Eurasian plates at the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the Nazca and Pacific plates at the East Pacific Rise. At convergent boundaries one plate sinks (subduction), forming trenches, volcanoes and fold mountains: Nazca under South America (the Andes), and the Indian plate into the Eurasian plate (the Himalayas). At transform boundaries plates slide past each other, as at the San Andreas Fault 2021 Q61.

Mountain types 2021 Q64:

  • Young fold: high and jagged. Himalayas, Alps, Andes, Rockies.
  • Old fold: worn down. Appalachians, Urals, Aravallis.
  • Block (horst): Black Forest, Vosges, Sierra Nevada. The Satpura is often given as an Indian example.
  • Volcanic: Kilimanjaro, Fuji, Mauna Loa.
  • Residual / relict: Scottish Highlands, Nilgiris, Aravallis.

Volcanoes: about 75% of active volcanoes lie on the Pacific Ring of Fire. Indonesia has the most active volcanoes of any country. Mount Lewotobi Laki-laki, which erupted in 2024–25, is on Flores island in Indonesia 2025 Q43. There are three main types: shield volcanoes (gentle runny lava, as in Hawaii, which sits over a hotspot), composite / strato volcanoes (steep and explosive, like Fuji and Vesuvius) and calderas (collapsed craters). India's only active volcano is Barren Island in the Andamans. Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.

Layers from the ground up:

  • Troposphere: all weather happens here. It is about 8 km thick at the poles and 18 km at the equator, and temperature falls with height.
  • Stratosphere: contains the ozone layer, and temperature rises with height. Calm, so jets cruise near its base.
  • Mesosphere: the coldest layer; meteors burn up here.
  • Thermosphere / ionosphere: reflects radio waves; auroras form here.
  • Exosphere.

Winds:

  • Air flows from high to low pressure and is deflected by the Coriolis force: right in the northern hemisphere, left in the southern.
  • The planetary winds are the trade winds (towards the equator), the westerlies (between 30° and 60°; in the south they're the "Roaring Forties", "Furious Fifties" and "Shrieking Sixties") and the polar easterlies.
  • The doldrums are the calm belt at the equator. The horse latitudes are the calm belts at about 30°.
  • Jet streams are fast winds high in the atmosphere. The subtropical westerly jet and the tropical easterly jet control India's monsoon.

Local winds 2021 Q67:

  • Hot and dry: Chinook (Rockies, the "snow eater"), Foehn (Alps), Sirocco (Sahara to Italy), Khamsin (Egypt and Arabia), Harmattan (West Africa, "the doctor"), Loo (North India), Samoon (Iran), Santa Ana (California), Zonda (Argentina), Brickfielder (Australia).
  • Cold: Mistral (France), Bora (Adriatic), Blizzard, Purga (Siberia), Pampero (Argentina).

Cyclones: tropical cyclones form over warm seas above 26–27°C, between about 5° and 30° latitude. They don't form at the equator, where the Coriolis force is too weak. Regional names 2021 Q68:

  • Hurricane: Atlantic and Caribbean (USA).
  • Typhoon: West Pacific (China, Japan).
  • Baguio: Philippines.
  • Willy-willy: Australia.
  • Cyclone: Indian Ocean.

The eye is calm, and the eyewall has the strongest winds. Cyclones in the Indian Ocean are named in turn from a list contributed by 13 countries (WMO/ESCAP panel), and IMD New Delhi is the regional centre.

El Niño and La Niña are changes in the Pacific. In El Niño, the central and eastern Pacific near Peru becomes unusually warm, and India's monsoon tends to weaken. La Niña is the opposite. The Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), called the "Indian Niño", is a similar see-saw of sea temperatures between the western and eastern Indian Ocean 2025 Q39. A positive IOD (warmer west) helps India's monsoon.

5.4 Oceans and seas

  • Pacific: the largest and deepest ocean. Its deepest point, and the world's, is the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench (about 10,935 m), near Guam 2025 Q70. Other trenches: Tonga (Horizon Deep, second deepest), Kuril, Philippine. The Atlantic's deepest is the Puerto Rico Trench, and the Indian Ocean's is the Java (Sunda) Trench.
  • Atlantic: S-shaped, the busiest for shipping, with the Mid-Atlantic Ridge running down its middle.
  • Indian Ocean: the only ocean named after a country. The Ninety East Ridge is a long underwater volcanic ridge running along 90°E in the Indian Ocean 2018 Q80.
  • Arctic: the smallest and shallowest ocean, with the widest continental shelf (Siberian) 2021 Q70.
  • The Sargasso Sea (North Atlantic) is the only sea with no coastline. It is bounded by currents, not land 2018 Q83.
  • Saltiest water bodies: Lake Van (Turkey) and the Dead Sea. Among open seas, the Red Sea is the saltiest. The Baltic Sea is among the least salty.
  • Ocean currents: warm currents run along east coasts and cold currents along west coasts in the tropics. The West Wind Drift circles Antarctica 2021 Q62. Where warm and cold currents meet, plankton thrive and great fishing grounds form, such as the Grand Banks off Newfoundland (Gulf Stream and Labrador Current).
  • Marine life zones: plankton drift in the sunlit upper layer, nekton swim actively (fish, whales), and benthos live on the bottom. The upper part of an aquatic ecosystem holds both plankton and nekton 2018 Q13.

5.5 Rivers and lakes

  • Nile: the longest river (about 6,650 km). It flows north to the Mediterranean. The White Nile starts at Lake Victoria, and the Blue Nile at Lake Tana (Ethiopia).
  • Amazon: the largest river by volume of water and basin size. It has no delta.
  • Congo (Zaire): the only major river that crosses the equator twice 2025 Q80. It is also the world's deepest river.
  • Mississippi: forms a bird's-foot delta 2021 Q66. The Nile and Ganga form arcuate (fan-shaped) deltas.
  • Lakes: the Caspian Sea is the largest lake of any kind. Superior is the largest freshwater lake by area. Baikal (Siberia) is the deepest and oldest, with the most fresh water by volume. Lake Victoria is the largest tropical lake 2018 Q84. Titicaca is the highest navigable lake.

5.6 Latitudes, longitudes and seasons

The Earth's axis is tilted 23.5°, which is why we have seasons. On the summer solstice (21 June) the sun is overhead at the Tropic of Cancer, giving the longest day in the northern hemisphere. On the winter solstice (22 December) it is overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn, giving the longest day in the southern hemisphere, for example in Australia 2018 Q81. On the equinoxes (21 March and 23 September) day and night are equal everywhere. The Earth is closest to the sun (perihelion) around 3 January and farthest (aphelion) around 4 July.

Each 15° of longitude equals one hour of time. The International Date Line roughly follows 180° and bends around island groups. Greenwich (0°) is the prime meridian.

6.1 Location, size and time

India lies between about 8°4′N and 37°6′N and between 68°7′E and 97°25′E. Its area is about 3.287 million km², the 7th largest country. The Tropic of Cancer (23.5°N) cuts the country roughly in half and passes through 8 states: Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Tripura and Mizoram. It does not pass through Odisha 2018 Q44.

India's east and west ends are about 29° of longitude apart, which is roughly two hours of sun time. So the country uses one Standard Meridian, 82°30′E, passing near Mirzapur (UP). It runs through UP, MP, Chhattisgarh, Odisha and Andhra Pradesh. It sets Indian Standard Time = GMT + 5:30 2018 Q68.

Coastline: traditionally about 7,516 km including the islands. A 2024 re-measurement using a new method put it at about 11,098 km. Gujarat has the longest coastline 2018 Q46, followed by Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu (or Tamil Nadu second in the new figures). There are 9 coastal states 2018 Q72: Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha and West Bengal. 6.3 Physiographic divisions

The Himalayas are young fold mountains with three parallel ranges:

  • Himadri (Greater Himalaya): Kanchenjunga, India's highest peak at 8,586 m, is here.
  • Himachal (Lesser Himalaya): hill stations such as Shimla and Mussoorie.
  • Shiwaliks (outer): the youngest; flat valleys between them are called "duns", like Dehradun.

In the east they turn south as the Purvanchal: Patkai, Naga, Mizo and Garo–Khasi–Jaintia hills.

The Northern Plains are built by the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra rivers. Their belts:

  • Bhabar: pebbles at the foot of the hills, where streams disappear underground.
  • Terai: marshy land where the streams re-emerge.
  • Bhangar: old alluvium.
  • Khadar: new, fertile alluvium.

The Peninsular Plateau is the oldest part of India. Its main features:

  • The Aravallis are the oldest fold mountains, highest at Guru Shikhar on Mount Abu. India's oldest rocks are in the peninsula, and the Aravallis are the usual answer for "oldest" 2018 Q47 · 2021 Q71.
  • The Vindhyas divide north India from the Deccan plateau 2018 Q73, and the Satpuras run parallel south of them.
  • The Western Ghats (Sahyadri) are continuous. Their highest peak is Anamudi (2,695 m) in the Anamalai hills, the highest point in south India.
  • The Eastern Ghats are broken up by rivers. Their highest peaks are Arma Konda in Andhra Pradesh and Deomali/Mahendragiri in Odisha.
  • The two Ghats meet in the Nilgiris (Doddabetta).
  • South of the Palghat gap lie the Anamalai, Palani and Cardamom hills. The Cardamom hills are the closest to Kanyakumari 2025 Q12.

The Thar Desert lies in western Rajasthan, with the Luni as its only significant river. Coastal plains: on the west, Konkan (Maharashtra–Goa) and Malabar (Kerala, with backwater lagoons called kayals). On the east, the Northern Circars (Odisha–Andhra) and Coromandel (Tamil Nadu). The east coast is wider because of the river deltas. Islands: the Andaman & Nicobar islands are mountainous and volcanic (Barren Island). Lakshadweep is coral.

6.4 Geology

Rock systems from oldest to youngest:

  1. Archaean (gneiss and granite).
  2. Dharwar: the oldest metamorphosed sedimentary system, rich in iron ore, manganese and gold. It is the oldest of the geosynclines asked about 2021 Q71.
  3. Cuddapah.
  4. Vindhyan: sandstone and limestone, used in Mughal buildings.
  5. Gondwana: about 98% of India's coal, in the Damodar, Mahanadi and Godavari valleys.
  6. Deccan Traps: basalt lava flows from the late Cretaceous, about 66 million years ago, which weathered into black soil.
  7. Tertiary rocks: the Himalayas and oil in Assam.

6.5 Rivers

Himalayan rivers are perennial, fed by snow and rain.

  • Ganga: formed at Devprayag, where the Bhagirathi (from Gangotri/Gaumukh) meets the Alaknanda. Right-bank tributaries: Yamuna and Son. Left-bank tributaries: Ramganga, Gomti, Ghaghara, Gandak, Kosi (the "Sorrow of Bihar"). The Yamuna joins at Prayagraj.
  • Brahmaputra: rises near Mansarovar as the Tsangpo, enters India as the Siang/Dihang, and has Majuli, the largest river island, in Assam.
  • Indus: tributaries Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas and Sutlej. India suspended the Indus Waters Treaty (1960) in April 2025.

Peninsular rivers are fed by rain.

  • East-flowing rivers form deltas: the Godavari (longest peninsular river, called "Dakshin Ganga"), Krishna, Kaveri (the longest river of Tamil Nadu) and Mahanadi.
  • West-flowing rivers: the Narmada and Tapi flow through rift valleys and form estuaries, not deltas. Others are the Sabarmati, Mahi and Luni.
  • Amarkantak (Maikal Range) is the source of the Narmada and Son. The Mahanadi rises at Sihawa (Chhattisgarh). The Tapi rises at Multai (Betul, Satpura), not in the Maikal 2021 Q72.

6.6 Climate and the monsoon

India has a tropical monsoon climate. In summer, the land heats faster than the sea, so the low-pressure zone (the ITCZ) moves north over the Gangetic plain. Moist south-west winds then blow in from the ocean. The heated Tibetan plateau creates the tropical easterly jet high overhead, and the subtropical westerly jet moves north of the Himalayas. When the westerly jet leaves north India late, the monsoon is delayed and erratic 2021 Q79. El Niño weakens the monsoon, and a positive IOD strengthens it.

The monsoon arrives in Kerala around 1 June and covers India by mid-July. Seasonal features:

  • Western disturbances: winter rain in the north-west, good for wheat.
  • Mango showers: pre-monsoon rain in Kerala and Karnataka.
  • Kal Baisakhi / Nor'westers: violent thunderstorms in Bengal and Odisha in April–May.
  • Loo: hot, dry winds in the north.
  • Retreating monsoon: October–November rain on the Tamil Nadu coast, which gets most of its rain in winter.

The wettest places are Mawsynram and Cherrapunji (Meghalaya). Western Rajasthan is the driest.

The India State of Forest Report (ISFR) is published every two years by the Forest Survey of India, Dehradun. ISFR 2023 (released December 2024) found:

6.9 Biogeographic zones

India has 10 biogeographic zones: Trans-Himalaya, Himalaya, Desert, Semi-arid, Western Ghats, Deccan Plateau (the largest), Gangetic Plain, North-East, Coasts and Islands 2025 Q93.

  • The Trans-Himalaya (Ladakh, Lahaul–Spiti) has sparse vegetation but the world's richest community of wild sheep and goats.
  • The Himalaya has the highest ranges and rich species diversity.
  • The Semi-arid zone lies next to the desert.
  • The Western Ghats have dense forests full of endemic species.

7.1 The state at a glance

  • Formation: separate province on 1 April 1936 (Utkal Divas / Odisha Day). "Orissa" was renamed "Odisha" in 2011.
  • Capital: Bhubaneswar since 1948 (Cuttack was the capital before). It was planned by the German architect Otto Königsberger. The Orissa High Court is at Cuttack (1948).
  • Size: about 1,55,707 km² (about 4.7% of India, 8th largest state). 30 districts in three revenue divisions: Northern (Sambalpur), Central (Cuttack) and Southern (Berhampur). Largest district by area is Mayurbhanj, then Sundargarh. Smallest is Jagatsinghpur. Most populous is Ganjam.
  • Legislature: unicameral (no Legislative Council), with 147 Assembly seats, 21 Lok Sabha seats and 10 Rajya Sabha seats.
  • Government: BJP, with Mohan Charan Majhi as Chief Minister since 12 June 2024 (the state's first BJP CM). He replaced Naveen Patnaik (BJD), who had served 24 years.
  • Language: Odia became the 6th classical language in 2014. It is in the 8th Schedule.
  • State symbols: animal Sambar; bird Indian Roller (Blue Jay); flower Ashoka; tree Ashwattha (peepal); song "Bande Utkala Janani" by Kantakabi Laxmikanta Mohapatra.
  • Coastline: about 480 km (longer under the 2024 re-measurement). The Tropic of Cancer does not pass through Odisha 2018 Q44.

7.2 Physiographic divisions

  1. Northern Plateau (Mayurbhanj, Keonjhar, Sundargarh): an extension of the Chotanagpur plateau and the mineral heartland. It includes Simlipal (highest point Meghasani, about 1,165 m) and the Gandhamardan of Keonjhar.

7.4 Climate and disasters

Odisha has a tropical monsoon climate, and its Bay of Bengal coast is among the most cyclone-prone in the world. The 1999 Super Cyclone (Paradip, about 10,000 deaths) led to the Odisha State Disaster Management Authority (OSDMA), India's first such state body, and the ODRAF rapid-action force. Odisha's "zero casualty" approach is internationally praised. It worked through evacuations during Phailin (2013), Hudhud (2014), Titli (2018), Fani (2019, Puri), Amphan (2020), Yaas (2021) and Dana (October 2024, Bhitarkanika–Dhamra). Western Odisha (Kalahandi, Balangir, Nuapada) is drought-prone, and coastal districts flood.

7.5 Soils, forests and wildlife

Soils:

  • Red and yellow soils cover the largest area, on the plateaus.
  • Laterite is found on high flat surfaces leached by heavy rain: Khurda, Puri uplands and the Eastern Ghats.
  • Alluvial deltaic soils make up the coast and deltas.
  • Black soil occurs in patches in the south-west (Malkangiri, Kalahandi).
  • Saline soils occur along the coast.
  • Brown forest soils are found in the hills.

Forests cover about a third of Odisha. They are mostly tropical deciduous, with sal as the main tree. Kendu leaf (used to wrap beedis) is a nationalised forest product and a major income source for tribal families. 7.7 History of Odisha

  • Kalinga War (261 BCE): Ashoka's conquest; its bloodshed turned him to Buddhism. Rock edicts survive at Dhauli and Jaugada. These separate Kalinga edicts replace edicts XI–XIII.
  • Kharavela (1st century BCE, Mahameghavahana/Chedi dynasty): a Jain king whose achievements are recorded in the Hathigumpha inscription at Udayagiri. The Udayagiri–Khandagiri caves near Bhubaneswar are Jain.
  • Bhaumakaras and Somavamshis: Mukteswar and Lingaraj temples in Bhubaneswar, the "Temple City".
  • Eastern Gangas: Anantavarman Chodaganga Deva began the Jagannath Temple, Puri (12th century). Narasimha Deva I built the Konark Sun Temple (about 1250), shaped like a chariot and called the "Black Pagoda". It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984.
  • Suryavamsi Gajapatis: Kapilendra Deva (1435), Prataparudra Deva (in his time Sri Chaitanya came to Puri).
  • Afghan, Mughal and then Maratha rule (1751–1803). The British conquered Odisha in 1803 (Wellesley's time). The Bhonsles ceded it by the Treaty of Deogaon 2018 Q27.
  • Paika Rebellion (1817): led by Bakshi Jagabandhu of Khurda against British revenue policy and loss of Paika lands. It is often called the first armed uprising against the British. Its bicentenary was marked in 2017.
  • 1857: Veer Surendra Sai of Sambalpur fought the British for years. Chakra Bisoi led the Kandha resistance. The Na'anka Durbhikhya (great famine, 1866) killed about a million people.
  • Utkal Sammilani (1903): founded by Madhusudan Das ("Utkal Gourab", first Odia graduate and lawyer) to unite the Odia-speaking areas. The result was the province of 1936.
  • Freedom movement:
    • Gopabandhu Das ("Utkalmani"): Satyavadi school (Sakhigopal, 1909), newspaper The Samaja (1919).
    • Gandhi's first visit: Cuttack, 23 March 1921 2018 Q31.
    • Salt Satyagraha at Inchudi (Balasore, 1930), led by Gopabandhu Choudhury and Acharya Harihar Das.
    • Prajamandal movements in the princely states, including Dhenkanal, where the 12-year-old boatman Baji Rout was shot in October 1938, India's youngest martyr. Also Nilgiri and Talcher.
    • Laxman Naik (Koraput, Quit India, hanged in 1943).
    • Women leaders Rama Devi, Malati Choudhury and Sarala Devi.
  • After 1947: 24–26 Garjat princely states merged into Odisha in 1948 through Sardar Patel. Mayurbhanj joined on 1 January 1949. Harekrushna Mahtab was the first Chief Minister after independence.

8.2 Energy flow and food chains

  • Producers (plants) → primary consumers (herbivores) → secondary and tertiary consumers (carnivores) → decomposers (bacteria and fungi, which recycle nutrients).
  • Lindeman's 10% law: about 10% of energy passes from one level to the next. That's why food chains are short.
  • Pyramids: the energy pyramid is always upright. The number pyramid is inverted for a tree full of insects. The biomass pyramid is inverted in the sea (a small mass of phytoplankton supports a larger mass of zooplankton).
  • Assimilation efficiency (food absorbed ÷ food eaten) is low in herbivores, because cellulose is hard to digest. It is high in carnivores (often 80% or more), and some predators such as spiders and insect predators exceed 90% 2021 Q100.
  • Biomagnification: toxins such as DDT and mercury become more concentrated at each step up the food chain. Eutrophication: nutrient runoff causes algal blooms, which use up oxygen in the water and kill fish.

8.3 Biodiversity: hotspots, IUCN, protected areas

Hotspots were conceived by Norman Myers (1988) 2021 Q94. A region qualifies if it has at least 1,500 endemic vascular plants and has lost at least 70% of its original habitat. There are 36 in the world. India has four: Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats–Sri Lanka and Sundaland (Nicobar).

IUCN Red List categories (IUCN headquarters: Gland, Switzerland):

  • Extinct.
  • Extinct in the Wild.
  • Threatened, which covers three categories: Critically Endangered, Endangered and Vulnerable.
  • Near Threatened.
  • Least Concern.
  • Data Deficient.

The olive ridley turtle is Vulnerable.

The first mammal declared extinct because of human-caused climate change is the Bramble Cay melomys, a small rodent from a low island in the Great Barrier Reef. Rising seas and storm surges destroyed its habitat. It was declared extinct by Queensland in 2016 and by Australia in 2019 2025 Q100.

Protected areas in India (under the Wildlife Protection Act 1972):

  • National parks: stricter, no human rights allowed inside.
  • Wildlife sanctuaries: some activities allowed.
  • Conservation reserves and community reserves: added in 2002.
  • Tiger reserves (Project Tiger, 1973; NTCA 2006). There are now more than 55, and the latest have been added in Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Rajasthan.
  • Biosphere reserves (UNESCO's Man and the Biosphere programme). India has 18, and 12–13 are in UNESCO's network, including the Nilgiri (first, 2000), Sundarbans, Gulf of Mannar and Simlipal.

Animals and their parks:

8.4 Pollution and monitoring

  • Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB): set up in 1974 under the Water Act. Laws: Water Act 1974, Air Act 1981, Environment (Protection) Act 1986 (an umbrella law passed after the Bhopal gas tragedy of 1984).
  • National Air Quality Monitoring Programme (NAMP): SO₂, NO₂, PM10 and PM2.5 are measured twice a week with 24-hour samples (about 104 readings a year) 2021 Q96.
  • Comprehensive Environmental Pollution Index (CEPI): developed in 2009 by CPCB with IIT Delhi to rate industrial clusters (Angul–Talcher and Ib Valley in Odisha are among the critically polluted ones) 2025 Q26.
  • AQI (2014): eight pollutants (PM10, PM2.5, NO₂, SO₂, CO, O₃, NH₃, Pb) and six categories from Good to Severe. National Clean Air Programme (2019): cut particulate pollution in 130 cities.
  • Greenhouse gas trap: N₂O (nitrous oxide) is a greenhouse gas, but NO₂ (nitrogen dioxide) is a pollutant and is not counted as a GHG 2021 Q93. Likewise, carbon monoxide is not a Kyoto gas 2018 Q63.

Orbits explained:

  • Geostationary (about 36,000 km above the equator): the satellite circles once in 24 hours and so appears fixed over one spot. Used for communication and weather (INSAT, GSAT).
  • Sun-synchronous polar (about 500–800 km): passes over each place at the same local time every day. Ideal for imaging and remote sensing (Cartosat, RISAT, EOS).
  • Low Earth Orbit (under 2,000 km): the ISS and Starlink.
  • Medium Earth Orbit: navigation systems such as GPS. India's NavIC uses geostationary and geosynchronous orbits.

Launch vehicles: PSLV (workhorse for polar orbits), GSLV Mk II, LVM3 (heavy, formerly GSLV Mk III; used for Chandrayaan-3 and OneWeb), and SSLV (small).

Its products include Falcon 9 rockets, Starlink and Starship. India's space regulator for private firms is IN-SPACe (2020). Indian start-ups include Skyroot (Vikram-S, the first private Indian rocket, 2022) and Agnikul.

9.5 Propulsion and computing

Colonial names have also been changed. Kitchener House became Manekshaw House, Fort William in Kolkata became Vijay Durg, and St George's Gate became Shivaji Gate. The 2025 question asked which pairs were wrong 2025 Q14.

11 · National & International Institutions and Organisations 3–9 questions

The syllabus asks for each body's "structure, mandate, etc." So for every organisation, know four things: when it was founded, where its headquarters are, who the members are, and what it does. Indian institutions such as the RBI, NHRC, NITI Aayog and CPCB are covered in earlier sections.

11.1 The United Nations system

The UN was founded on 24 October 1945 (UN Day) with 51 original members, India among them. It now has 193 members. Its six principal organs:

  • General Assembly: all members, one vote each.
  • Security Council: 15 members. The 5 permanent members with a veto are the USA, UK, France, Russia and China, so Japan is not one 2018 Q99. The other 10 are non-permanent members elected for 2-year terms, five each year 2021 Q57–58. India has served eight times, most recently in 2021–22.
  • Economic and Social Council.
  • Trusteeship Council: inactive since 1994.
  • International Court of Justice: The Hague. It has 15 judges with 9-year terms. Dr Nagendra Singh was the only Indian to serve as its President (1985–88) 2018 Q98. Justice Dalveer Bhandari has been a judge there since 2012.
  • Secretariat: headed by the Secretary-General (António Guterres since 2017). A new Secretary-General takes office in January 2027. Check who was chosen.

Specialised agencies are independent organisations linked to the UN by agreement: FAO (Rome), ILO (Geneva), IMF and World Bank (Washington), UNESCO (Paris), WHO (Geneva), ITU (Geneva), UPU (Bern), WMO (Geneva), WIPO (Geneva), ICAO (Montreal), IMO (London), IFAD (Rome), UNIDO (Vienna) and UN Tourism (Madrid). The International Committee of the Red Cross (Geneva) is NOT a UN agency. It is a private Swiss humanitarian body 2018 Q97.

Funds and programmes include UNICEF (New York), UNDP (New York), UNEP (Nairobi), UNHCR (Geneva), WFP (Rome) and UNFPA. Related organisations include the IAEA (Vienna) and WTO (Geneva).

Goodwill Ambassadors are celebrities appointed by UN agencies such as UNICEF, UNDP and UNHCR to raise awareness 2025 Q74.

UNESCO programmes are often confused, so learn what each preserves:

  • World Heritage List: monuments and natural sites. India has 44; the latest additions are Moidams of Assam (2024) and the Maratha Military Landscapes (2025).
  • Intangible Cultural Heritage: living traditions such as Chhau, Durga Puja and Garba.
  • Memory of the World Register: documentary heritage, meaning manuscripts, archives and historical documents 2025 Q86. The Bhagavad Gita and Natyashastra manuscripts were added in 2025.
  • Biosphere reserves (Man and the Biosphere programme), Creative Cities, Global Geoparks.

12.2 The collection system

  1. Use one source only. Pick one monthly current-affairs compilation (any reputable English one) plus one Odisha news source. Several compilations only multiply your revision load.
  2. Write by category, not by date. Keep a notebook (or a file) with one page per category from the table above. When you read "Exercise X between India and Y", it goes on the Defence page, not under "March". At revision time, each page becomes a ready list.
  3. Write it as an exam question. For each item record: what it is, who is involved, where, the number or edition, and one surprising detail. The 2025 paper's multi-statement questions tested exactly these details, such as which fleet took part in TROPEX, Pralay's range, or WMO's headquarters.
  4. Link news to static topics. If Odisha gets a new Ramsar site, also revise the existing list. If the RBI changes the repo rate, revise how the MPC works. Examiners write news questions with static options.
  5. Revise weekly, then monthly. Spend 15 minutes each Sunday on that week's notes and an hour at the end of each month on the month's pages. In the last 30 days, go over the whole year twice.
