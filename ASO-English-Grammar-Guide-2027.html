Chapter 00 · READ FIRST · 5 minutes

Start here

English is Paper III (A): 100 questions, 100 marks, sharing two hours with the Odia paper. Each right answer gives 1 mark, and each wrong answer takes away 0.25. Our goal is simple. English should be the paper where you lose almost nothing. Unlike General Studies, English has no new facts to chase. The same rules come back every year, so a student who knows them well can score 85 or more.

This guide is built on the 120 rules of grammar in the order Nimisha Bansal teaches them. Her course opens with each and every, then both, then who, whom, which and that, then conditional sentences, then "one of the" and numbers with units. This guide opens the same way. After that, the rules are grouped by the official syllabus, and every rule is checked against the three past papers. The wording, the examples and the numbering after the opening rules are this guide's own, so a rule number here may not match her book.

0.1What the syllabus asks

The official line for English is: Grammar (Verb, Preposition, Adverb, Subject Verb Agreement, Error Correction / Recognition, Tenses, Fill in the Blanks with Articles etc., Vocabulary, Synonyms, Antonyms), Comprehension, and Sentence Rearrangement. Nothing else. Narration, question tags, analogies and parts of speech were asked in 2018 and 2021, but they are not in the syllabus, and the 2025 paper dropped them all.

Chapter 01 · GRAMMAR · Rules 1–10

Each, every, both and other small words

Determiners are the small words that stand before a noun and tell us how many or which one: each, every, both, either, few, little, much, many, some, any. They look easy, which is why examiners love them. One wrong determiner makes the whole sentence wrong, and the error is easy to miss.

RULE 1Each and every

Each looks at the members one by one. It can be used for two people or for more. Every looks at the whole group as a set, and it needs at least three. So for two hands, two eyes or two sides, use each, never every. Both words take a singular noun and a singular verb, because we are thinking of one member at a time. Each can also stand alone as a pronoun ("Each has a book"), but every cannot. With "of", use "each of the + plural noun", still with a singular verb. Every cannot take "of" directly, so write "every one of".

✗ WrongEvery hand of the boy was dirty.
✓ RightEach hand of the boy was dirty.
✗ WrongEach of the buildings have a unique design.
✓ RightEach of the buildings has a unique design. 2025 Q55

RULE 2Both

Both means two together, so it takes a plural verb. Its partner is always and: "both Ram and Shyam". Do not write "both … as well as" or "both … also". Both is also wrong with "not". For "not this one and not that one", English uses neither … nor. When a possessive word is used, both comes first: "both my brothers", not "my both brothers".

✗ WrongBoth Ravi as well as his sister were present.
✓ RightBoth Ravi and his sister were present.
✗ WrongBoth of them did not come.
✓ RightNeither of them came.

RULE 3Either and neither

Either means one of two. Neither means not this one and not that one. Both are only for two. For three or more, use any (one) or none. On their own, or as "either of / neither of", they take a singular verb. Neither already carries the "not", so never add another "not" after it.

✗ WrongNeither of the two candidates are experienced.
✓ RightNeither of the candidates is very experienced. 2025 Q39 2018 Q52
✗ WrongEither of these four roads leads to the station.
✓ RightAny one of these four roads leads to the station.

RULE 4Few, a few, the few

These three words go with countable nouns (books, friends, people). Few is negative: "Few people came" means almost nobody came. A few is positive: "A few people came" means some did. The few means "all of the small number": "The few books he had were old" means he had some books and all of them were old. Few and a few take a plural verb. 2021 Q90

✗ WrongHe has few friends, so he is never lonely.
✓ RightHe has a few friends, so he is never lonely.
✓ RightFew applicants have backed out. 2021 Q90

RULE 5Little, a little, the little

Little (almost none), a little (some) and the little (all of the small amount) work exactly like few, but for things we cannot count: water, time, money, hope, knowledge. They take a singular verb. Words that sit before little to show a very small amount are so, too, very and "precious": so little, too little, precious little. 2018 Q92

✗ WrongThere is a little hope, so we must give up.
✓ RightThere is little hope, so we must give up.
✓ RightThe little money he had was stolen.

RULE 6Much and many

Many goes with things we count: many books, many times. Much goes with things we cannot count: much water, much time, much information. The same split works for "a large number of" (countable) and "a great deal of" or "a large amount of" (uncountable). 2018 Q38

✗ WrongThere is not many water in the tank.
✓ RightThere is not much water in the tank.
✓ RightThere was a great deal of concern about the power cuts. 2018 Q38

RULE 7Less and fewer

Fewer is the comparative of few, so it goes with countable nouns. Less is the comparative of little, so it goes with uncountable nouns. Write "fewer mistakes" and "less sugar". With a comparative, remember that "than" must follow. 2018 Q51

✗ WrongLess students passed this year.
✓ RightFewer students passed this year.
✓ RightIt takes fewer cars than boats to ferry them. 2018 Q51

RULE 8Some and any

Some is used in positive statements: "I have some money." Any is used in negative sentences and questions: "I don't have any money." "Do you have any money?" Some is also fine in a question that is really an offer or request: "Would you like some tea?" After hardly, scarcely and without, use any, because those words are negative.

✗ WrongI did not buy some apples.
✓ RightI did not buy any apples.
✓ RightHe left without any help.

RULE 9Many a

Many a means "many", but its grammar is singular. It is followed by a singular noun and a singular verb: "many a man has", "many a student was". Compare "a great many", which is plural: "a great many students were".

✗ WrongMany a man have suffered during the partition.
✓ RightMany a man has suffered during the partition. 2021 Q85

RULE 10Enough, such and such a

Such a goes before a singular countable noun: such a good man. Plain such goes before plurals and uncountables: such good men, such bad weather. In a "such … that" sentence, the noun must be present: "It was such a hot day that we stayed in." Enough comes after an adjective (old enough) but before a noun (enough money).

✗ WrongHe is enough old to vote.
✓ RightHe is old enough to vote.
✓ RightIt was such a good film that we saw it twice.

Chapter 02 · GRAMMAR · Rules 11–20

Who, whom, which, that and other pronouns

A pronoun stands in place of a noun. A relative pronoun (who, whom, which, that, whose) joins two ideas: "I met the man who helps the poor." The examiner tests three things here: the case (who or whom), the right word for people or things, and whether the pronoun matches its noun.

RULE 11Who and whom

Use who when the person does the action, and whom when the action is done to the person or when the word follows a preposition. A quick test: answer the question in your head. If the answer is "he", use who. If the answer is "him", use whom. "____ wishes to see you?" → He wishes → who. "____ do you wish to see?" → see him → whom. 2018 Q4

✗ WrongThe man who I met yesterday is a doctor.
✓ RightThe man whom I met yesterday is a doctor.
✓ RightTo whom did you give the letter?

RULE 12Which, that and whose

Who and whom are for people (and pets with names, in some books). Which is for things and animals. That can be used for people, animals and things. Whose shows belonging and works for both people and things: "a house whose roof leaks". Extra information about a known person sits between commas, and then you must use who or which, never that. 2021 Q27

✗ WrongThe book who you lent me is lost.
✓ RightThe book which (or that) you lent me is lost.
✓ RightMy aunt, who lives in Mumbai, is a doctor. 2021 Q27

RULE 13That after special words

After a superlative (the best, the tallest), after all, only, same, any, none, nothing, everything, much, little, and after "It is … that", English uses that. The rule exists because these words point to one exact thing.

✗ WrongThis is the best film which I have seen.
✓ RightThis is the best film that I have seen.
✓ RightAll that glitters is not gold.

RULE 14Whoever, whatever and what

When there is no noun for the pronoun to point back to, use whoever (any person who), whatever (anything that) or what (the thing which). "Which" and "that" need a noun before them. Never write "what" after a noun.

✗ WrongThe thing what he said was true.
✓ RightWhat he said was true.
✓ RightWhoever comes first will get the prize.

RULE 15Pronoun after a preposition or than

After a preposition (between, with, for, to, except, like), the pronoun must be the object form: me, him, her, us, them. After than or as, finish the sentence in your head: "He is taller than I (am)." "She likes you more than (she likes) me."

✗ WrongThis is a secret between you and I.
✓ RightThis is a secret between you and me.
✓ RightHe is taller than I.

RULE 16Order of pronouns

When pronouns of different persons stand together, good manners decide the order. In a normal or good sentence, use second, third, first: "You, he and I will go." When you admit a mistake, take the blame first: "I, you and he are to blame."

✗ WrongI and you will attend the meeting.
✓ RightYou and I will attend the meeting.
✓ RightI and my brother broke the window. (owning a fault)

RULE 17One and one's

If the subject is one meaning "any person", keep using one, one's and oneself. Do not switch to his or her. 2025 Q67 uses "one gets", so one stays one throughout that sentence.

✗ WrongOne should keep his promises.
✓ RightOne should keep one's promises.

RULE 18Each other and one another

Traditional grammar books and exam keys say: each other for two people, one another for three or more. Modern English mixes them, but the exam follows the book rule.

✓ RightThe two brothers love each other.
✓ RightThe members of the team help one another.

RULE 19Pronoun agrees with its noun

A pronoun must match the noun it stands for. Singular words like everyone, each, everybody, nobody take his or her (or his, in older keys). A collective noun acting as one unit takes its, not their. If the sentence already treats the noun as singular ("the committee has"), every later word must be singular too. 2025 Q69

✗ WrongThe committee, which has reached a decision, are preparing their report.
✓ RightThe committee, which has reached a decision, is preparing its report. 2025 Q69
✓ RightEveryone must bring his or her own pen.

RULE 20Reflexive pronouns

A few verbs need a reflexive pronoun after them: avail oneself of, absent oneself from, enjoy oneself, pride oneself on, apply oneself to. Some verbs never take one: keep, hide, qualify, bathe, rest. "He hid" is right; "He hid himself" is extra.

✗ WrongHe availed of the offer.
✓ RightHe availed himself of the offer.
✗ WrongThey enjoyed at the party.
✓ RightThey enjoyed themselves at the party.

Likely next

Fill in: "The officer ____ you complained against has been transferred." (a) who (b) whom

(b) whom. You complained against him.

Fill in: "He is the only man ____ can solve it." (a) who (b) that

(b) that. After "only", use that.

Spot the error: "Let you and I finish the work."

"I" should be "me". "Let" is followed by an object.

Fill in: "One must do ____ duty." (a) his (b) one's

(b) one's.

Fill in: "The jury gave ____ verdict." (a) its (b) their

(a) its. The jury acts as one body.

Chapter 03 · GRAMMAR · Rules 21–27

If-sentences and wishes

A conditional sentence has two parts: the if part (the condition) and the result part. Nimisha Bansal calls the tense pairs here "golden rules", because once you know the pair, the blank fills itself. In 2025 they gave four marks: 2025 Q1 2025 Q13 2025 Q25 2025 Q38, plus an error-spotting question 2025 Q66.

If partResult partMeaning
present (If it rains)will + verb (I will stay)a real, possible future
past (If I had money / If I were)would + verb (I would buy)unreal now, imaginary
past perfect (If I had known)would have + V3 (I would have come)unreal past, too late now
present (If you heat ice)present (it melts)always true

RULE 21First conditional

For a real chance in the future, the if part is in the simple present, and the result has will. Do not put will in the if part. The same is true after when, before, after, until, as soon as and unless when they talk about the future (Rule 75).

✗ WrongIf it will rain tomorrow, I will bring my umbrella.
✓ RightIf it rains tomorrow, I will bring my umbrella. 2025 Q25

RULE 22Second conditional and "if I were"

For something unreal or imaginary now, use the past in the if part and would + verb in the result. With "be", formal English uses were for all persons: if I were, if he were, if she were. "If I were you" is a fixed phrase for advice.

✗ WrongIf I was a bird, I would fly.
✓ RightIf I were a bird, I would fly.
✓ RightIf I were you, I would have seized that opportunity. 2025 Q38

RULE 23Third conditional

For a past that did not happen, put had + past participle in the if part and would have + past participle in the result. A common error is "will have" or "would" alone in the result.

✗ WrongIf I had been rich in my youth, I will have bought a car.
✓ RightIf I had been rich in my youth, I would have bought a car. 2025 Q66

RULE 24Conditional without if

The word if can be dropped when the helping verb moves to the front. "Had she known about the meeting, she would have been there." "Were I rich, I would help." "Should you need help, call me." The result part keeps the same pair as before.

✗ WrongHad she known about the meeting, she would be there on time.
✓ RightHad she known about the meeting, she would have been there on time. 2025 Q13

RULE 25Wish

Wish always talks about something unreal, so the verb moves one step back. For a wish about now, use the past: "I wish I knew the answer." "He wishes he lived with his parents." 2018 Q54 For a regret about the past, use the past perfect: "I wish I had brought an umbrella." 2018 Q74 The 2025 paper offered only "had" for a past regret, which was the closest choice. 2025 Q1 Never use will or can after wish.

✗ WrongI wish I will have more time.
✓ RightI wish I had had more time for this project.

RULE 26Unless and lest

Unless means "if not", so a second "not" makes a double negative. Lest means "so that … not" or "for fear that", and it is followed by should or by the base verb, never by "not".

✗ WrongUnless you do not work hard, you will fail.
✓ RightUnless you work hard, you will fail.
✗ WrongWalk carefully lest you may not fall.
✓ RightWalk carefully lest you should fall.

RULE 27It is time, would rather and had better

"It is time" (or "It is high time") for an action that is late takes the past: "It is time we left." Would rather and had better are followed by the base verb, with no "to": "I'd rather stay home." 2018 Q36 "You had better leave." When would rather has a new subject, the verb goes into the past: "I'd rather you went now."

✗ WrongIt is high time we start working.
✓ RightIt is high time we started working.
✗ WrongYou had better to see a doctor.
✓ RightYou had better see a doctor.

Likely next

Fill in: "If he ____ harder, he would have passed." (a) worked (b) had worked

(b) had worked. Third conditional.

Fill in: "Were I in your place, I ____ apologise." (a) will (b) would

(b) would. "Were I" = "If I were".

Spot the error: "If you will heat ice, it melts."

"will heat" should be "heat". A general truth uses the present in both parts.

Fill in: "She wishes she ____ the job last year." (a) took (b) had taken

(b) had taken. Regret about the past.

Fill in: "Should it rain, the match ____ cancelled." (a) will be (b) would have been

Chapter 04 · GRAMMAR · Rules 28–49

Subject–verb agreement

The basic law is short: a singular subject takes a singular verb, and a plural subject takes a plural verb. "The boy plays." "The boys play." The hard part is finding the real subject. The examiner hides it behind long phrases, joins two subjects with tricky words, or uses nouns that look plural but are not. This chapter gave 11 marks in 2021 and 7 in 2025, plus most of the error-spotting questions. Learn it well.

The one-line method

Cover everything between the subject and the verb with your finger. "The collection [of rare manuscripts] is housed in the archive." Then ask: is the word left before the verb one thing or many? 2025 Q53

RULE 28One of the

"One of the" picks one from a group. The noun after it must be plural (one of the boys), but the verb agrees with "one", so it is singular. This was Nimisha Bansal's fifth rule.

✗ WrongOne of the boy is absent.
✗ WrongOne of the boys are absent.
✓ RightOne of the boys is absent.

RULE 29One of those who

In "He is one of those men who work hard", the word "who" points to "men", not to "one", so the verb after who is plural. When "the only one" is used, who points to "one", so the verb is singular: "He is the only one of the boys who works hard."

✗ WrongShe is one of the best singers who has ever lived.
✓ RightShe is one of the best singers who have ever lived.
✓ RightHe is the only one of the players who has scored a century.

RULE 30Number + unit before a noun

When a number and a unit stand before a noun like an adjective, joined by a hyphen, the unit has no -s: a ten-rupee note, a five-year plan, a two-hour film, a six-foot man. This is Nimisha Bansal's sixth rule.

✗ WrongHe gave me a hundred-rupees note.
✓ RightHe gave me a hundred-rupee note.
✗ WrongIt is a three-days course.
✓ RightIt is a three-day course.

RULE 31Amount as one sum

"Ten kilometres is a long walk." "Five hundred rupees is too much for a shirt." "Two hours is enough." The plural noun is treated as one total amount, so the verb is singular.

✗ WrongFifty kilometres are too far to walk.
✓ RightFifty kilometres is too far to walk.

RULE 32Dozen, hundred, thousand

When an exact number comes before dozen, score, hundred, thousand, lakh or million, the word stays singular: two dozen, three hundred, five lakh. When there is no exact number and "of" follows, add -s: dozens of, hundreds of, thousands of.

✗ WrongHe bought three dozens bananas.
✓ RightHe bought three dozen bananas.
✓ RightThousands of people came.

RULE 33Each, every, everyone, someone, nobody

Words ending in -one, -body and -thing (everyone, someone, nobody, anything, everything) and the words each and every are all singular. Two subjects joined by "and" become singular too when each or every stands before them: "Every boy and girl was given a book." 2021 Q24

✗ WrongEveryone feel a great sense of elation when one gets a dream job.
✓ RightEveryone feels a great sense of elation when one gets a dream job. 2025 Q67
✓ RightEveryone at the party was amazed. 2021 Q24

RULE 34Either of, neither of, none of

"Either of", "neither of" and "each of" are followed by a plural noun but take a singular verb. 2025 Q39 None can take either verb in modern English, but exam keys prefer the singular when none means "not one". 2018 Q91 With an uncountable noun, none is always singular: "None of the milk was spilt."

✗ WrongNeither of the candidates are very experienced.
✓ RightNeither of the candidates is very experienced. 2025 Q39
✓ RightNone of us wants to change the schedule. 2018 Q91

RULE 35Either … or, neither … nor, not only … but also

When two subjects are joined by either … or, neither … nor, or, nor, not only … but also, the verb agrees with the subject closest to it. To sound natural, put the plural subject last: "Neither the manager nor his assistants were aware."

✗ WrongNeither the manager nor his assistant were aware of the new rules.
✓ RightNeither the manager nor his assistant was aware of the new rules. 2025 Q65
✓ RightEither you or he is to blame.

RULE 36As well as, along with, together with

Words such as as well as, along with, together with, with, besides, in addition to, like, and not, rather than add extra information but do not join the subjects. The verb agrees with the first subject only. 2025 Q9 2021 Q86

✗ WrongThe team, as well as their coach, are optimistic.
✓ RightThe team, as well as their coach, is optimistic. 2025 Q9
✓ RightThe chief along with his family is involved. 2021 Q86

RULE 37Two subjects joined by and

Two subjects joined by and take a plural verb: "The dog and the cat are fed." 2021 Q83 But when both words name one person, use one article and a singular verb: "The secretary and treasurer is here" (one person holds both posts). "The secretary and the treasurer are here" (two people). Pairs that form one idea are singular too: bread and butter, rice and curry, slow and steady.

✗ WrongBread and butter are my breakfast.
✓ RightBread and butter is my breakfast.
✓ RightThe collector and the magistrate were present. (two people)

RULE 38A number of and the number of

"A number of students were absent" means many students. "The number of students is rising" means the count. The same split holds for "a variety of" (plural) and "the variety of" (singular).

✗ WrongA number of factors has influenced the result.
✓ RightA number of factors have influenced the outcome. 2025 Q20
✓ RightThe number of road accidents is increasing.

RULE 39Collective nouns

A collective noun names a group: team, committee, jury, family, class, crowd, group, board, army, collection. When the group acts as one body, use a singular verb. When its members act as separate people (quarrel, disagree, take their seats), use a plural verb. Keys argue about feelings ("The board of directors are anxious"), so look for a clue such as "their" or "among themselves". 2021 Q89 CHECK keys split; the 2021 answer is "are" in this guide

✓ RightThe collection of rare manuscripts is housed in the archive. 2025 Q53
✓ RightThe jury were divided in their opinions.
✓ RightA group of students is planning to go to the beach. 2025 Q35 CHECK some keys accept "are"

RULE 40Nouns that look plural

Some nouns end in -s but name one thing: news, subjects (mathematics, physics, economics, civics, politics), diseases (measles, mumps, rickets), games (billiards, draughts), and some names (the United States, the Arabian Nights). They take a singular verb.

✗ WrongThe news are good.
✓ RightThe news is good.
✓ RightMeasles is a common disease among children. 2021 Q88

RULE 41Pair nouns and "a pair of"

Things made of two equal parts are always plural: scissors, trousers, jeans, spectacles, tongs, pliers, shorts. "The scissors are sharp." But with a pair of, the subject is "pair", so the verb is singular.

✗ WrongThe pair of shoes are on sale.
✓ RightThe pair of shoes is on sale. 2025 Q62
✓ RightMy trousers are torn.

RULE 42Some, all, most, half, part + of

Words of portion (some of, all of, most of, half of, part of, a lot of, plenty of) take their number from the noun after "of". "Some of the rice is left" (rice is uncountable). "Some of the apples are rotten."

✗ WrongSome of the rice are still left.
✓ RightSome of the rice is still left. 2021 Q91
✓ RightHalf of the students were absent.

RULE 43Gerund, infinitive or clause as subject

When the subject is an action (an -ing word or a to-verb) or a whole clause, treat it as one thing: "Public speaking is a skill." 2021 Q92 "To err is human." "What he says is true." "That Ajita was not invited was a shame." 2021 Q7

✗ WrongReading novels are her hobby.
✓ RightReading novels is her hobby.

RULE 44Long subjects with of

When the subject is "X of Y", the verb agrees with X. "The quality of the mangoes was poor." "The rules of the game have changed." The examiner places a noun of the opposite number right before the verb to trap you. 2025 Q68

✗ WrongImmigration rules has become so strict that …
✓ RightImmigration rules have become so strict that … 2025 Q68
✗ WrongThe cost of all these books are high.
✓ RightThe cost of all these books is high.

RULE 45There is and there are

Nouns: number and form

A noun is a naming word. The exam tests nouns through agreement and articles: you cannot choose "a" or "is" until you know if the noun is countable, uncountable, always plural or always singular. These six rules settle that.

RULE 50Uncountable nouns

These nouns name a mass, not separate items. They have no plural, take no "a", and take a singular verb. To count them, use a unit: a piece of advice, an item of news, an article of furniture. 2018 Q42

✗ WrongHe gave me many advices.
✓ RightHe gave me a lot of advice.
✗ WrongThe sceneries of Kashmir are beautiful.
✓ RightThe scenery of Kashmir is beautiful.

RULE 51Nouns with the same form in both

Some nouns do not change: one sheep, ten sheep; one series, two series; a species, many species. The verb shows the number: "A deer was seen." "Three deer were seen."

✗ WrongI saw many sheeps in the field.
✓ RightI saw many sheep in the field.

RULE 52Plurals of compound nouns

In a compound noun, the main word takes the plural sign: mother-in-law → mothers-in-law, passer-by → passers-by, commander-in-chief → commanders-in-chief. When there is no main noun, add -s at the end: forget-me-nots, grown-ups.

✗ WrongHis two brother-in-laws came.
✓ RightHis two brothers-in-law came.

RULE 53Possessive forms

Living things take 's: the boy's book. A plural ending in -s takes only an apostrophe: the boys' hostel, a students' canteen. 2018 Q77 Plurals without -s take 's: children's books, men's shoes. Non-living things usually take of: the leg of the table, not the table's leg. Fixed time and distance phrases are exceptions: a day's work, a stone's throw.

✗ WrongThe roof's of the house leak.
✓ RightThe roof of the house leaks.
✓ RightA canteen for students = a students' canteen. 2018 Q77

RULE 54Double possessive and "of mine"

English cannot put "a" and "my" together. Use the pattern a + noun + of + mine/yours/his/hers/ours/theirs: "a friend of mine". With a name: "a friend of Ravi's".

✓ RightHe is a friend of mine.

Articles: a, an, the

English has three articles. A and an mean "any one" and go with singular countable nouns. The means "the one we both know". Sometimes no article is right at all. The 2021 paper put ten article blanks in a row 2021 Q63 to 2021 Q72, and 2025 asked three 2025 Q24 2025 Q31 2025 Q57. Each one is a free mark if you know the eight situations below.

RULE 56A or an: go by sound, not spelling

Use an when the next word starts with a vowel sound, and a when it starts with a consonant sound. Spelling can trick you. A silent h gives a vowel sound: an hour, an honest man, an honour, an heir. A "yoo" sound is a consonant sound: a university, a unique idea, a European, a useful book. "One" starts with a "w" sound: a one-way street. Letters said aloud follow their sound: an MLA (em), an SP, but a BA.

✓ RightLos Angeles has an ideal climate. 2021 Q64

Use a/an when you mention a thing for the first time, or when any one of its kind will do. ✓ RightMount Everest is the highest peak.

RULE 61School, hospital, church and prison

✗ WrongMy brother goes to the school every day to study.
✓ RightMy brother goes to school every day.
✓ RightAlice is not at home. She is at work. 2021 Q25

RULE 62The + adjective and the + class

The + adjective means a whole group of people (the poor, the blind, the young), with a plural verb (Rule 47). The + singular noun can stand for a whole class: "The cow is a useful animal." Musical instruments take the: "She plays the flute." Games do not: "She plays chess."

✗ WrongHe plays violin very well.
✓ RightHe plays the violin very well.

RULE 63Article position with such, what, quite, half

With such, what, quite, rather, half and many, the article comes after the word: such a man, what a day, quite a long way, half an hour, many a time. With so, too, as, how, the adjective comes before the article: so good a man, too big a risk.

✓ RightHe is such a fool.

Chapter 10 · GRAMMAR · Rules 101–110

Inversion and joining words

In a normal sentence the subject comes before the verb: "She had never seen such a sight." When a negative or limiting word moves to the front for force, English swaps the order, and the helping verb jumps before the subject: "Never had she seen such a sight." This is called inversion. In 2025 it gave seven marks 2025 Q8 2025 Q11 2025 Q21 2025 Q40 2025 Q70, and the pairs of joining words (no sooner … than, not only … but also) were tested alongside it.

The inversion pattern

Negative word + helping verb (do, does, did, had, have, is, can, will) + subject + main verb. If the plain sentence has no helping verb, add do, does or did: "He rarely smiles" → "Rarely does he smile."

RULE 101No sooner … than

"No sooner" is a comparative, so its partner is than, never "when" or "then". The verb after it is inverted, usually with had + V3 in the past: "No sooner had he reached the station than the train left." In the present, use do or does.

✓ RightNo sooner did the child touch the tree than it broke out into blossoms. 2025 Q8
✗ WrongNo sooner he had left than it began to rain.
✓ RightNo sooner had he left than it began to rain.

RULE 102Hardly and scarcely … when

Hardly, scarcely and barely pair with when (or before), not than. They also cause inversion: "Hardly had I sat down when the bell rang." On its own at the start, hardly still inverts: "Hardly does one encounter such a feat." 2025 Q11

Error spotting: the method

An error-spotting question gives one sentence in four parts. One part breaks a rule. The questions gave 7 marks in 2018, 12 in 2021 and 6 in 2025, but they really test every rule in Chapters 1 to 11 at once. The way to win is a fixed order of checks, so you never read the sentence aimlessly.

13.1The six checks, in order

  • 1. Find the subject. Strip away "as well as", "along with", "of the …" and anything between commas. Is it one thing or many? (Rules 28–49)
  • 2. Check its verb. Does the verb match the subject you found? This alone solved four of the six 2025 questions: were → was 2025 Q65, feel → feels 2025 Q67, has → have 2025 Q68, are → is 2025 Q69.

  • 5. Check articles and determiners. a/an by sound; the with superlatives; each/every; few/a few.

Sentence rearrangement

This part grew faster than any other: 1 question in 2018, 5 in 2021, 20 in 2025. There are two kinds. In the first, four parts of one sentence (P, Q, R, S) must be put in order 2025 Q71 to 2025 Q84. In the second, four sentences must be put in order to make a paragraph 2025 Q85 to 2025 Q90. Both are pure grammar and logic, so with practice they become the fastest marks in the paper.

14.1Parts of one sentence (P, Q, R, S)

  • Find the subject first. It is a noun or pronoun with no word before it that needs one: "she has made", "the teacher bought", "not everyone". 2025 Q77
  • Join the subject to its verb. The verb part usually starts with has, was, will, or a past form: "The team (P) was ecstatic (S)". 2025 Q74
  • Put the object right after the verb. "painted a vivid description of the scene". 2025 Q81
  • Time and place phrases go last: "over the years", "yesterday", "at the conference". 2025 Q71 2025 Q82 2025 Q84 They can also open the sentence: "Last semester I took a course …" 2025 Q76
  • A part ending in "and", "of" or "the" must be followed by a part that completes it. "the complexities of" + "modern technology". 2025 Q77
  • Use the options. Fix the first two parts, then look for the one option that starts that way. You often need not order all four.

When no option fits

Once in 2025 the correct order (SPQR) was not among the options 2025 Q72 CHECK. If this happens, choose the option that agrees with your first two parts, or skip the question. A wrong answer costs 0.25.

14.2Sentences of a paragraph

  • Find the opening sentence. It introduces the topic and makes sense alone. It never starts with However, This, These, Such, It, Also, So, Consequently. "Recently, the company announced a new policy." 2025 Q85 "Blockchain technology operates on …" 2025 Q90
  • Follow the pointer words. "This influx", "This lack of evidence", "This decentralization" point back to the idea just before them. 2025 Q88 2025 Q89 2025 Q90
  • Follow the turn words (Rule 108). "However" brings a contrast, usually late in the paragraph; "So", "Consequently" and "Therefore" bring a result, usually last. 2025 Q86 2025 Q87
  • Follow the story order: cause before effect, problem before solution, pilot before expansion, allegation before dismissal.
  • Look for pairs. Find two sentences that must stand together (a "this" and its idea). Most options can then be cut quickly.
