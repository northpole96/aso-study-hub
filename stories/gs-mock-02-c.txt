# odisha | Odisha | Its rivers, hills, minerals, heroes and forests
## odi-rivers | Six rivers, one coast | Odisha's coast was not always there. Six rivers built it, grain by grain. | 63, 64
Look at a map of Odisha's coast, from Balasore down to Ganjam. Thousands of years ago much of that green strip was sea. Six rivers carried mud down from the hills and laid it at their mouths, and the six deltas slowly joined into one plain. Geographers call it the **Hexadeltaic region**, the land of six deltas. The 2025 paper asked why it has that name.

From north to south the six are the **Subarnarekha, Budhabalanga, Baitarani, Brahmani, Mahanadi and Rushikulya**. The **Mahanadi** did the most work, which was the 2021 question. One river that is **not** on the list is the **Indravati**. It rises in Kalahandi and turns its back on the coast, flowing west and south to join the Godavari.

Each of the six has a birthplace. The **Mahanadi** rises at Sihawa in Chhattisgarh. The **Baitarani** rises at Gonasika in Keonjhar. The **Budhabalanga** comes out of the Similipal hills. The **Rushikulya** rises in the Rushimala hills and is unusual because it has **no delta** at its mouth; that sandy mouth is where the olive ridley turtles nest.

The **Brahmani** has the best story. It has no single source. Two rivers come down from the Chota Nagpur plateau, the **Sankh** and the **Koel**, and they meet at **Vedavyas near Rourkela**. From that meeting point the river is called the Brahmani. The **Rengali Dam** stands on it further down. The 2021 paper matched the Rajgangpur–Panposh uplands with these same two rivers.

The Mahanadi's helpers are the **Ib, Tel, Ong and Jira**. In the far south, the **Sabari and Sileru** of Malkangiri flow to the Godavari.
+ Six delta rivers: Subarnarekha, Budhabalanga, Baitarani, Brahmani, Mahanadi, Rushikulya.
+ Indravati is not one of them; it joins the Godavari.
+ Brahmani = Sankh + Koel, meeting at Vedavyas near Rourkela.
+ Rushikulya has no delta. Baitarani rises at Gonasika.

## odi-hills | Four hills and what lies under them | The examiner asked the hills from north to south. The districts and the minerals come next. | 65, 66
In 2025 the paper named four hills of Odisha and asked for them in order from north to south. Take the same four and add the district of each, because that is the natural next question.

In the north-east stands **Meghasani**, "the seat of clouds", in the **Similipal** hills of **Mayurbhanj**. In the middle of the State is **Malayagiri**, near Pallahara in **Angul**. In the west is **Gandhamardan**, on the border of **Bargarh and Balangir**, with the Nrusinghanath temple on one side and Harishankar on the other. In the south is **Mahendragiri**, in **Gajapati**, a hill of the Eastern Ghats tied to the story of Parashurama.

Higher than all of them is **Deomali** in **Koraput**, **1,672 metres**, the highest peak of Odisha.

Now dig under the hills. Odisha is one of India's richest States in minerals, and the 2025 paper matched places with what is mined there.

**Bauxite**, the ore of aluminium, lies on flat hilltops. The biggest deposit is at **Panchpatmali in Koraput**, mined by **NALCO**, whose refinery is at Damanjodi. Gandhamardan has bauxite too.

**Chromite** comes from the **Sukinda valley in Jajpur** and from **Baula-Nuasahi in Keonjhar**. **Iron ore** comes from **Joda and Barbil in Keonjhar**, and from Gorumahisani and Badampahar in Mayurbhanj. **Coal** comes from **Talcher**, in the Brahmani valley, and from the Ib valley. The beach sands at **Chhatrapur in Ganjam** give **monazite**, a source of rare earths.
+ Mahendragiri: Gajapati. Malayagiri: Angul. Meghasani: Mayurbhanj. Gandhamardan: Bargarh–Balangir.
+ Highest peak: Deomali, Koraput, 1,672 m.
+ Panchpatmali, Koraput: bauxite (NALCO).
+ Sukinda and Baula-Nuasahi: chromite. Joda–Barbil: iron ore. Talcher: coal. Chhatrapur: monazite.

## odi-1866 | The famine and the fighter | One terrible year, 1866, woke up Odia public life. One man in Sambalpur had never stopped fighting. | 68, 67
The British took Odisha from the Marathas in **1803** (asked in 2018). Two stories from the years that followed belong in every Odia's memory.

The first is a tragedy. In **1865** the rains failed. Officials in Calcutta refused to believe there was a problem, and by the time rice was sent, the monsoon seas had closed the coast. In **1866** about **one-third of the people** of coastal Odisha died of hunger. It is called the **Na'anka famine**, because it fell in the ninth regnal year, the "anka", of the Gajapati king of Puri. **T. E. Ravenshaw** was the Commissioner.

Out of that grief came anger, and out of anger a voice. In the same year **Gourishankar Ray** started **Utkal Dipika**, the first Odia newspaper. Educated Odias began to ask why their land was split among three provinces. That question led to the **Utkal Sammilani** of 1903 under **Madhusudan Das**, and at last to a separate Odisha province on **1 April 1936**.

The second story is of a fighter. **Surendra Sai** of **Sambalpur** believed the throne of Sambalpur was his by right. The British jailed him at Hazaribagh. In **1857** the rebel sepoys broke open that jail and he walked free. He went home and fought a hill war that outlasted the great Revolt itself. He gave up arms only in **1862**, was arrested again, and died in **Asirgarh fort in 1884**. He is remembered as **Veer Surendra Sai**.

Keep three other heroes separate from him. **Buxi Jagabandhu** led the **Paika Rebellion of 1817** in Khurda. **Chakhi Khuntia**, a priest of Puri, helped the rebels of 1857. **Laxman Naik** of Koraput was hanged in 1943 for the Quit India Movement.
+ 1866 famine: Na'anka. About one-third of coastal Odisha died.
+ Utkal Dipika began in 1866 (Gourishankar Ray).
+ Sambalpur, 1857 and after: Veer Surendra Sai; died at Asirgarh, 1884.
+ Paika Rebellion 1817: Buxi Jagabandhu. Quit India in Koraput: Laxman Naik.

## odi-wild | The black tigers of Similipal | Odisha waited forty-five years for its second national park. | 69
In the hills of Mayurbhanj lives a tiger found almost nowhere else on earth. Its stripes are so thick and close that the animal looks nearly black. These are the **melanistic tigers of Similipal**.

Similipal had been proposed as a national park as far back as 1980, but the final order never came. In **April 2025** it came at last. Similipal became **Odisha's second national park** and India's **107th**, with about **846 square kilometres**. Odisha's first was **Bhitarkanika**, in 1998.

Similipal carries three more labels. It is a **tiger reserve**, one of two in Odisha with **Satkosia**. It is a **biosphere reserve** recognised by UNESCO. And its name comes from the simul, the red silk cotton tree.

Odisha's other wild places each have one animal to remember. **Bhitarkanika** is mangrove forest with the giant **saltwater crocodile**. **Chilika**, Asia's largest brackish water lagoon, has the **Irrawaddy dolphin** and the bird island of Nalabana. **Nandankanan** is known for white tigers.

Then there are the turtles, asked in 2018 and again in 2025. Every winter **olive ridley** turtles come ashore together to lay eggs, an event called arribada. Odisha has three such beaches: **Gahirmatha**, the largest in the world, the **Rushikulya** mouth, and the **Devi** mouth. The 2018 paper wrote "Daya" in place of Devi as the trap. The species is **Vulnerable** on the IUCN Red List. In **2026** Rushikulya had mass nesting, but **Gahirmatha had none**.

Odisha has six Ramsar wetlands: **Chilika, Bhitarkanika, Satkosia, Tampara, Hirakud and Ansupa**.
+ Similipal: national park in April 2025, Odisha's second, India's 107th.
+ Odisha's first national park: Bhitarkanika.
+ Tiger reserves of Odisha: Similipal and Satkosia.
+ Olive ridley beaches: Gahirmatha, Rushikulya, Devi. Status: Vulnerable.

# world | World Geography | The earth inside, the air above, and water, wind and ice
## wor-layers | Down to the core, up to space | The earth has layers below your feet and above your head. Each border has a name. | 72, 71
Imagine a lift that can go straight down into the earth and straight up into the sky. Every time the surroundings change, the lift crosses a border, and the exam asks for the names of those borders.

Go down first. You start in the **crust**, the thin rocky skin. Inside the crust, between its upper and lower parts, is the **Conrad discontinuity**. Then comes the first big border, between the **crust and the mantle**. It is the **Mohorovičić discontinuity**, or **Moho** for short, named after a Croatian scientist.

The mantle is thick and hot. At its bottom you cross the **Gutenberg discontinuity** into the **outer core**, which is liquid. Deeper still, the **Lehmann discontinuity** leads into the solid **inner core**.

How do we know all this? From earthquake waves. **P waves** pass through solids and liquids. **S waves** pass only through solids, and they stop at the outer core. That is how scientists learned it is liquid. Two more words from the 2018 paper: the **focus** is the point underground where an earthquake starts, and the **epicentre** is the point on the surface right above it.

Now go up. The first layer of air is the **troposphere**, where all weather happens. Above it is the **stratosphere**, calm and clear, where jets fly and where the **ozone layer** sits. Then comes the **mesosphere**, the coldest layer, where meteors burn up. Then the **thermosphere**, very hot and very thin. It holds the **ionosphere**, which bounces radio waves back to earth. Last is the exosphere, which fades into space.

The air itself is **78% nitrogen** and **21% oxygen**, with argon third (asked in 2018 and 2025).
+ Crust to mantle: Moho. Mantle to core: Gutenberg. Outer to inner core: Lehmann.
+ Air layers upward: troposphere, stratosphere, mesosphere, thermosphere.
+ Ozone is in the stratosphere. Radio waves bounce off the ionosphere.
+ S waves cannot pass through liquid.

## wor-water | Rivers that turn back, currents that meet | Some rivers cross the same line twice. Some currents make the sea rich where they collide. | 70, 73
A few rivers bend so much that they cross the same line on the map twice. The exam found one in 2025, and there are two more.

The **Congo** in Africa flows north across the **Equator**, curves, and comes back south across it again. That was the 2025 question. Further south, the **Limpopo** rises in South Africa, swings north in a great arc and then south-east to the Indian Ocean, and so it crosses the **Tropic of Capricorn twice**. India has its own example: the **Mahi** crosses the **Tropic of Cancer twice**.

While you are with rivers: the **Nile** is the longest, the **Amazon** carries the most water, and the **Mississippi** has a delta shaped like a **bird's foot** (asked in 2021).

Now the sea. The oceans have rivers too, called currents. Some are warm, flowing from the tropics. Some are cold, flowing from the poles.

Where a **warm current meets a cold one**, two things happen. Fog forms. And tiny floating life, the plankton, grows in huge amounts, so fish gather to feed. The world's great fishing grounds lie at these meeting points.

Off **Newfoundland** in Canada, the warm **Gulf Stream** meets the cold **Labrador Current**. The shallow sea there is the **Grand Banks**. Off **Japan**, the warm **Kuroshio** meets the cold **Oyashio**.

Other cold currents worth knowing are the **Humboldt** off Peru, the **Benguela** off south-west Africa, the **Canaries** and the **California**. The 2021 paper asked which current lies in the southern hemisphere: the **West Wind Drift**.

A last sea fact from 2018: the **Sargasso Sea** has no coast at all. It is held in place only by currents.
+ Limpopo: Tropic of Capricorn twice. Congo: Equator twice. Mahi: Tropic of Cancer twice.
+ Grand Banks: Gulf Stream meets Labrador Current.
+ Off Japan: Kuroshio meets Oyashio.
+ Mississippi: bird-foot delta. Sargasso Sea: no coastline.

## wor-shapers | Wind, ice, water and stone | Four workers shape the land. Learn which worker made which shape. | 74, 75
The surface of the earth is carved by four workers: rivers, ice, wind and the water that moves underground. A match question gives you a landform and asks who made it.

The **river** cuts a V-shaped valley in the hills. On the plain it swings in loops called meanders. When a loop is cut off, it becomes an **oxbow lake**. At the sea, the river drops its mud as a delta.

The **glacier**, a river of ice, carves a U-shaped valley. It scoops out an armchair-shaped hollow called a cirque. The rocks and mud it carries and drops form a **moraine**.

The **wind** works in deserts. It piles sand into crescent dunes called **barchans**, and it grinds rocks into mushroom shapes.

**Underground water** works in limestone country. Dripping water leaves stone icicles hanging from a cave roof, the **stalactites**, and pillars growing from the floor, the stalagmites. Remember it this way: stalactites hold on tight to the ceiling.

The wind also gives us a second kind of question, the local winds. The 2021 paper asked a set of them.

The **Chinook** blows down the eastern side of the **Rocky Mountains**. It is warm and dry and melts snow so fast that it is called the **Snow Eater**. The **Foehn** is its twin in the **Alps**. The **Sirocco** is a hot wind from the Sahara to southern Europe. The **Harmattan** blows from the Sahara to West Africa. The **Khamsin** blows in Egypt.

Two are cold: the **Mistral** in France and the **Bora** on the Adriatic coast. The **Loo** is the hot summer wind of north India.
+ Moraine: glacier. Barchan: wind. Stalactite: underground water. Oxbow lake: river.
+ Chinook: Rockies, the Snow Eater. Foehn: Alps.
+ Cold winds: Mistral (France), Bora (Adriatic).
+ Sirocco and Harmattan come from the Sahara.

# india | India Geography | Passes, borders, rivers and the monsoon
## ind-edges | Along the edge of India | Walk the northern wall through its passes, then count the neighbours at each State's door. | 76, 77
The Himalaya is a wall, and a pass is a door in that wall. Travel from west to east and open each door.

In **Ladakh**, **Zoji La** carries the road from Srinagar to Leh. In **Himachal Pradesh**, **Rohtang** links Kullu with Lahaul and Spiti, **Bara Lacha La** lies on the road to Leh, and **Shipki La** is where the Sutlej enters India from Tibet. In **Uttarakhand**, **Lipulekh** is on the old route to Kailash Mansarovar, and **Mana** and **Niti** lead to Tibet as well.

In **Sikkim** there are two, **Nathu La** and **Jelep La**. The 2021 paper placed Jelep La in Arunachal Pradesh as its trap. In **Arunachal Pradesh** the well-known pass is **Bomdi La**, close to the **Sela Tunnel** (asked in 2021).

Now walk along the border and count neighbours. The 2025 paper matched States with the countries they touch.

**Myanmar** touches four States: **Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Manipur and Mizoram**. **Tripura** does not touch Myanmar. It is wrapped on three sides by **Bangladesh**.

**Sikkim** is small but touches three countries: China, Nepal and Bhutan. **West Bengal** also touches three: Bangladesh, Nepal and Bhutan. **Arunachal Pradesh** touches Bhutan, China and Myanmar. **Uttarakhand** touches China and Nepal. **Himachal Pradesh** touches only China. **Gujarat** touches only Pakistan.

India's longest land border is with **Bangladesh**. Its longest coastline belongs to **Gujarat** (asked in 2018), and nine States have a coast.
+ Zoji La: Ladakh. Rohtang: Himachal. Lipulekh: Uttarakhand. Bomdi La: Arunachal.
+ Nathu La and Jelep La: Sikkim.
+ Myanmar touches Arunachal, Nagaland, Manipur, Mizoram. Not Tripura.
+ Longest land border: Bangladesh. Longest coastline: Gujarat.

## ind-south | Where the two Ghats meet | The rivers of the plateau and the hills that give birth to them. | 78, 80
Two lines of hills run down the two sides of south India. The **Western Ghats** are high and unbroken. The **Eastern Ghats** are lower and cut into pieces by rivers. Follow both lines south and they come together in one knot of hills, the **Nilgiris**, the Blue Mountains. The highest peak there is **Doddabetta**.

South of the Nilgiris is a gap in the wall, the **Palghat Gap**. Beyond it rise the **Anamalai hills**, with **Anamudi**, the highest peak in south India. Further south still are the **Cardamom hills**, the hills closest to Kanyakumari. That was the 2025 question, and it used the same four options you will see again: Cardamom, Nilgiri, Anamalai and Shevaroy. The **Shevaroy** hills belong to the Eastern Ghats in Tamil Nadu.

These hills are the birthplace of the southern rivers.

The **Kaveri** rises at **Talakaveri** in the **Brahmagiri hills of Kodagu** in Karnataka, and it is the longest river of Tamil Nadu. The **Krishna** rises at **Mahabaleshwar** in Maharashtra. The **Godavari**, the longest river of the peninsula, rises at **Trimbak near Nashik**. All three flow east to the Bay of Bengal.

Now move to the middle of India, to the **Maikal range**. At **Amarkantak** two rivers are born and go opposite ways: the **Narmada** flows west and the **Son** flows north-east to the Ganga. The **Mahanadi** rises not far away, at Sihawa. The 2021 paper asked which river does not rise in the Maikal range. The answer was the **Tapi**, which rises at **Multai** in the Satpura hills.

The Narmada and the Tapi are the two big rivers that flow west, through rift valleys, into the Arabian Sea.
+ Western and Eastern Ghats meet at the Nilgiri hills.
+ Closest to Kanyakumari: Cardamom hills. Highest in the south: Anamudi.
+ Kaveri: Talakaveri, Brahmagiri hills, Kodagu.
+ Narmada and Son: Amarkantak. Tapi: Multai. Godavari: Trimbak. Krishna: Mahabaleshwar.

## ind-monsoon | Two oceans decide the rain | El Niño in the Pacific and a dipole in the Indian Ocean pull the monsoon in different ways. | 79
Every June, India waits for the monsoon. Whether it comes strong or weak is decided far away, in the temperature of two oceans.

The first is the Pacific. In some years the sea off **Peru** becomes unusually **warm**. Fishermen there named it **El Niño**, "the boy child", because it comes around Christmas. In an El Niño year the winds over the Pacific change, and India usually gets a **weak monsoon**. The opposite is **La Niña**, when the same waters turn **cool**. That usually brings India **good rain**.

The second ocean is our own. Sometimes the **western Indian Ocean**, near Africa, becomes **warmer than the eastern part** near Indonesia. This is a **positive Indian Ocean Dipole**. It pushes moist air towards India and usually **helps the monsoon**. When the east is warmer, the dipole is negative, and the monsoon suffers.

Because it behaves like a small El Niño in our own ocean, the dipole is also called the **Indian Niño**. The 2025 paper asked exactly that in two statements.

A few more monsoon words. The monsoon first reaches **Kerala around 1 June**. Before it come the summer showers: **mango showers** in Kerala and Karnataka, and the violent evening storms of Bengal called **Kalbaisakhi**. In winter the north-west gets rain from **western disturbances**, which come all the way from the Mediterranean.

The 2021 paper asked a hard one on why the monsoon fluctuates. The short answer to carry: high winds called **jet streams** over north India must shift north before the monsoon can arrive.
+ Positive Indian Ocean Dipole: west warmer than east; helps the monsoon.
+ Indian Ocean Dipole = "Indian Niño".
+ El Niño: warm Pacific off Peru, weak monsoon. La Niña: cool, good rain.
+ Monsoon reaches Kerala about 1 June.

# institutions | Institutions | The United Nations, the clubs of countries, and who publishes what
## ins-un | The house on the East River | Fifteen seats at one table, a new person in the top chair, and offices spread around the world. | 81, 86, 84
The United Nations was founded on **24 October 1945** and has **193 members**. Its most powerful room is the **Security Council**, with **15 seats** at a horseshoe table.

Five seats never change: the **USA, Russia, China, the United Kingdom and France**. Each holds a veto. The other **ten** are filled by election. **Five are elected each year**, and each serves **two years** (asked in 2021).

In **June 2026** the General Assembly chose five countries for **2027–28**: **Austria, Kyrgyzstan, Portugal, Trinidad and Tobago, and Zimbabwe**. Two results stood out. **Kyrgyzstan** won a seat for the **first time**, beating the Philippines. **Germany lost**. Pakistan's term ends in December 2026. The 2021 paper asked this same question for the election of June 2022, so learn the list.

The year also brings a new face. The **Secretary-General** is **appointed by the General Assembly on the recommendation of the Security Council**. A permanent member can block any name, so the choice is really made in the smaller room. The term is five years. **António Guterres** finishes his second term on 31 December 2026, and a new Secretary-General starts on 1 January 2027. Learn that name before the exam.

The UN's agencies live in many cities. **Geneva** has the most: WHO, ILO, WTO and the World Meteorological Organization. The 2025 paper falsely placed the WMO in Vienna. **Vienna** has the IAEA. **Rome** has the FAO. **Paris** has UNESCO. **Bern** has the Universal Postal Union. **Montreal** has the ICAO. **Nairobi** has UNEP.

And **London** has just one: the **International Maritime Organization**, which makes the safety rules for ships.

The UN's court, the International Court of Justice, sits at **The Hague**. One Indian, **Dr Nagendra Singh**, has been its President (asked in 2018).
+ New Security Council members 2027–28: Austria, Kyrgyzstan, Portugal, Trinidad and Tobago, Zimbabwe.
+ Ten non-permanent members, two-year terms, five elected each year.
+ Secretary-General: General Assembly appoints, on the Security Council's recommendation.
+ IMO: London. WMO: Geneva. IAEA: Vienna. FAO: Rome. UNESCO: Paris.

## ins-clubs | Three clubs and their hosts | BRICS, the SCO and the G20 meet once a year. The examiner asks who hosted. | 82, 83, 87
Besides the UN, countries form smaller clubs. Each club meets once a year in a different member's country, and "who hosted?" is a question this exam asked in both 2021 and 2025.

**BRICS** began with Brazil, Russia, India and China; South Africa joined in 2010. In 2024 came Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran and the UAE, and **Indonesia** became a full member in **January 2025**. The 17th summit was at Rio de Janeiro in 2025. In **2026 India held the chair** and hosted the **18th summit in New Delhi** in September, which ended with the New Delhi Declaration. The club has its own bank, the **New Development Bank**, and its headquarters is in **Shanghai**, not Delhi.

The **Shanghai Cooperation Organisation** was founded in **Shanghai in 2001**. Its secretariat is in **Beijing**, and its anti-terror office, RATS, is in **Tashkent**. India and Pakistan joined in 2017, Iran in 2023 and Belarus in 2024. The 2025 summit was at **Tianjin, China**. The **2026 summit**, in the club's 25th year, was at **Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan**.

The **G20** is the club of the largest economies. Its chair moves each year, and the last four make an easy chain: **India 2023** (New Delhi, where the African Union became a member), **Brazil 2024** (Rio de Janeiro), **South Africa 2025** (Johannesburg, the first summit in Africa) and the **United States 2026**. The G20 has no permanent office. The last, present and next chairs work together as a "troika".

Others asked before: **BIMSTEC** has its secretariat in **Dhaka**. **SAARC** is in Kathmandu. **NATO** is in **Brussels**. The **International Solar Alliance**, started by India and France, is at **Gurugram**, and its dream is "One Sun, One World, One Grid".
+ BRICS 2026: India chaired, 18th summit in New Delhi. Bank in Shanghai.
+ SCO 2026: Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan. 2025: Tianjin.
+ G20: India 2023, Brazil 2024, South Africa 2025, USA 2026.
+ Indonesia joined BRICS in January 2025.

## ins-reports | Who published that report? | Every famous index has a publisher. Match them once and the marks are easy. | 85
When a newspaper says "India ranks 38th", the exam does not ask the rank. It asks **who published the report**. The 2021 paper did it twice, with the World Bank's "South Asia Economic Focus" and the World Giving Index of the Charities Aid Foundation.

Sort the reports by their publishers.

The **UN family**. The **Human Development Report** comes from the **UNDP**. The **Emissions Gap Report** comes from **UNEP**; it measures the gap between what countries promised on climate and what the planet needs. The **World Investment Report** comes from **UNCTAD**, the UN's trade and development body. The **Global Innovation Index** comes from **WIPO**, the World Intellectual Property Organization. The **World Employment and Social Outlook** comes from the ILO.

The **money twins** in Washington. The **IMF** publishes the **World Economic Outlook** and the Global Financial Stability Report. The **World Bank** publishes the **World Development Report**.

The **private groups**. The **Global Gender Gap Report** comes from the **World Economic Forum**. The **Global Hunger Index** comes from two charities, **Concern Worldwide and Welthungerhilfe**. The **World Press Freedom Index** comes from **Reporters Without Borders**. The **Global Peace Index** comes from the **Institute for Economics and Peace**. The **Living Planet Report** comes from the **WWF**.

Inside India, the **India State of Forest Report** comes from the Forest Survey of India, and the national poverty index from NITI Aayog.

A few 2026 ranks, in case the examiner returns to the 2018 style: India was **38th** in the Global Innovation Index, **127th** in the Global Peace Index and **100th** in the Global Hunger Index.
+ Emissions Gap Report: UNEP. Human Development Report: UNDP.
+ World Investment Report: UNCTAD. Global Innovation Index: WIPO.
+ World Economic Outlook: IMF. Global Gender Gap: World Economic Forum.
+ Global Hunger Index: Concern Worldwide and Welthungerhilfe.

# science | Science and Technology | Germs, rockets and the chemistry in your kitchen
## sci-germs | Four kinds of germ | Ask two things about any disease: what causes it, and how it travels. | 88
In 2025 the paper asked about mumps and gave four choices made from two questions. Is it caused by a **virus or a bacterium**? Does it spread through **air or water**? Mumps is an **airborne viral** disease. The same grid fits every other disease, so learn the grid.

There are four kinds of germ.

**Bacteria** are single living cells, and antibiotics can kill them. **Cholera** is caused by a bacterium, Vibrio cholerae, and it spreads through **dirty water**. So cholera is a **waterborne bacterial** disease. **Typhoid** is the same kind. **Tuberculosis** is bacterial too, but it travels through the **air**. Plague, tetanus and leprosy are also bacterial.

**Viruses** are far smaller and are not killed by antibiotics. **Mumps, measles, chickenpox, influenza and COVID-19** are airborne viral diseases. **Polio and hepatitis A** are viral but spread through water and food. **Dengue** is viral and carried by the Aedes mosquito. **Rabies** comes from a dog's bite. AIDS is caused by the virus HIV.

**Protozoa** are one-celled animals. **Malaria** is caused by the protozoan Plasmodium and carried by the female Anopheles mosquito. Kala-azar and sleeping sickness are also protozoan.

**Fungi** cause skin diseases such as **ringworm** and athlete's foot.

A few diseases have no germ at all. They come from a missing vitamin. No **vitamin C** gives scurvy. No **vitamin D** gives rickets. No **vitamin A** gives night blindness. No **vitamin B1** gives beriberi.

One date for the calendar: **World TB Day is 24 March**, the day Robert Koch announced the TB germ in 1882 (asked in 2025).
+ Cholera: waterborne, bacterial. Typhoid too.
+ Mumps: airborne, viral. Tuberculosis: airborne, bacterial.
+ Malaria: protozoan. Dengue: viral. Ringworm: fungal.
+ Scurvy: vitamin C. Rickets: vitamin D.

## sci-space | A hard year for the workhorse | Two PSLV failures in a row, and four people around the Moon. | 89, 90
For thirty years the **PSLV** was called ISRO's workhorse, a rocket that almost never failed. Then it failed twice in a row.

On **18 May 2025**, **PSLV-C61** lifted off with an earth-watching satellite, **EOS-09**. The third stage lost pressure and the satellite was lost. The 2025 paper asked which orbit EOS-09 was meant for: a **Sun-synchronous polar orbit**.

On **12 January 2026**, **PSLV-C62** tried again. Once more the trouble came in the **third stage**. This time the lost satellite was **EOS-N1**, named **Anvesha**, built for the DRDO to watch the seas, along with a group of small satellites.

Other missions went well. **NISAR**, a radar satellite made by **NASA and ISRO** together, was launched on a **GSLV** in July 2025. **Shubhanshu Shukla** became the first Indian to stay on the International Space Station, on the Axiom-4 mission in 2025; the first Indian in space was Rakesh Sharma in 1984. India's own crewed programme is **Gaganyaan**, and its first test flight will carry a robot named **Vyommitra**.

The biggest space story of 2026 was American. In **April 2026** NASA's **Artemis II** carried **four astronauts around the Moon** and back. They were three Americans and one Canadian, **Jeremy Hansen**. They rode in the **Orion** capsule on top of the **SLS** rocket.

Note the trap. Artemis II **did not land**. It only flew around. It was still the first time people had travelled to the Moon since **Apollo 17 in 1972**. A landing is the job of a later Artemis mission.

From older papers: Chandrayaan-2's lander was **Vikram** (2018), China's Chang'e-5 brought back Moon rock (2021), and **SpaceX** has its headquarters at Hawthorne, California (2025).
+ PSLV-C62, 12 January 2026: failed in the third stage; lost EOS-N1 Anvesha.
+ PSLV-C61, May 2025: lost EOS-09.
+ Artemis II, April 2026: crewed flight around the Moon, no landing.
+ NISAR: NASA and ISRO, launched on a GSLV.

## sci-kitchen | Chemistry at home, and a prize for empty space | Everyday things with exam names, and the Nobel Prize for a crystal full of holes. | 91, 92
The syllabus says "use of science in everyday life", and the 2018 paper took it literally: what are non-stick pans coated with? The answer was **Teflon**. Walk through a house and collect the rest.

The iron sheet on the roof does not rust because it is **galvanised**, which means coated with **zinc**. Food cans are coated with **tin**. The steel plates in the kitchen are **stainless steel**: iron mixed with **chromium and nickel**.

The gas in the cylinder, **LPG**, is mostly butane and propane. It has no smell of its own, so a strong-smelling chemical, **ethyl mercaptan**, is added to warn you of a leak. **CNG** is mostly **methane**.

On the shelf, **baking soda** is sodium bicarbonate. **Washing soda** is sodium carbonate. **Vinegar** is weak acetic acid. Common salt is sodium chloride. **Dry ice**, used to keep ice cream cold, is solid **carbon dioxide**. Bleaching powder cleans the water tank.

Now from the kitchen to Stockholm. The 2021 paper asked who won the **2019 Chemistry Nobel** for the **lithium-ion battery**, the one inside your phone. So this exam is willing to ask a prize from a few years back.

The **2025 Chemistry Nobel** went to **Susumu Kitagawa, Richard Robson and Omar Yaghi** for **metal–organic frameworks**. These are crystals built like scaffolding, with metal joints and carbon rods, and they are full of tiny empty rooms. Those rooms can trap gases, store hydrogen, or pull drinking water out of desert air.

Keep the chain of Chemistry prizes: **2019** lithium-ion batteries, **2020** CRISPR gene editing, **2023** quantum dots, **2024** protein design, **2025** metal–organic frameworks. The **2025 Peace Prize** went to **María Corina Machado** of Venezuela.
+ Galvanising: a coat of zinc. Food cans: tin. Non-stick pans: Teflon.
+ LPG smell: ethyl mercaptan. Dry ice: solid carbon dioxide.
+ Chemistry Nobel 2025: metal–organic frameworks (Kitagawa, Robson, Yaghi).
+ Chemistry Nobel 2019: lithium-ion batteries.

## new-unesco | Three lists of UNESCO | Places, living traditions and old documents each have their own list. | 96, 98
UNESCO keeps three different lists, and the examiner enjoys mixing them. Keep them in three boxes.

**Box one: places.** This is the **World Heritage List**. In **July 2026**, at a meeting in **Busan, South Korea**, the **Ancient Buddhist Site of Sarnath** was added as India's **45th** World Heritage Site. Sarnath, near Varanasi, is where the Buddha gave his **first sermon**, and its lion pillar gave India its national emblem.

Go back two steps to be safe. The **44th** site, in 2025, was the **Maratha Military Landscapes**, twelve forts of Shivaji's kingdom. The **43rd**, in 2024, was the **Moidams** of Assam, the burial mounds of the Ahom kings. **Santiniketan** and the Hoysala temples came in 2023. Odisha has one site on this list, the **Sun Temple at Konark**.

**Box two: living traditions.** This is the list of **Intangible Cultural Heritage**: festivals, dances, crafts. In **December 2025**, at a session held in New Delhi, **Deepavali** was added. Earlier Indian entries include **Durga Puja** of Kolkata (2021), **Garba** of Gujarat (2023), Kumbh Mela and Yoga. From Odisha, **Chhau** dance is on this list.

**Box three: documents.** This is the **Memory of the World Register**, for old manuscripts and records. The 2025 paper asked what it protects: documentary heritage. In 2025 the manuscripts of the **Bhagavad Gita** and Bharata's **Natyashastra** were added.

So when the question says "added to UNESCO's list", stop and ask: a place, a tradition or a document?
+ Sarnath: India's 45th World Heritage Site, July 2026.
+ 44th: Maratha Military Landscapes. 43rd: Moidams of Assam.
+ Deepavali: Intangible Cultural Heritage, December 2025.
+ Memory of the World Register is for documents.

## new-vande | A song turns 150 | It was written on a single day in 1875 and became the cry of a freedom movement. | 97
On **7 November 1875** a deputy magistrate in Bengal wrote a poem to his motherland. His name was **Bankim Chandra Chatterjee**, and the poem began with two words, **Vande Mataram**, "Mother, I bow to thee".

For some years it stayed in his papers. In **1882** he placed it in his novel **Anandamath**, a story of monks who rise against foreign rule. Then, in **1896**, at the Calcutta session of the Congress, **Rabindranath Tagore** sang it in public for the first time.

The song truly caught fire in **1905**. When Curzon divided Bengal, crowds marched through the streets shouting its first two words. The British banned the cry, which only made it louder. It became the slogan of the Swadeshi movement and the name of a newspaper.

When India became free there was a debate over which song should be the anthem. On **24 January 1950** the Constituent Assembly settled it. Tagore's **Jana Gana Mana** became the **National Anthem**, and Vande Mataram was given equal honour as the **National Song**.

On **7 November 2025** the country began a year-long celebration of the song's **150 years**. In 2026 Parliament went further and changed the law on insults to national honour, so that deliberately stopping the singing of Vande Mataram is now an offence.

Keep the two poets and their songs straight. **Bankim Chandra: Vande Mataram, the National Song. Tagore: Jana Gana Mana, the National Anthem.** Tagore also wrote Bangladesh's anthem, Amar Sonar Bangla.
+ Vande Mataram: Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, 1875; in Anandamath, 1882.
+ First sung at the 1896 Congress session by Rabindranath Tagore.
+ National Song since 24 January 1950.
+ 150th year celebrations began 7 November 2025.
