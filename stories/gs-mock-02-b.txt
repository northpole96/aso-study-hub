# defence | Defence | Who commands, who trains, and what flew or sailed this year
## def-chiefs | Four new chairs | In 2026 every top post in the armed forces got a new name on the door. | 29, 36
The year 2026 was unusual for the forces. All four of the top chairs changed hands within five months. The 2021 paper asked "the present Chief of Air Staff", so this is a question waiting to happen.

On **31 May 2026** two men took over on the same day. **General N. S. Raja Subramani** became the **third Chief of Defence Staff**, after Bipin Rawat and Anil Chauhan. **Admiral Krishna Swaminathan** became Chief of the Naval Staff, after Admiral Dinesh K. Tripathi. On **30 June 2026 General Dhiraj Seth** became Chief of the Army Staff, after General Upendra Dwivedi. On **31 October 2026 Air Chief Marshal Ashutosh Dixit** became Chief of the Air Staff, after A. P. Singh.

The Chief of Defence Staff is the newest of these posts. It was created in 2019 so that the three forces plan and fight together. The main job of the new CDS is to set up joint **theatre commands**.

Now the ranks, which the 2021 paper also asked. The three forces use different names for the same level, and one word is a trap. A **Captain in the Navy is equal to a Colonel in the Army**. He is far senior to an Army Captain.

Go up the ladder with three names on each step, Army, Navy, Air Force. **Major, Lieutenant Commander, Squadron Leader. Lieutenant Colonel, Commander, Wing Commander. Colonel, Captain, Group Captain. Brigadier, Commodore, Air Commodore. Major General, Rear Admiral, Air Vice Marshal. Lieutenant General, Vice Admiral, Air Marshal. General, Admiral, Air Chief Marshal.**

Above them all sit three honorary ranks: Field Marshal, Admiral of the Fleet and Marshal of the Air Force. Arjan Singh is the only officer ever made Marshal of the Air Force.
+ CDS (3rd): General N. S. Raja Subramani, 31 May 2026.
+ Army: Dhiraj Seth. Navy: Krishna Swaminathan. Air: Ashutosh Dixit.
+ Navy Captain = Army Colonel = Group Captain.
+ Squadron Leader = Major. Commodore = Brigadier.

## def-exercises | The exercise calendar | Each exercise has a partner country. The examiner swaps the partners. | 30, 31, 32
Every year the forces train with friendly countries, and each exercise has a fixed name. The exam has asked **Dharma Guardian** with **Japan** twice, in 2021 and 2025. Its seventh edition was held in **Uttarakhand** in 2026.

The Army's exercises are the largest group. **Yudh Abhyas** is with the USA. **Shakti** is with France. **Nomadic Elephant** is with Mongolia (asked in 2025). **AUSTRAHIND** is with Australia. **Surya Kiran** is with Nepal, **Sampriti** with Bangladesh and **Mitra Shakti** with Sri Lanka. **Garuda Shakti** is a special forces exercise with **Indonesia**.

Then comes the trap: three Central Asian neighbours with names that sound alike. **KHANJAR** is with **Kyrgyzstan**; in 2026 it was held at Misamari in Assam. **DUSTLIK** is with **Uzbekistan**; in 2026 it was at Aundh, Pune. **KAZIND** is with **Kazakhstan**. Say them three times.

At sea, **Malabar** began in **1992** between India and the USA. **Japan** became a permanent member in 2015 and **Australia** joined in 2020. So it has four members, the same four as the **Quad** (asked in 2021). France is not one of them; India's naval exercise with **France is Varuna**. With Japan alone it is JIMEX, with Singapore SIMBEX and with the United Kingdom Konkan.

Do not confuse these with **MILAN**. MILAN is not with one country. It is a **multilateral** meeting of many navies, hosted by the Indian Navy every two years. It began in **1995** in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and later moved to **Visakhapatnam**, the home of the Eastern Naval Command. In **February 2026** MILAN and the **International Fleet Review** were held there ⟨CHECK⟩. The Navy's own biggest drill is **TROPEX** (asked in 2025).
+ KHANJAR: Kyrgyzstan. DUSTLIK: Uzbekistan. KAZIND: Kazakhstan.
+ Dharma Guardian: Japan. Nomadic Elephant: Mongolia. Garuda Shakti: Indonesia.
+ Malabar: India, USA, Japan, Australia. Varuna: France.
+ MILAN: multilateral, hosted by the Indian Navy at Visakhapatnam.

## def-missiles | The missile family | Know each missile by where it starts and what it hits. | 33
In 2025 the paper said Pralay was an air-to-surface cruise missile and asked if that was correct. It was not. The examiner changes one word in the description, so you must know each missile by two things: where it is fired from and what it hits.

Start with the **Agni** family. They are **surface-to-surface ballistic missiles**, fired from land at targets on land, and they rise high before falling. **Agni-5** is the longest, with a range of **more than 5,000 km**. **Prithvi** is the short-range member of the same kind. **Pralay** is also surface-to-surface. It is called quasi-ballistic because it can change its path in flight, and its range is only about **150 to 500 km**.

**BrahMos** is different. It is a **cruise missile**, which flies low like a plane without a pilot. It is **supersonic**, faster than sound, and it was built by **India and Russia** together. Its name joins two rivers, the **Brahmaputra and the Moskva**. **Nirbhay** is India's slower, subsonic cruise missile.

**Akash** is a **surface-to-air** missile: it is fired from the ground at enemy aircraft. **Astra** is **air-to-air**, fired from one aircraft at another. **Nag** is an **anti-tank** missile. The **S-400** air defence system was bought from **Russia** (asked in 2021).

Many of these came from one programme, the Integrated Guided Missile Development Programme, led by Dr A. P. J. Abdul Kalam. **Dr Tessy Thomas**, who led the Agni projects, is called the Missile Woman of India (asked in 2021).

Most tests happen on the Odisha coast: the **Integrated Test Range at Chandipur** and **Dr A. P. J. Abdul Kalam Island**. A **scramjet** engine, asked in 2025, burns fuel in air moving faster than sound and has no moving parts.
+ Agni-5: surface-to-surface ballistic, over 5,000 km.
+ BrahMos: supersonic cruise missile, India and Russia.
+ Akash: surface-to-air. Astra: air-to-air. Nag: anti-tank.
+ Pralay: surface-to-surface, quasi-ballistic, short range.

## def-borders | Who guards which border | Six forces, six jobs, and one ministry that is not the Ministry of Defence. | 34
The soldier you see at a border post is often not from the Army. He belongs to one of the **Central Armed Police Forces**, and all of them work under the **Ministry of Home Affairs**, not the Ministry of Defence. This one fact is the examiner's favourite false statement.

Each force was born from an event. After the war with China in 1962, India raised the **Indo-Tibetan Border Police** to watch the **China** border (the 2025 paper had three statements on it). The **Sashastra Seema Bal** followed in 1963 and now guards the open borders with **Nepal and Bhutan**.

After the war with Pakistan in 1965, India raised the **Border Security Force** on **1 December 1965**. The BSF guards the borders with **Pakistan and Bangladesh**. The link with Bangladesh also explains "Operation Zero Line" in the 2025 paper.

The oldest force of all is the **Assam Rifles**, raised in **1835**. It guards the border with **Myanmar**. The **Central Reserve Police Force** began in **1939** and handles trouble inside the country. The **Central Industrial Security Force**, raised in **1969**, protects airports, the Delhi Metro and big industries. The **National Security Guard**, the black cat commandos, came in 1984.

One force breaks the rule. The **Indian Coast Guard**, set up in **1978**, is under the **Ministry of Defence**. It runs SAREX, the national sea rescue exercise, which was held off Paradip in 2026.
+ BSF: raised 1965; Pakistan and Bangladesh borders; Home Ministry.
+ ITBP: 1962, China. SSB: Nepal and Bhutan. Assam Rifles: 1835, Myanmar.
+ CISF 1969, CRPF 1939.
+ Coast Guard is under the Ministry of Defence.

## def-academies | Where officers are made, and how they are honoured | A map of the training academies, and the two ladders of medals. | 35, 37
A young cadet's road begins near Pune. The **National Defence Academy** is at **Khadakwasla, Pune**, and trains cadets of all three forces together. After that the roads part.

Army cadets go to the **Indian Military Academy at Dehradun**. Navy cadets go to the **Indian Naval Academy at Ezhimala** in Kerala. Air Force cadets go to the **Air Force Academy at Dundigal** near Hyderabad. Those who join the Army for a short service go to the **Officers Training Academy at Chennai**; a second one is at Gaya. The examiner likes to place the OTA in Pune, because the NDA is there.

Senior officers return to study. The **Defence Services Staff College** is at **Wellington** in the Nilgiris. The **College of Defence Management** is at Secunderabad. The **National Defence College** is in New Delhi. The **Indian National Defence University** is coming up at **Binola in Haryana**, and that one has been asked twice (2018 and 2021). The police have their own academy, the **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel National Police Academy at Hyderabad** (asked in 2025).

Now the medals. There are two ladders, and each has three steps.

For bravery **in war**: the **Param Vir Chakra**, then the Maha Vir Chakra, then the Vir Chakra. For bravery **in peacetime**: the **Ashoka Chakra**, then the Kirti Chakra, then the Shaurya Chakra.

The first Param Vir Chakra went to **Major Somnath Sharma** in 1947. The only Air Force officer to get it is **Flying Officer Nirmal Jit Singh Sekhon**, for the 1971 war (asked in 2021). The medal itself was designed by Savitri Khanolkar.
+ OTA: Chennai (and Gaya). NDA: Khadakwasla, Pune.
+ IMA: Dehradun. Naval Academy: Ezhimala. Air Force Academy: Dundigal.
+ Indian National Defence University: Binola, Haryana.
+ Highest in peacetime: Ashoka Chakra. Highest in war: Param Vir Chakra.

## def-operations | An operation needs a name | The 2025 paper asked three operation names. Here are the ones since then. | 38
When the forces go out to fight or to help, the mission gets a name. The 2025 paper asked three: **Operation Jindagi** (the rescue at the Silkyara tunnel), **Operation Keller** (against terrorists in Shopian) and **Operation Olivia** (the Coast Guard's watch over nesting olive ridley turtles in Odisha).

The year 2025 gave four more, and they tell a story of a busy year.

On **28 March 2025** a great earthquake hit **Myanmar**. India sent rescue teams, a field hospital and ships within hours. This was **Operation Brahma**.

On **22 April 2025** terrorists killed tourists at **Pahalgam** in Kashmir. On **7 May 2025** India struck terror camps across the border. This was **Operation Sindoor**. A later operation that hunted down the Pahalgam attackers was named Operation Mahadev.

In **June 2025** fighting broke out between Israel and Iran, and thousands of Indian students were stuck. India brought them home in **Operation Sindhu**.

In late **2025 Cyclone Ditwah** flooded **Sri Lanka**. India sent ships and aircraft with relief. This was **Operation Sagar Bandhu**, "friend across the sea".

In **2026** came **Operation Urja Suraksha**. "Urja" means energy. When the Strait of Hormuz crisis put oil and gas ships in danger, the **Navy escorted** them to India.

Notice the pattern. Most of these are not about fighting. They are about rescue and relief, and the examiner likes exactly these.
+ Brahma: earthquake relief to Myanmar, 2025.
+ Sindoor: strikes after the Pahalgam attack, 7 May 2025.
+ Sindhu: bringing Indians home from Iran, 2025.
+ Sagar Bandhu: cyclone relief to Sri Lanka, 2025.
+ Urja Suraksha: Navy escorts for oil and gas ships, 2026.

# environment | Environment | The words of ecology, the treaties, the forests and their keepers
## env-words | The words of ecology | A handful of scientists gave ecology its vocabulary. Every paper asks one of their words. | 39, 40, 47
Every ASO paper has asked the meaning of some word from ecology: plankton in 2018, hemiparasites in 2021, edge species and endemic species in 2025. The words become easy when you meet the people who made them.

**Ernst Haeckel** coined the word **ecology**, the study of living things and their surroundings. **A. G. Tansley** coined **ecosystem** in 1935. **Charles Elton** drew the first **pyramid of numbers**: many plants at the bottom, fewer plant-eaters, very few hunters at the top.

Why does the pyramid narrow? **Raymond Lindeman** answered in 1942 with the **10 per cent law**. Only about **10%** of the energy at one level passes to the next. The rest is lost as heat. So a pyramid of **energy is always upright**; it can never stand on its tip.

Next, how do you measure the richness of life? **R. H. Whittaker** gave three measures. **Alpha diversity** is the variety inside one community, such as one pond. **Beta diversity** is the **change in species as you move from one community to the next**, say from the pond to the forest beside it. **Gamma diversity** is the total over the whole region (asked in 2021).

Where two communities meet there is a border zone called an **ecotone**. Species that live mainly in that zone are **edge species** (asked in 2025). A species found in only one region of the world is **endemic** (asked in 2025).

That brings us to **Norman Myers**. In 1988 he gave the idea of the **biodiversity hotspot** (asked in 2021). A region must pass two tests: it must have at least **1,500 endemic plant species**, and it must have **lost at least 70%** of its original vegetation. The world has **36** hotspots. **Four** touch India: the Himalaya, Indo-Burma, the Western Ghats with Sri Lanka, and Sundaland, which includes the Nicobar Islands.
+ 10 per cent law: Raymond Lindeman, 1942.
+ Beta diversity: change between communities. Gamma: the whole region.
+ Hotspot: 1,500 endemic plants and 70% of vegetation lost. Idea of Norman Myers.
+ Ecosystem: Tansley. Ecology: Haeckel. Pyramid of numbers: Elton.

## env-treaties | A town, a poison, a promise | Most environment treaties are named after the town where they were signed. | 41, 42
Environment treaties are easy to mix up, because each is known by a town's name. Tie each town to one problem.

Begin in **Stockholm**, twice. In **1972** the first big UN meeting on the environment was held there; World Environment Day, 5 June, marks its opening. In **2001** the **Stockholm Convention** was signed, and it deals with **persistent organic pollutants**. These are poisons such as DDT that do not break down and travel up the food chain.

**Minamata** is a town in Japan where factory waste poisoned the sea. The Minamata Convention deals with **mercury** (asked in 2021). **Basel** controls the movement of hazardous waste between countries. **Rotterdam** says dangerous chemicals can be traded only with the buyer's informed consent. **Ramsar**, a town in Iran, gave its name to the wetlands treaty of 1971. **CITES**, signed in Washington, controls trade in endangered animals and plants. **Montreal (1987)** protects the ozone layer.

Now the climate story, which has its own line of towns.

At the **Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro in 1992**, countries signed the **UN Framework Convention on Climate Change**. The same summit gave the Convention on Biological Diversity and Agenda 21. Rio also gave the rule of **common but differentiated responsibilities** (asked in 2021): all must act, but rich countries, who polluted first, must do more.

The countries then meet every year in a "COP". At **Kyoto**, on **11 December 1997**, they adopted the Kyoto Protocol (asked in 2025), which came into force in 2005 and named six greenhouse gases. At **Paris in 2015**, COP21, they agreed to hold warming well below 2°C. COP30 was at **Belém, Brazil** in 2025, and COP31 at **Antalya, Türkiye** in 2026.
+ Stockholm Convention 2001: persistent organic pollutants.
+ Minamata: mercury. Basel: hazardous waste. Ramsar: wetlands. Montreal: ozone.
+ UNFCCC: Rio Earth Summit, 1992.
+ Kyoto Protocol adopted 11 December 1997. Paris Agreement 2015.

## env-forests | Counting trees and tigers | India counts its forests every two years and its tigers every four. | 43, 45, 44
Every two years the **Forest Survey of India** at **Dehradun** photographs the country from satellites and publishes the **India State of Forest Report**. The exam has used it twice, in 2018 and 2025.

The 2023 report has a few lines worth keeping. Forest and tree cover together make up **25.17%** of India. **Madhya Pradesh** has the **largest forest area**, followed by Arunachal Pradesh and Chhattisgarh. By share of its own land, **Lakshadweep** is first, and **Mizoram** is first among the States. The four States that added the most forest and tree cover were **Chhattisgarh, Uttar Pradesh, Odisha and Rajasthan**; that was the 2025 question, and Odisha is in it.

Tigers get their own count. **Project Tiger** began in **1973**. The count of 2022 found **3,682** tigers, and the next result is due in 2027. The list of tiger reserves keeps growing: the **56th** was Guru Ghasidas–Tamor Pingla in Chhattisgarh, the **57th** was Ratapani in Madhya Pradesh, and the **58th**, in **March 2025**, was **Madhav**, also in Madhya Pradesh. That State now has nine, the most in India. Odisha has two: Similipal and Satkosia.

Some parks are known for a single animal. **Kaziranga** in Assam means the **one-horned rhinoceros**. **Gir** in Gujarat holds every wild **Asiatic lion** on earth, 891 at the 2025 count. **Dachigam**, near Srinagar, protects the **hangul**, the Kashmir stag. **Keibul Lamjao** in Manipur floats on Loktak Lake and is the only home of the **sangai**, the brow-antlered deer. Manas in Assam was asked in 2018.

In Odisha, Bhitarkanika has saltwater crocodiles, Chilika has Irrawaddy dolphins and Similipal has black tigers.
+ Largest forest area: Madhya Pradesh. Total forest and tree cover: 25.17%.
+ Top four in increase: Chhattisgarh, Uttar Pradesh, Odisha, Rajasthan.
+ 58th tiger reserve: Madhav, Madhya Pradesh, March 2025.
+ Keibul Lamjao: sangai. Dachigam: hangul. Gir: lion. Kaziranga: rhino.

## env-watchers | The pollution watchers | One board, born from a water law, now measures the air as well. | 46
In 1974 Parliament passed a law to keep rivers clean, the **Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act**. To enforce it, the law created a new body, the **Central Pollution Control Board**. So the CPCB was **born from the Water Act in 1974**. Its duties over air came later, with the **Air Act of 1981**.

Then came a night that changed Indian law. In December 1984 gas leaked from a factory in **Bhopal** and killed thousands. Parliament answered with the **Environment (Protection) Act, 1986**, a wide law that lets the Centre act on any kind of pollution.

Two more dates complete the set. The **Wild Life (Protection) Act** is older, from **1972**. The **National Green Tribunal**, a special court for environment cases, came in **2010**.

The CPCB's daily work has already given two exam questions. Under the **National Air Quality Monitoring Programme** it measures pollutants at each station **twice a week** (asked in 2021). And it built the **Comprehensive Environmental Pollution Index**, a score for polluted industrial areas, together with **IIT Delhi** (asked in 2025).

It also runs the **Air Quality Index** you see in the news, which uses eight pollutants and six colour bands from Good to Severe. Under the **National Clean Air Programme**, cities must cut the dust called PM10 by up to 40%.

A few poison names travel with this topic. **Minamata disease** comes from mercury. **Itai-itai** comes from cadmium. **Blue baby syndrome** comes from nitrates in drinking water.
+ CPCB: set up 1974 under the Water Act.
+ Air Act 1981. Environment (Protection) Act 1986, after Bhopal.
+ Wild Life Act 1972. National Green Tribunal 2010.
+ CEPI was made with IIT Delhi. Air monitoring is twice a week.

# economy | Economy and Planning | Plans, deficits, prices and the poor
## eco-plans | Everyone had a plan | Before India had a Planning Commission, four different groups had already written a plan. | 52
In the 1940s freedom was close, and everybody asked the same question: how will a poor country grow? Four groups wrote four answers, and the exam can ask any of them.

In **1944** eight big businessmen, among them **J. R. D. Tata and G. D. Birla**, wrote the **Bombay Plan**. The same year **Shriman Narayan Agarwal** wrote the **Gandhian Plan**, built around villages and small industry. In **1945 M. N. Roy**, the old communist who appears in the labour story too, wrote the **People's Plan**, which put farming and the needs of the poor first. In **1950 Jayaprakash Narayan** wrote the **Sarvodaya Plan**.

Then the government took over. The **Planning Commission** was set up on **15 March 1950** by a resolution of the Cabinet, and the Prime Minister was its chairman. The **First Plan (1951–56)** stressed farming. The **Second (1956–61)** followed **P. C. Mahalanobis** and built heavy industry.

Things did not always go to plan. After two wars and a drought, India ran three one-year plans from **1966 to 1969**, a gap known as the **Plan Holiday**.

Now the question this exam has asked three times. In **1978** the **Janata government of Morarji Desai** ended the Fifth Plan early and tried a new idea, the **Rolling Plan**. Each year the targets and the money were looked at again and the plan was "rolled" one year forward. The idea came from the economist **Gunnar Myrdal**. It lasted only till 1980 and was **not part of any Five Year Plan**. The paper asked what it means (2018), who adopted it (2021) and which Plan introduced it, where the answer was "none of the above" (2025).

Planning ended on **1 January 2015**, when **NITI Aayog** replaced the Commission. "Economic and social planning" sits in the **Concurrent List** (asked in 2021).
+ People's Plan 1945: M. N. Roy. Gandhian Plan: S. N. Agarwal.
+ Bombay Plan 1944: industrialists. Sarvodaya Plan 1950: Jayaprakash Narayan.
+ Rolling Plan: 1978–80, Morarji Desai, idea of Gunnar Myrdal, not in any Five Year Plan.
+ Planning Commission: 15 March 1950. NITI Aayog: 1 January 2015.

## eco-deficits | The four gaps | A budget has four kinds of gap. Each is the one before it with something taken out. | 48, 54
A government, like a family, can spend more than it earns. The budget measures that gap in four ways. Learn them as a chain.

The **revenue deficit** is the simplest. It is the gap between the government's day-to-day spending and its day-to-day income. The **fiscal deficit** is the total gap: all spending minus all income other than loans. In plain words, it is **what the government must borrow this year**. Budget 2026-27 aims at a fiscal deficit of **4.3% of GDP**.

Much of each year's borrowing only pays interest on old loans. Remove that and you get the **primary deficit**: **fiscal deficit minus interest payments**.

The fourth is the newest, from the Budget of 2011-12. The Centre gives grants to the States, and part of that money builds lasting things such as roads and schools. It seems unfair to count that as wasteful spending. So the **effective revenue deficit** is the **revenue deficit minus grants given to States for creating capital assets**.

In 2025 the paper listed these four and added a fake fifth, "Monetary Deficit". There is no such deficit in the budget.

Where does the States' own money come from? Every five years the President sets up a **Finance Commission** under **Article 280**. It has **a chairman and four other members** (asked in 2021), and it decides how central taxes are shared.

The 14th Commission gave the States **42%**. The 15th cut it to **41%** after Jammu and Kashmir became a Union Territory. The **16th Commission**, under **Arvind Panagariya**, covers **2026 to 2031** and **kept 41%**. The law that keeps the deficits in check is the FRBM Act, reviewed by the N. K. Singh committee.
+ Effective revenue deficit = revenue deficit − grants to States for capital assets.
+ Primary deficit = fiscal deficit − interest payments.
+ "Monetary deficit" is not a budget term.
+ Finance Commission: Article 280, chairman plus four members.
+ 16th Finance Commission: Arvind Panagariya, 2026–31, States' share 41%.

## eco-prices | Who watches prices, who counts growth | The RBI has one target, the statistics office has one base year, and both changed their yardsticks in 2026. | 51, 55, 49
Two numbers rule the economic news: how fast prices are rising, and how fast the economy is growing. Each has a keeper.

Prices first. Since 2016 the **Reserve Bank of India** has had one clear target: keep inflation at **4%**, with a band of **2% on either side**. So anything from 2% to 6% is allowed. The target is fixed by the **Government after talking to the RBI**. It is measured by the **Consumer Price Index**, the prices a family pays in the shop, and not by the Wholesale Price Index.

Who makes these indexes? The **CPI** comes from the **National Statistics Office**. The **WPI** comes from the Office of the Economic Adviser in the commerce ministry. To hit the target, a **six-member Monetary Policy Committee** moves the **repo rate**, the rate at which the RBI lends to banks. This whole design came from the **Urjit Patel Committee**.

That is one of many committee names. The **Narasimham Committee** reformed **banking**. The **Malhotra Committee** reformed **insurance**. The **Tendulkar Committee** drew the **poverty line**, and **Rangarajan** redrew it. The **Pherwani Committee** proposed the **National Stock Exchange** (asked in 2025). The 2025 paper also asked what the RBI does **not** do: it does not regulate stock exchanges. **SEBI** does, and SEBI also regulates mutual funds (asked in 2018).

Now growth. GDP is always compared with a **base year**, a normal year whose prices are used as the yardstick. In **February 2026** the statistics office released a new GDP series and moved the base year from 2011-12 to **2022-23**. The CPI got a new base too, **2024**.

Whichever year you use, one fact holds: the **services sector** gives the largest share of India's GDP (asked in 2025).
+ RBI inflation target: 4%, plus or minus 2%, measured by CPI.
+ New GDP base year: 2022-23 (released February 2026). New CPI base: 2024.
+ Narasimham: banking. Malhotra: insurance. Tendulkar: poverty line. Urjit Patel: inflation targeting.
+ Pherwani: National Stock Exchange. SEBI regulates stock exchanges, not the RBI.

## eco-poor | Counting the poor, and giving them work | Poverty is no longer measured by income alone, and the job guarantee has a new name. | 50, 53
For decades India counted the poor by one test: does a person spend less than a fixed amount a day? The Tendulkar and Rangarajan committees argued over where to draw that line.

Then the idea changed. A family may have some income and still be poor if the children are out of school and there is no toilet or clean water. So the UNDP and Oxford University built the **Multidimensional Poverty Index**. It looks at **three dimensions, health, education and standard of living**, each with equal weight.

The global index uses **10 indicators**. India's own version, made by **NITI Aayog**, uses **12**. The two extra ones are **maternal health** and a **bank account**. The data comes from the **National Family Health Survey** (asked in 2025). The 2021 paper asked who is in charge of measuring poverty, and the answer was NITI Aayog. By this measure, about **24.82 crore** Indians left poverty between 2013-14 and 2022-23.

Measuring is one job. Helping is another, and the biggest help in villages has been a promise of work.

In **2005** Parliament passed **MGNREGA**, which promised every rural household **100 days** of paid work a year. In **December 2025** Parliament replaced it with a new law, the **Viksit Bharat – Guarantee for Rozgar and Ajeevika Mission (Gramin) Act**, called **VB–G RAM G** for short. It raises the promise to **125 days**.

"Employment" and "Poverty Alleviation" are both written in the syllabus. A change this large, in the best-known scheme of all, is close to a certain question.
+ National MPI: NITI Aayog, three dimensions, 12 indicators (global index: 10).
+ Data source: National Family Health Survey.
+ MGNREGA 2005: 100 days.
+ VB–G RAM G Act, December 2025: 125 days.

# governance | Governance and Civil Service | The service you are trying to join, and the bodies that watch it
## gov-frame | The steel frame | The story of the civil service, from a Company clerk to a protected officer. | 58, 62, 59
You are sitting this exam to enter the civil service, so its history is your own family history. The 2025 paper asked five questions around it.

In the Company's days, a job in India was a gift. Directors in London handed posts to their nephews. The **Charter Act of 1853** ended that, and the **Macaulay Committee (1854)** designed an open exam. But the exam was held in London, so very few Indians could try.

Indians pressed for change, and four bodies answered, in this order. The **Aitchison Commission (1886)** split the service into Imperial, Provincial and Subordinate branches. The **Islington Commission (1912)** looked again. The **Lee Commission (1923)**, whose full name is the Royal Commission on the Superior Civil Services (asked in 2025), asked for a Public Service Commission in India. The first one opened in **1926**.

The British Prime Minister **Lloyd George** called this service the **steel frame** that held the empire together. After freedom, **Sardar Patel** kept the frame and gave it a new purpose.

The Constitution protects the officer in three Articles. **Article 309** lets the government make service rules. **Article 310** says an officer serves at the pleasure of the President or the Governor. Then **Article 311** limits that pleasure with two shields: an officer **cannot be dismissed by anyone lower than the authority that appointed him**, and **not without an inquiry** where he can defend himself. **Article 312** lets Parliament create a new All India Service after the Rajya Sabha asks for it by a two-thirds vote (asked in 2021 and 2025).

New IAS officers train at the **Lal Bahadur Shastri National Academy of Administration** at **Mussoorie**, set up in **1959**. It works under the **Department of Personnel and Training**, not the Home Ministry. The police academy at Hyderabad is the one under the Home Ministry.
+ Order: Macaulay 1854, Aitchison 1886, Islington 1912, Lee 1923.
+ First Public Service Commission: 1926, after the Lee Commission.
+ Article 311: no dismissal by a lower authority, and not without an inquiry.
+ LBSNAA: Mussoorie, 1959, under the Department of Personnel and Training.
+ "Steel frame": Lloyd George.

## gov-citizen | The citizen at the counter | Sevottam, the Citizen's Charter and the websites that replaced the queue. | 56, 61
Picture a man at a government counter. He does not know how long his work will take, whom to complain to, or whether anyone will listen. A set of reforms tried to answer those three questions.

The first answer came from Britain in **1991**: the **Citizen's Charter**. An office writes down, in public, what service it gives and in how many days. India adopted the idea in **1997**.

A charter on the wall is not enough. So in **2006** the **Department of Administrative Reforms and Public Grievances** built a full model named **Sevottam**, from "seva" and "uttam", excellent service. The 2025 paper asked what it is: a **quality management system** for public services. It has exactly **three modules**: the **Citizen's Charter**, a **Public Grievance Redress** system, and **Service Delivery Capability**. Nothing in it is about staff pay.

The same department runs **CPGRAMS**, the Centralised Public Grievance Redress and Monitoring System. Any citizen can **lodge a complaint online** against any central office and track it.

Other websites took over other queues. **RTI Online** takes information requests. **GeM** is where government offices buy goods. **iGOT Karmayogi** trains officials. **DigiLocker** keeps your certificates. **UMANG** puts many services in one app. **PRAGATI** is the Prime Minister's own screen for reviewing stuck projects.

Behind all of this stands the **Second Administrative Reforms Commission**. Its first report, "Right to Information: Master Key to Good Governance", was asked in 2021. Its twelfth report was called "Citizen Centric Administration". Odisha's own step was **Mo Sarkar** in 2019, where officers phone citizens to ask how they were treated.
+ Sevottam: three modules, Citizen's Charter, Grievance Redress, Service Delivery Capability.
+ Sevottam is a quality management system, made by DARPG in 2006.
+ CPGRAMS: online public grievances.
+ Citizen's Charter: UK 1991, India 1997.

## gov-corruption | Catching the corrupt | One committee in 1962 started a chain that ended with the Lokpal. | 60
In **1962** the government asked **K. Santhanam** to study corruption in public life. His committee has now been asked twice: in 2021 and, for its year, in 2025. Almost every body that fights corruption today grew from its report.

Its first child was the **Central Vigilance Commission**, set up in **1964**. For decades it stood only on a government order. A law gave it **statutory status in 2003**.

The second idea took far longer. In **1963** the lawyer **L. M. Singhvi** coined the word **Lokpal**, a people's guardian who could look into complaints against ministers. In 1966 the **First Administrative Reforms Commission**, led at first by Morarji Desai, asked for a Lokpal at the Centre and Lokayuktas in the States. Bill after bill failed for almost fifty years.

After the great public protest of 2011, Parliament passed the **Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act in 2013**. It came into force in January 2014. The Lokpal has a **Chairperson and not more than eight Members**, and **half of them must be judicial Members**.

Can it look into the Prime Minister? **Yes**, but with limits. It cannot touch matters of foreign relations, security, public order, atomic energy and space. The first Chairperson was **Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose** in 2019. The present one is **Justice A. M. Khanwilkar**.

Beside these stands the citizen's own tool, the **Right to Information Act, 2005**. An office must answer in **30 days**, or in **48 hours** if life or liberty is at stake. The 2018 paper asked about tools of accountability, and the 2021 paper said the strongest control by citizens is the **election**.
+ Santhanam Committee: 1962. CVC: 1964, statutory in 2003.
+ Lokpal: word coined by L. M. Singhvi in 1963; Act of 2013.
+ Lokpal: Chairperson plus up to eight Members, half judicial; Prime Minister is covered with limits.
+ RTI: 30 days; 48 hours for life or liberty.

## gov-village | Four committees and a village | It took thirty-five years and four committees to put the Panchayat into the Constitution. | 57
Gandhi wanted every village to be a small republic. The Constitution only put that wish in **Article 40**, as a Directive Principle. Turning it into real power took four committees.

In **1957** the **Balwant Rai Mehta Committee** studied why village development schemes were failing. Its answer was that the people had no say. It proposed a **three-tier** system: the village panchayat, the block samiti and the district parishad. On 2 October 1959 Nehru opened the first of these at **Nagaur in Rajasthan**.

The new bodies soon grew weak, because State governments did not hold elections or give them money. In **1977** the **Ashok Mehta Committee** suggested a simpler **two-tier** system.

In **1985** the **G. V. K. Rao Committee** gave the sharpest line of all. Officials had taken over, it said, and Panchayati Raj had become **"grass without roots"**. It wanted the **district** to be the main unit of planning.

In **1986** the **L. M. Singhvi Committee**, led by the same man who coined "Lokpal", said the only cure was to give Panchayats **constitutional status**, so that no State could ignore them.

That came with the **73rd Amendment of 1992**. It added Part IX and the **Eleventh Schedule** with **29 subjects**, made elections compulsory every five years, reserved **one-third of seats for women**, and created a State Election Commission and a State Finance Commission. The **74th Amendment** did the same for towns, with the Twelfth Schedule and 18 subjects.

One caution from 2025: the paper named the "B. R. Mehta Committee" among civil service committees. It does not belong there. It belongs here.
+ Balwant Rai Mehta 1957: three-tier. First at Nagaur, Rajasthan, 1959.
+ Ashok Mehta 1977: two-tier.
+ G. V. K. Rao 1985: "grass without roots"; district as the unit of planning.
+ L. M. Singhvi 1986: constitutional status, which came with the 73rd Amendment.
