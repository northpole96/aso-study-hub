#CH speed-basics | Speed, time and distance: the one formula | SPEED FAMILY · 2025 Q54, Q64
Every question in this family grows from one line: **distance = speed × time**. If you know two of the three, you can find the third. Speed = distance ÷ time, and time = distance ÷ speed. The examiner never changes this rule; he only hides it under trains, races and boats.

In 2025 the family took **6 of the 50** maths questions: Robert on his cycle ⟦2025 Q54⟧, a train passing a man ⟦2025 Q59⟧, a race "by 28 metres in 7 seconds" ⟦2025 Q60⟧, a car going 10 km/h faster ⟦2025 Q64⟧, a 100 m race with a head start ⟦2025 Q65⟧ and another train passing a man ⟦2025 Q82⟧.

##Units: km/h and metres per second
Most mistakes in this family are unit mistakes. A train question gives lengths in metres and times in seconds, but speeds in km/h. Change them before you do anything else.

One km is 1,000 m and one hour is 3,600 seconds, so 1 km/h = 1000/3600 = **5/18 m/s**. To go back, multiply by **18/5**.

|Change|Multiply by|Example|
|---|---|---|
|km/h → m/s|5/18|90 km/h × 5/18 = 25 m/s|
|m/s → km/h|18/5|20 m/s × 18/5 = 72 km/h|

>TIP:Learn these four by heart
18 km/h = 5 m/s · 36 km/h = 10 m/s · 54 km/h = 15 m/s · 72 km/h = 20 m/s. Every multiple of 18 turns into a whole number of metres per second, which is why examiners love 54 and 72.

##Speed and time go opposite ways
For a **fixed distance**, if the speed goes up, the time goes down in the same ratio. If you go at 3/4 of your usual speed, you take 4/3 of your usual time. This one idea solves the "late" questions.

Example: walking at 3/4 of his usual speed, a man is 20 minutes late. The new time is 4/3 of the usual, so the extra time is 4/3 − 1 = **1/3 of the usual time**. That 1/3 is 20 minutes, so the usual time is 60 minutes.

##Late at one speed, early at another
The question gives two speeds and says he is late by one amount and early by another. The two times differ by **late + early** (the right time sits between them). If he is late both times, they differ by the difference.

Example: at 4 km/h a boy is 10 minutes late; at 5 km/h he is 5 minutes early. The times differ by 15 minutes = 1/4 hour. So d/4 − d/5 = 1/4, which is d/20 = 1/4, and **d = 5 km**.

Shortcut: distance = (product of speeds ÷ difference of speeds) × time difference = (20 ÷ 1) × 1/4 = 5 km.

The real 2025 question was this type with clock times. Robert reaches at 2 p.m. at 10 km/h and at 12 noon at 15 km/h; at what speed will he reach at 1 p.m.? ⟦2025 Q54⟧ The times differ by 2 hours: d/10 − d/15 = 2, so d/30 = 2 and d = 60 km. At 10 km/h he rides 6 hours, so he starts at 8 a.m. To arrive at 1 p.m. he has 5 hours: 60 ÷ 5 = **12 km/h**. Note that the answer is not 12.5, the plain average of 10 and 15.

##Faster by a few km/h, so hours less
The 2025 car question: a car covers 715 km; at 10 km/h more it would take 2 hours less. Find the speed. ⟦2025 Q64⟧ This makes a quadratic: 715/v − 715/(v + 10) = 2. Do not solve it. **Try the options.** 715 ÷ 55 = 13 hours and 715 ÷ 65 = 11 hours, a difference of exactly 2. So the speed is **55 km/h**.

>WARN:Try the options
In speed questions with a quadratic inside, checking each option is faster than algebra. Start with an option that divides the distance exactly.

##Average speed
Average speed is **total distance ÷ total time**. It is almost never the plain average of the speeds.

@@fig avg

When the two distances are equal (going and coming back), use **2xy ÷ (x + y)**. At 30 km/h there and 60 km/h back: 2 × 30 × 60 ÷ 90 = **40 km/h**, not 45. For three equal distances, use 3xyz ÷ (xy + yz + zx), or simply take each part as the LCM of the speeds and add up the times.

When the distances are not equal, work out each time and add them. 30 km at 10 km/h (3 h) and 30 km at 15 km/h (2 h) is 60 km in 5 hours = 12 km/h.

##Stoppages
A bus runs at 80 km/h without stops but averages 60 km/h with them. In each hour it loses 20 km. At 80 km/h, 20 km takes 20/80 hour = **15 minutes**. So it stops 15 minutes every hour. Formula: (difference ÷ speed without stops) × 60.

>NEXT:Likely next in this chapter
?? At 5/6 of his usual speed a man is 12 minutes late. What is his usual time? || New time is 6/5 of usual, so the extra 1/5 of usual is 12 minutes. Usual time = 60 minutes.
?? 54 km/h in metres per second? || 54 × 5/18 = 15 m/s.
?? A man goes at 20 km/h and returns at 30 km/h. Average speed? || 2 × 20 × 30 ÷ 50 = 24 km/h.
?? Without stops a train runs at 45 km/h; with stops 36 km/h. Minutes stopped per hour? || 9/45 × 60 = 12 minutes.

#CH speed-relative | Meeting and chasing: relative speed | SPEED FAMILY · moving objects
When two things move at the same time, think about the **gap** between them. The gap closes (or opens) at the **relative speed**.

- Moving **towards each other** (opposite directions): speeds **ADD**.
- Moving **the same way** (one chasing the other): speeds **SUBTRACT**.

This one rule is the key to trains, races and boats too, so learn it well.

##Meeting
Two towns are 200 km apart. Cars leave both towns at the same time towards each other at 30 km/h and 20 km/h. The gap closes at 30 + 20 = 50 km/h, so they meet after 200 ÷ 50 = **4 hours**, at 30 × 4 = 120 km from the first town.

If one car leaves an hour later, first move the early car forward by one hour, then use the gap that is left. Forgetting this head start is the common trap.

##Chasing
A thief is 1 km ahead. He runs at 8 km/h and a policeman follows at 10 km/h. The gap closes at 10 − 8 = 2 km/h, so the policeman catches him after 1 ÷ 2 = **half an hour**. In that time the thief runs 4 km and the policeman 5 km. Read whose distance the question asks for.

>WARN:Whose distance?
The options always hold both runners' distances. The policeman runs the thief's distance PLUS the starting gap.

>NEXT:Likely next
?? Two trains start towards each other from places 330 km apart at 50 and 60 km/h. After how long do they meet? || The gap closes at 110 km/h: 330 ÷ 110 = 3 hours.
?? A cyclist leaves 2 hours after a walker at 6 km/h, riding at 10 km/h on the same road. When does he catch up? || Walker's head start = 12 km; gap closes at 4 km/h; 3 hours after the cyclist starts.

#CH trains | Trains: poles, platforms and other trains | SPEED FAMILY · 2025 Q59, Q82
A train question is a relative-speed question where **the length of the train counts**. The front of the train must travel until its back end has cleared whatever it is passing.

@@fig trains

|Train passes|Distance covered|Speed to use|
|---|---|---|
|a pole, a tree, a standing man|length of the train|train's speed|
|a platform, a bridge, a tunnel|train + platform|train's speed|
|a man walking or running|length of the train|add speeds if he comes towards it; subtract if he goes the same way|
|another train|both lengths together|add speeds if opposite; subtract if same way|

##Worked: the two 2025 questions
A train 100 m long takes 6 seconds to cross a man walking at 5 km/h in the opposite direction. Find the train's speed. ⟦2025 Q59⟧ Relative speed = 100/6 m/s = 100/6 × 18/5 = 60 km/h. Opposite directions add, so train + 5 = 60 and the train runs at **55 km/h**.

A train 220 m long runs at 59 km/h. How long does it take to pass a man running at 7 km/h towards it? ⟦2025 Q82⟧ Opposite directions, so 59 + 7 = 66 km/h = 66 × 5/18 = 55/3 m/s. Time = 220 ÷ 55/3 = **12 seconds**.

Both questions are one family, asked from two sides: find the speed, then find the time. The next paper may ask for the **length** (given speed and time) or put the man on the **same side** (subtract).

##Pole and platform together
A train passes a pole in 10 s and a 200 m platform in 20 s. The extra 10 s cover the 200 m platform, so the speed is 20 m/s and the train is 20 × 10 = **200 m** long.

##Two trains
Trains of 100 m and 200 m at 40 and 50 km/h, opposite ways: 300 m at 90 km/h = 25 m/s, so **12 seconds**. Same pair going the same way: 300 m at 10 km/h = 25/9 m/s, so **108 seconds**.

##After they meet
Two trains start at the same time towards each other. After meeting, one takes a hours and the other b hours to finish. Then **speed of first : speed of second = √b : √a**. With 4 hours and 9 hours, the ratio is 3 : 2. The faster train is the one with less time left.

>NEXT:Likely next
?? A train at 72 km/h crosses a 250 m bridge in 25 seconds. Length of the train? || 20 m/s × 25 = 500 m in all, so the train is 250 m.
?? A 150 m train passes a man walking at 6 km/h in the same direction in 10 seconds. Speed of the train? || 15 m/s = 54 km/h relative, so the train runs at 60 km/h.
?? Two trains, 120 m and 80 m, cross each other in 10 s going opposite ways. If one runs at 30 km/h, the other? || 20 m/s = 72 km/h together, so 42 km/h.

#CH races | Races and circular tracks | SPEED FAMILY · 2025 Q60, Q65
A race is two runners on the same road. Three phrases do all the work, so learn what each one means.

|Phrase|Meaning|
|---|---|
|A beats B by 20 m|when A finishes, B is 20 m short of the line|
|A beats B by 5 seconds|B finishes 5 seconds after A|
|A gives B a start of 20 m|B starts 20 m ahead, so B runs 20 m less|
|dead heat|both reach the line together|

##Beats by metres AND by seconds
"A beats B by 28 metres or 7 seconds" means B needs 7 seconds to run those last 28 m. So **B's speed = 28 ÷ 7 = 4 m/s**. This is the key step. ⟦2025 Q60⟧ In a 1 km race, B takes 1000 ÷ 4 = 250 s, and A finishes 7 s earlier: **243 s = 4 min 3 s**.

##Head start and still wins
In a 100 m race, A runs at 8 km/h. A gives B a start of 4 m and still beats him by 15 seconds. Find B's speed. ⟦2025 Q65⟧ 8 km/h = 20/9 m/s, so A takes 100 ÷ 20/9 = 45 s. B runs only 96 m but takes 45 + 15 = 60 s. B's speed = 96/60 = 1.6 m/s = 1.6 × 18/5 = **5.76 km/h**.

##Chains
In a km race, A beats B by 100 m and B beats C by 100 m. When A runs 1,000, B runs 900; when B runs 900, C runs 900 × 900/1000 = 810. So A beats C by **190 m**, not 200.

##Circular tracks
On a closed track the runners meet when the faster one gains **one full lap** (same direction) or when together they cover **one full lap** (opposite directions). On a 400 m track at 6 and 4 m/s: same direction, 400 ÷ 2 = 200 s; opposite, 400 ÷ 10 = 40 s. To meet again **at the starting point**, take the LCM of their lap times.

>NEXT:Likely next
?? In a 500 m race A beats B by 50 m or 10 seconds. A's time? || B's speed 5 m/s, B's time 100 s, so A takes 90 s.
?? A runs 7/4 times as fast as B. What start must A give B so they finish 84 m together? || Distances 7 : 4 in the same time; B runs 4/7 × 84 = 48 m, so a start of 36 m.

#CH boats | Boats and streams | SPEED FAMILY · downstream and upstream
A river adds its own speed to everything floating in it. Going **downstream** (with the water), the stream helps: D = b + s. Going **upstream** (against it), the stream slows the boat: U = b − s. Here b is the boat's speed in still water and s is the stream's speed.

@@fig boat

Two lines answer most questions. **Boat = (D + U) ÷ 2** and **stream = (D − U) ÷ 2**. If a boat goes downstream at 20 km/h and upstream at 12 km/h, the boat is 16 km/h and the stream 4 km/h.

##There and back
A boat at 8 km/h in still water, on a stream of 2 km/h, goes 30 km and back. Down at 10 km/h takes 3 hours; up at 6 km/h takes 5 hours. Total **8 hours**. If the question gives the total time and asks the distance, write d/D + d/U = total time and solve.

##Upstream takes n times as long
If going up takes **n times** as long as coming down the same distance, then b : s = (n + 1) : (n − 1). Twice as long gives 3 : 1; three times gives 2 : 1.

>WARN:Three traps
- Using the still-water speed for a trip on the river.
- Giving the stream's speed when the question asks for the boat's.
- Giving the downstream speed (b + s) as the boat's speed.

>NEXT:Likely next
?? A man rows 24 km downstream in 3 hours and 12 km upstream in 3 hours. Speed of the stream? || D = 8, U = 4; stream = (8 − 4) ÷ 2 = 2 km/h.
?? A man rows at 6 km/h in still water. It takes him 3 times as long upstream as downstream. Stream speed? || b : s = 4 : 2 = 2 : 1, so s = 3 km/h.

#CH speed-sheet | One-page sheet and traps | SPEED FAMILY · revision
Read this page the night before you take the speed practice set, and again in the last week.

|Situation|Rule|
|---|---|
|units|km/h × 5/18 = m/s; m/s × 18/5 = km/h|
|same distance, new speed|time changes in the opposite ratio|
|late and early|times differ by late + early|
|average speed, equal distances|2xy ÷ (x + y)|
|stoppages|minutes per hour = (difference ÷ fast speed) × 60|
|meeting|speeds add|
|chasing|speeds subtract|
|train and pole|train length only|
|train and platform|train + platform|
|two trains|both lengths; add speeds if opposite, subtract if same way|
|after meeting|speed ratio = √(other's time) : √(own time)|
|beats by x m or t s|loser's speed = x ÷ t|
|circular track|same way: lap ÷ difference; opposite: lap ÷ sum|
|boats|D = b + s, U = b − s, b = (D + U) ÷ 2, s = (D − U) ÷ 2|

>WARN:The traps the options set
- The plain average of two speeds (always wrong for average speed).
- The answer in the wrong unit: 15 when the option needed 54 km/h.
- The relative speed given as the train's speed.
- The other person's distance or time (the policeman, B instead of A).
- Forgetting a head start in time or in metres.
