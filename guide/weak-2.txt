#CH tense | 1. Tense signals: today, the third time, as if | WEAK SPOT · Q3, Q77, Q81
Three of your misses were tense questions, and in all three the sentence held a **signal word** that decides the tense. A tense question is rarely about knowing the forms. It is about noticing the one word that points to past, present or unreal time. Train your eye to look for that word first.

@@fig tenses

##Signal 1: a past condition with a present result
Most if-sentences keep both halves in the same time. Type 3 talks about the past in both halves: "If you had taken the medicine, you **would have felt** better" (then, yesterday). But sometimes the condition is in the past and the result is **now**. This is called a **mixed conditional**. The if-part keeps had + past participle, but the result takes **would + base verb**, because the result is in the present.

The signal is a present time word in the result part: **today, now, at present, these days**.
✗ If you had taken the medicine yesterday, you would have felt much better today.
✓ If you had taken the medicine yesterday, you would feel much better today.
✓ If he had studied medicine, he would be a doctor now.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q3)
"If you had taken the medicine on time yesterday, you ____ much better today." You chose **would have felt**. The right answer was **would feel**. The trap: "had taken" looks like type 3, and type 3 pairs with "would have". But the result happens **today**, so it takes would + base verb. Always read both time words, not just the if-part. The 2025 paper used the same family twice ⟦2025 Q13⟧ ⟦2025 Q38⟧.

##Signal 2: "This is the first (second, third) time"
After **It is / This is the first time** (or second, third, only time), English uses the **present perfect**: has done, have seen. The reason is that the sentence counts experiences up to now, and counting up to now is the job of the present perfect. If the sentence is in the past ("It was the first time"), use the past perfect.
✗ This is the third time she wins the debate.
✓ This is the third time she has won the debate.
✓ It was the first time I had flown in a plane.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q77)
"This is the third time she ____ the state-level debate competition." You chose **wins**. The right answer was **has won**. The trap: "wins" sounds lively, like a news headline, and "this is" looks present. But the sentence counts wins up to now, so it needs **has won**.

##Signal 3: "as if" and "as though"
**As if** and **as though** compare with something that is **not true**. For an untrue present, English uses the past form, and for "be" it uses **were** with every subject: as if I were, as if she were. This is the same unreal past that comes after "I wish" ⟦2025 Q1⟧ and in "If I were you" ⟦2025 Q38⟧.
✗ She gives orders as if she is the owner of the house.
✓ She gives orders as if she were the owner of the house.
✓ He talks as though he knew everything. (He does not.)

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q81)
You chose **is**. The right answer was **were**. The trap: in everyday speech many people say "as if she is", and it does not sound wrong. The exam follows the textbook rule. The clue is that she is not the owner, so the comparison is unreal and takes **were**.

#CH inv | 2. "Only when" at the start turns the verb round | WEAK SPOT · Q4
In a normal sentence the subject comes before the verb: "We saw the damage." But when certain words open the sentence, English puts a **helping verb before the subject**, as in a question: "Only then **did we see** the damage." This is called **inversion**. The 2025 paper asked four inversion openers ⟦2025 Q8⟧ ⟦2025 Q11⟧ ⟦2025 Q21⟧ ⟦2025 Q40⟧, so it is one of the most likely grammar questions in your exam.

##Which openers cause inversion
- Negative words: **Never, Never before, Seldom, Rarely, Hardly, Scarcely, No sooner, Little, Not until, Not only, Under no circumstances, At no time**.
- **Only** + a time or a way: **Only when, Only after, Only then, Only by, Only in this way**.
- **So** + adjective or adverb: So compellingly did she speak that…

##Where the helping verb comes from
If the sentence already has a helping verb (had, was, can, will), that verb jumps in front of the subject. If it has none, English brings in **do, does or did** and puts the main verb back in its base form.
✗ Only when the lights came back on we saw the damage.
✓ Only when the lights came back on did we see the damage.
✓ Only after the doctor had checked him was he allowed to go home.
✓ Only by working late every night was she able to finish.

Note one detail. With "Only when" and "Not until", the inversion happens in the **main clause**, not in the "when" clause. "Only when the lights came back on" keeps its normal order; "did we see" is where the change happens.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q4)
"Only when the lights came back on ____ how much damage the storm had done to the hall." You chose **we saw**. The right answer was **did we see**. The trap: the first half of the sentence has normal order ("the lights came back on"), so normal order feels right in the second half too. But the sentence opened with **Only when**, so the main clause must turn round.

#CH verb | 3. Verb patterns: would rather, deny, lest | WEAK SPOT · Q12, Q19, Q29
Some words decide the **shape of the verb** that follows them. You do not choose the tense by meaning alone; you follow the pattern that the word demands. Three of your misses were of this kind. Learn each pattern as a fixed frame.

##Pattern 1: would rather + another person + past form
When the subject talks about **its own** choice, "would rather" takes the bare verb: "I would rather **stay** at home." When it talks about **what another person should do**, it takes the **past form**, even though the time is the present or future: "I would rather **you stayed** at home." This past form is the same unreal past as in chapter 1. It is softer and more polite than a direct order.
✓ I would rather go by train. (my own action: bare verb)
✓ I would rather you went by train. (your action: past form)
✗ I would rather you not mention it to anyone.
✓ I would rather you did not mention it to anyone.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q12)
"I would rather you ____ this matter to anyone until the enquiry is over." You chose **not mention**. The right answer was **did not mention**. The trap: "not mention" looks like the bare verb that follows "would rather" in "I would rather not go". That bare form is only used when the subject and the doer are the same person. Here the speaker is talking about **you**, so the past form "did not mention" is needed. "Had not mentioned" would be a regret about the past, but the enquiry is still going on.

##Pattern 2: deny, admit, avoid + -ing (or having + V3)
A small group of verbs is always followed by the **-ing form**, never by "to": **deny, admit, avoid, enjoy, finish, mind, risk, regret (the past), suggest, consider**. When the action happened before the denying, you can use the **perfect gerund**: having + past participle.
✗ The accused denied to be near the bank.
✓ The accused denied being near the bank.
✓ The accused denied having been near the bank. (best: it happened earlier)
✓ He admitted having taken the files.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q19)
"The accused denied ____ anywhere near the bank on the night of the robbery." You chose **to be**. The right answer was **having been**. The trap: "to be" is the most common verb form after a verb, so it feels safe. But "deny" is on the -ing list. Keep this short list in mind: **deny, admit, avoid, mind, risk + -ing** ⟦2025 Q34⟧.

##Pattern 3: lest + should + base verb
**Lest** means "so that … not" or "for fear that". Because the "not" is already inside it, you never add another "not". The verb after it is **should + base verb** (some books allow the bare verb alone, but "should" is the exam form).
✗ Walk carefully lest you should not fall.
✗ Walk carefully lest you will fall.
✓ Walk carefully lest you should fall.
✓ Speak softly lest you should wake the baby.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q29, skipped)
You skipped this one. The right sentence was **Walk carefully lest you should fall.** The other three added a "not" or used "will" or "may not". Once you know the rule (lest = so that … not, + should), this question takes five seconds.

#CH imp | 4. Sentence improvement: when nothing is wrong, and "the reason is because" | WEAK SPOT · Q10, Q14
Sentence improvement questions give you a sentence with a part in brackets and four choices, the last of which is **No improvement**. The 2021 paper had five of them in a row ⟦2021 Q12⟧ ⟦2021 Q13⟧. Two of your misses came from this style, and they teach two opposite lessons.

##Lesson 1: sometimes nothing is wrong
Students learn to expect a mistake in every bracket, so they change a correct sentence. A good paper always has a few brackets that are already right. Before you choose a change, **read the bracketed part with each option in turn**. If the sentence is already correct and every option breaks something, the answer is No improvement.

The frame **The more …, the more …** (also the less, the better, the sooner) has three parts that must all be present: **the** before each comparative, the plain comparative (**less**, not lesser), and the normal word order (you will feel, not will you feel).
✓ The more you practise, the less nervous you will feel.
✗ The more you practise, less nervous you will feel.
✗ The more you practise, the lesser nervous you will feel.
✓ The sooner you start, the better it will be.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q10)
"The more you practise speaking in public, (the less nervous you will feel) on the stage." You chose **less nervous you will feel**, which drops the second "the". The right answer was **No improvement**. The trap: the bracket was long, so it looked like something must be wrong with it. Ask of each option: does it make the sentence better, or only different?

##Lesson 2: do not say the same thing twice
"The reason … is because" uses two words that both mean reason. The textbook form is **The reason is that …**. In the same way, "return back", "repeat again" and "though … but" all say one thing twice.
✗ The reason why he failed is because he did not read the questions.
✓ The reason why he failed is that he did not read the questions.
✗ The reason for his absence is due to he was ill.
✓ The reason for his absence is that he was ill.

A related rule: **because of** is followed by a noun ("because of his illness"), while **because** is followed by a full clause ("because he was ill"). That is the exact past question ⟦2021 Q12⟧.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q14)
You chose **was because**. The right answer was **is that**. The trap: "was because" fixed the tense and looked like a careful correction, but it kept "because". The real fault was the double reason, not the tense.

>NEXT:Check yourself and likely next
?? Improve: "The higher you climb, (more colder) it becomes."||the colder.
?? Improve: "He (returned back) the book."||returned.
?? Improve: "(Though he is poor, but) he is honest."||Though he is poor, (or Although he is poor,). Drop "but".
?? Improve: "The reason he left is (that) he was unhappy."||No improvement.
?? Likely next: "He could not come (because of he was ill)."||because he was ill.

#CH who | 5. Who or whom when "I thought" sits in the middle | WEAK SPOT · Q28
**Who** is used for the subject of a verb (the one who does). **Whom** is used for the object (the one to whom something is done). Most students know this. The hard part comes when a small clause like **I thought, we believed, they said** sits between "who" and its verb. That small clause hides the real verb.

##The test: take the small clause out
Cover "I thought" with your finger and read again. If "who/whom" is now followed by a verb, it is the subject of that verb, so it must be **who**.
- The man who/whom **I thought** was honest cheated me.
- Cover "I thought": The man **who was honest** cheated me. So: **who**.
- The man whom I trusted cheated me. Here "whom" is the object of "trusted" (I trusted him), so **whom** is right.
✗ The man whom I thought was honest cheated me.
✓ The man who I thought was honest cheated me.
✓ The man whom I trusted cheated me.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q28)
You chose **The man whom I thought was honest cheated me.** The right answer used **who**. The trap: "whom I thought" sounds like "whom I trusted", and "whom" also sounds more formal, so it feels like the careful answer. Cover the small clause and the verb "was" appears; "whom was honest" is clearly wrong.

>NEXT:Check yourself and likely next
?? Fill in: "She is the girl ____ we believe will top the exam." (who / whom)||who. Cover "we believe": the girl who will top.
?? Fill in: "She is the girl ____ we chose as captain." (who / whom)||whom. We chose her: object.
?? Fill in: "The officer ____ everyone said was strict turned out to be kind." (who / whom)||who.
?? Likely next: "____ do you think will win?" (Who / Whom)||Who. Cover "do you think": who will win.

#CH comp | 6. Comparisons must be complete | WEAK SPOT · Q31
A comparison has fixed partner words. **as … as** compares two equal things; **-er … than** compares two unequal things. When a sentence puts both comparisons together ("as tall as, if not taller than"), **each half must still be complete on its own**. The quickest test is to read each half alone with the end of the sentence.

##Read each half alone
"He is as tall as, if not taller than, his brother."
- Half 1: He is **as tall as** his brother. Complete.
- Half 2: He is **taller than** his brother. Complete.
✗ He is as tall, if not taller than, his brother. (half 1 reads "as tall his brother")
✗ He is as tall as, if not taller, his brother. (half 2 reads "taller his brother")
✓ He is as tall as, if not taller than, his brother.
✓ This school is as good as, if not better than, the one in town.

##The second half of a comparison: that of, those of
In Mock 09 you got the "climate of Puri … than that of Bhubaneswar" question right. It belongs to the same family: compare **like with like**. Climate is compared with climate (that of), not with a city.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q31)
You chose **He is as tall, if not taller than, his brother.** The right answer kept the second "as". The trap: when read quickly, the sentence sounds smooth because the ear fills in the missing word. On paper, test each half.

#CH pos | 7. Parts of speech: before, but and seeing | WEAK SPOT · Q22, Q25, Q26
This chapter continues Weak Spots 1. The rule is the same: **the job of a word in this sentence decides its part of speech**, not the word itself. Mock 09 used three words that can do two or three different jobs. The 2021 paper had six direct questions of this kind ⟦2021 Q17–22⟧, so one or two are likely in your exam.

##Before, after, since: three jobs
Look at what **follows** the word.
|What follows|Part of speech|Example|
|---|---|---|
|nothing (end of the clause)|Adverb|I have met him **before**.|
|a noun or pronoun|Preposition|Come **before** lunch.|
|a full clause (subject + verb)|Conjunction|Come **before** the guests arrive.|

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q22)
"I am sure I have met this gentleman somewhere (before)." You chose **Preposition**. The right answer was **Adverb**. The trap: "before" is most often seen as a preposition (before lunch). Here nothing follows it; it simply tells **when** about the verb "have met", so it is an adverb.

##But: conjunction or preposition
"But" is usually a conjunction that joins two ideas: "He is poor **but** honest." When it means **except** and stands before a noun or pronoun, it is a **preposition**: "All **but** one passed." "Nobody **but** Ravi knew." A good test: if you can replace it with "except", it is a preposition.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q25, skipped)
"All (but) one of the students passed the final examination." The answer was **Preposition**. Try the test: "All **except** one of the students passed." It works, so "but" is a preposition here.

##-ing words: gerund or participle
An -ing word that **works as a noun** (subject, object, after a preposition) is a **gerund**: "**Seeing** is believing." An -ing word (or -ed word) that **describes a noun** is a **participle**: "**Seeing** the police, the thief ran." Here "seeing the police" tells us something about the thief, like an adjective. The same is true of past participles: "**Tired** of waiting, the villagers left."

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q26, skipped)
"(Seeing) the police at the gate, the thief ran out through the back door." The answer was **Participle**. In Weak Spots 1 the -ing word at the start ("Swimming is good exercise") was a gerund, because it was the subject. Here the subject is "the thief", and "Seeing the police" only describes him. The question to ask: **is the -ing word the subject, or does it describe the subject?**

?? Name the part of speech: "Everyone (but) Meena was present."||Preposition (= except).

#CH agr | 8. Agreement: scenery and a pair of shoes | WEAK SPOT · Q34, Q36
You got 11 of the 12 agreement questions right, so the main rule is clear to you. The two misses were both cases where a **plural-looking word sits next to the verb but is not the subject**. The fix is the habit from Weak Spots 1: cross out the "of" phrase and find the real subject.

##Uncountable nouns are singular
**Scenery, machinery, furniture, luggage, information, advice, news, poetry, jewellery** are uncountable. They have no plural and always take a singular verb.
✗ The scenery of the Koraput hills are charming.
✓ The scenery of the Koraput hills is charming.
✓ The machinery in the mill has been sold.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q34)
"[The scenery of] [the Koraput hills] [are charming] [during the monsoon.]" You chose **the Koraput hills**. The right answer was **are charming**. The trap: the plural "hills" stands right before the verb, so "are" sounds natural, and you looked for the mistake in the noun instead. Cross out "of the Koraput hills": "The scenery … are charming" is clearly wrong.

##A pair of: the pair is the subject
**Trousers, scissors, shoes, spectacles, earrings** are plural on their own: "My shoes **are** tight." But when "pair" is the subject, the verb follows "pair", which is singular.
✓ These shoes are too tight.
✓ This pair of shoes is too tight.
✓ The pair of shoes that I bought last week is already too tight.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q36)
You chose **are**. The right answer was **is**. The trap was the long gap: "that I bought at the fair last week" pushed the verb far away from "pair", and "shoes … are" sounded right. In Weak Spots 1 you missed "A pair of trousers" for the same reason. This is now the second time, so make it a fixed habit: **find the first noun of the subject, ignore everything after "of"**.

#CH prep | 9. Prepositions: resemble, dispense with, part with | WEAK SPOT · Q18, Q32, Q63
Prepositions after verbs are fixed by habit, not by logic, so they must be learnt in pairs. Your three misses show the two kinds of trap: a verb that takes a preposition you did not expect, and a verb that takes **no** preposition at all.

##Verbs that take no preposition
**Resemble, discuss, reach, enter, attack, marry, order, request, approach** take their object directly. Students often add "with", "about" or "to" because the meaning seems to need it.
✗ The boy resembles with his father.
✓ The boy resembles his father.
✗ We reached at the station.
✓ We reached the station. (but: we arrived at the station)
✗ They discussed about the plan.
✓ They discussed the plan.

"Resemble" is also a **state verb**, so it is not used in the -ing form: "He is resembling" is wrong.

>WARN:Your question (Mock 09 Q32)
You chose **The boy resembles with his father in every way.** The right answer had no preposition. The trap: "is similar to" and "looks like" need a preposition, so "resembles with" sounds natural. Learn the short list above.

##Verbs that keep a fixed preposition
|Pair|Meaning|Example|
|---|---|---|
|dispense **with**|do without, leave out|They dispensed with the speeches.|
|part **with**|give away something you own|He would not part with a coin.|
|part **from**|leave a person|She parted from her friends.|
|cope **with**|deal with a difficulty|We could not cope with the rush.|
|dispose **of**|get rid of|Dispose of the waste safely.|
|comply **with**|obey a rule|Comply with the new rules.|
|focus **on**|give attention to|The study focuses on diet ⟦2025 Q42⟧.|

>WARN:Your questions (Mock 09 Q18 and Q63)
In Q18 (dispense ____ the usual speeches) you skipped; the answer was **with**. In Q63 (refused to part ____ even a single coin) you chose **away**; the answer was **with**. The trap in Q63: "give away" means almost the same thing, so "part away" felt natural. But the fixed phrase is **part with**. Many of these "with" verbs (dispense with, part with, cope with, comply with, do away with) share the idea of dealing with or letting go of something.

|zeal|great energy and eagerness|anger|its opposite is lack of feeling: apathy|
|succinct|brief and clear|eloquent|eloquent is about beauty of speech, not length|

##Three habits for word questions
- **Say the meaning first.** Before you read the options, say in three words what the word means ("not serious", "make better"). Then look for that meaning.
- **For antonyms, find the synonym first.** The examiner nearly always puts a synonym among the options as a trap (destabilisation for subversion, passion for zeal). Cross it out, then pick its opposite.
- **In a sentence, find the clue.** In Q54 the clue was "finished in five minutes", which is about **length**. A word about beauty (eloquent) does not answer a clue about length.

##Pick-the-pair questions
In "Select the pair of synonyms" ⟦2021 Q43⟧, two of the four words mean the same and the other two are decoys. Decoys are often two **faults** (careless, idle) or two **opposites** of the real pair. Test each possible pair with one sentence: "He is a **diligent** worker" / "He is an **industrious** worker" mean the same; "He is a **careless** worker" / "He is an **idle** worker" do not.

##The four habits that would have saved most of these marks
- In tense questions, **circle every time word** (today, now, yesterday, the third time) before choosing.
